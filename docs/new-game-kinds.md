## What the garden already plays like

The atlas names seven play patterns (search, allocate, experiment, read, compose, build, create). In practice almost every destination now takes one of four shapes:

| Shape | Examples | What it is good at |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Teaching bench | Ribbon Recut, Lantern Circuit, Shadow Theatre, Cold Window, Same Pour | One bounded model, one contrast, supplied cases |
| Guided reading | English and French Paris, the Library, the Travelers' Archive | Passages at three lengths with questions |
| Composition | Story Loft, Notice Printer, Bell Studio, Exhibit Label Maker | Arranging supplied pieces for an audience |
| Practice with a rival or errand | Café, Bramble, Post Office, Willow routes, Riddles | Repeated questions in a story errand |

These are careful and honest, and they are mostly *one sitting, one model, supplied cases*. The kinds of play still missing are the ones that work differently: play that runs across real days, play with an opponent who plans, play where the child designs and tests, play in another language, and play with sound and image as the material. Those are also where an older explorer finds room to go deep, because the player's own choices set the difficulty.

## The missing kinds

Each entry gives the kind of play, what it teaches, a bounded first slice and how far it can grow for an older explorer. "Older explorer" means the far end of the roughly-thirteen horizon, never a particular child.

### 1. Rhythm and music making (spec 115)

**Kind:** an instrument the child plays with, then a bar the child fills. Bell Studio has pitch order; nothing yet has *duration*.
**Teaches:** pulse, beat, note and rest lengths, and the fact that a bar is one whole cut into halves, quarters and eighths. Music is an explicit extension outside Marble.
**First slice:** Rhythm Studio at `#studio/rhythm`. Fill a four-beat bar with whole, half, quarter and eighth notes and rests; hear it; find two rhythms that last the same; echo a rhythm by ear or by sight; set words to a work song for the mending crews.
**Older depth:** three-beat bars, dotted notes, and two parts at once (three against two), where the child discovers that the parts line up again after a common multiple of beats.

### 2. Logbook play across real days (spec 116)

**Kind:** a field guide the child fills from the real world, a little at a time. Nothing in the garden currently asks a child to look outside and come back tomorrow.
**Teaches:** weather and seasons, the moon's phases, classification keys for garden minibeasts, and building tallies, pictograms and bar graphs from data the child collected.
**First slice:** the Field Guide Almanac at `#almanac`, with a weather page, a sky page, a garden-neighbors key and a data desk. It adopts the weather log already proposed in [spec 33](specs/33-rebuilding-the-town.md) and can ship before or after the town scene.
**Older depth:** compare two months and ask whether one month tells you about climate. Sapling explorers can compare their moon notes with a calculated moon table and predict the next full moon; spec 116 fixes the model, its limits and twelve reference dates.

### 3. A second additional language: Spanish (spec 117)

**Kind:** language as action. Read a short Spanish note from a visitor, then *do* what it says in the scene: carry cargo, guide a walker, cook from a recipe, prepare a boat for tomorrow's weather. French Paris asks reading questions; this asks the child to act on what they understood.
**Teaches:** high-frequency Spanish for real tasks (numbers, places, directions, food, weather), with a collected phrasebook and short grammar notes.
**First slice:** the River Market at `#mercado`, four errands from a barge that arrives from a Spanish-speaking river town during the floating market of Act III. The six Spanish riddle cards already merged in spec 94 gain a destination to link to.
**Older depth:** the longer note at each errand, grammar notes (*hay*, *va a* plus a verb, giving directions with *sigue* and *gira*), and a postcard written back from phrase tiles plus the child's own words. Content is chosen so a teen beginner meets real situations at beginner language load.

### 4. Strategy games against Bramble (spec 121)

**Kind:** two-sided games on one device, the child against Bramble. The first opponent in the garden who *plans*.
**Teaches:** thinking ahead, working backwards from the end, and number bonds through repeated play. Fluency without timers.
**First slice:** three small games at a Games Table in the orchard: Nim with seed piles, Shut the Box with two dice, and a short mancala with an attributed origin card. Bramble's strength is a visible dial the child sets: "Bramble is sleepy", "Bramble is paying attention", "Bramble has a plan". No score kept across sessions; a game can be abandoned at any time.
**Older depth:** the winning strategy for Nim is learnable. A "Bramble explains" page walks through why certain piles are safe, and the child can test the rule by playing it. Hex on a small board adds a game with no draws, which invites the question "why can't Hex end in a tie?"

### 5. Deduction mysteries (spec 122)

**Kind:** a short mystery solved with a logic grid. Clues are notes and pictures; the child crosses out possibilities.
**Teaches:** careful reading, inference, the difference between "the clues show this" and "this is my guess", and elimination as a method. A non-science home for observation versus interpretation.
**First slice:** three mysteries in the town: who borrowed the ladder, which basket the seeds went in, whose boots left the muddy prints. Three suspects and three objects, five or six clues each, and a "this is still open" state when the clues don't yet decide.
**Older depth:** four-by-four grids, clues that only matter in combination, a clue that turns out to be unreliable and has to be weighed, and a "write the mystery yourself" page where the child sets clues and checks whether they produce exactly one answer.

### 6. Programming puzzles with loops and functions (spec 123)

**Kind:** a puzzle programming game. The Post Office has literal sequences and the Sorting Desk has if/else; nothing yet has repetition, functions or debugging someone else's program.
**Teaches:** loops, functions, decomposition, tracing and debugging. This is the programming extension path in the strand map.
**First slice:** the Clockwork Mill. A small mill robot moves sacks on a grid. Commands are tiles; a "repeat" tile and a "make a new move" tile (a function) appear in later puzzles. Each puzzle has a step budget only as an optional challenge line.
**Older depth:** functions that take a number, a broken program Pip wrote that the child must trace and fix, and an open level editor where the child designs a puzzle and checks that their own program solves it.

### 7. Design, build and test (spec 124)

**Kind:** an engineering sandbox. The Storm Workshop and Fair Test benches compare supplied set-ups; here the child designs the thing.
**Teaches:** the design cycle (constraint, design, test, revise), materials and cost trade-offs, and the honesty of a model test.
**First slice:** the Riverbank Levee. Place sandbags, stones and planted grass along a bank with a fixed budget; run the authored flood model; see where water gets through; revise. The model is openly authored and says so.
**Older depth:** two goals that conflict (cheap versus strong, protect the path versus keep the meadow), and a page that asks which design the town should pick and why. Several designs can be defensible.

### 8. Visual art and design (spec 125)

**Kind:** a making studio for images. The game has no place where color, shape and pattern are the material.
**Teaches:** shape, symmetry and pattern; color mixing; composition for a viewer. Visual art is an explicit extension outside Marble, and artistic quality is never scored.
**First slice:** the Sign Painter. The town needs new shop signs after the storm. Choose shapes, flip and turn them, build a repeating border, and mix colors on two palettes: finite pigment recipes and additive lamplight, which is a real and surprising contrast.
**Older depth:** rotational symmetry, tessellations that tile a floor, and a studied page on one credited public-domain artwork per visit, asking what choices the artist made.

### 9. Spatial puzzles (spec 126)

**Kind:** wordless shape puzzles.
**Teaches:** spatial reasoning, rotation and reflection, nets and solids. Geometry is thin across the garden, and these work for a child who can't yet read.
**First slice:** tangram window repairs in the Workshop, then folding nets into parcel boxes at the Post Office (which nets fold into a closed box?).
**Older depth:** pentomino floors, all eleven nets of a cube, and a "prove it can't be done" page for an impossible tiling.

### 10. Stewardship over seasons (spec 127)

**Kind:** a slow management game. One garden plot, planned across the seasons that the almanac tracks.
**Teaches:** systems thinking, trade-offs, crop rotation and soil, planning under uncertainty.
**First slice:** plan four beds for one season; at the season's turn the authored model reports what grew and what the soil now holds.
**Older depth:** multi-year rotation, a pest that spreads between neighboring beds, and a planning page with two goals. This kind leans on the town and the act structure, so its spec should follow the act-structure work.

### 11. Off-screen missions, a mode for every kind (spec 128)

A card that sends the child to the real kitchen, yard or window: measure a real cup of water, clap a rhythm for a grown-up, find three minibeasts, watch tonight's moon. The game records nothing and welcomes them back with a question for a grown-up conversation. This is the honest way to reach handwriting, physical measurement and real observation, and it costs only text. Each spec above includes a few, and spec 128 gathers them with new cards for older places.

## Kinds deliberately left out

- **Multiplayer, sharing and online content.** Out of scope for this game. Strategy games use Bramble, and two siblings sharing a device take turns on their own explorers.
- **Timed play and reaction games.** Rhythm Studio plays sound but never asks the child to tap in time; clapping along stays an off-screen, unrecorded invitation.
- **Feelings and mood logs.** Sensitive data about a child, and against the device-only posture.
- **Real money, real locations and live weather.** The almanac stores what a child reports, never a location or a fetched forecast.
- **Word Workshop and phonics.** Wanted, but it is reading, and it waits on reviewed audio ([brief 55](specs/55-phonics-content-audio-brief.md)).
- **The town meeting.** A deliberation game fits Act IV and belongs with the act-structure work.

## Suggested order

1. **Rhythm Studio (115).** Smallest new kind, reuses Bell Studio's audio and save shape, and joins music to fractions.
2. **Field Guide Almanac (116).** Gives a reason to come back tomorrow and starts the collect, classify, connect pattern every knowledge strand can reuse.
3. **Spanish River Market (117).** A second language world, with content that works for a teen beginner.
4. **Strategy games against Bramble.** The cheapest high-reuse kind after these, and it scales well for older explorers.
5. **Deduction mysteries** and **the Clockwork Mill**, which give older explorers the most room.
6. **Design and build**, **Sign Painter** and **spatial puzzles**, in whichever order the art work allows.
7. **Stewardship over seasons**, after the act structure settles.

## Jane and Marble

As [AGENTS.md](../AGENTS.md) requires, specs 115–117 refreshed both upstreams on 2026-10-23 (Marble `f186a79`, Jane `1927912`). The most useful new material is Marble's **teaching research** file, which now gives teaching approaches, common misconceptions and constraints for about 1,050 topics. Jane's own review of it advises following its `learn` constraints where fields disagree, and these specs do.

It changed the almanac most. The moon explanations for younger players now describe the pattern without the cause and never show a shadow, with the cause kept for older players alongside the orbit view. The minibeast key was rewritten as two-way questions about visible features, the picture gallery was removed, and a "build a key" page was added. The weather prompt now asks the child to look outside before choosing, the pictogram leads with its key, and a weather-or-climate sort makes the timescale explicit. Rhythm Studio's fraction labels always name the whole bar, following the unit-fractions misconceptions. Jane's learn-card pattern (one idea, one worked example, revealed a tap at a time) became Rhythm Studio's **Listen first**, and its writing rules (speak to the learner, keep it short, three choices) shaped the Spanish questions. Jane's mastery, medals, bosses and schedules stay out, as in every earlier spec.

Marble has no music, art or Spanish topics, so those are original extensions; its newer graph adds "Rhythm in rhymes and songs", which is clap-based and stays an off-screen invitation. Specs 121 to 128 read the teaching-research records for their own targets before their text was written, and each has its own upstream table and hard-prerequisite crosswalk. The records that shaped them most: Observation vs Interpretation (the I SAW / I THINK frame in the mysteries), Could there be another explanation? (the wet corner), Designing for Weather Hazards and Preventing Erosion (the Bank Builder's comparisons, claims and fair test), Lines of symmetry and White Light & Colour (the Sign Painter's fold check and its separate paint and light benches), Nets of 3-D Shapes (predict before folding), and What Plants Need to Thrive (soil goodness as vitamins, never food). Marble's refreshed graph has loop and debugging topics that the pin lacks; spec 123 notes them without moving the pin.
