A child learns: the subject and object of a clause; that the active puts the doer first and the passive puts the receiver first, built with *be* plus a past participle, with the doer optional after *by*; that not every *was* is passive; why writers choose the passive, including that it can leave out a doer for honest or less honest reasons; the four moods school grammar names (indicative, imperative, subjunctive, conditional) with the formal subjunctive *if I were* and *that he be*; the Year 6 grammar words; and, at Keeper's Desk, to name a feature, quote it and say its effect. ## The knowledge

Sourced in the [audit](../source-audits/274-gazette-grammar.md); unconfirmed claims were cut.

**Subject, object, voice.** The subject is normally the noun phrase before the verb that the clause is about; the object normally follows the verb and shows what the verb acts on, and can become the subject of a passive (DfE glossary). In the active voice the subject is the doer. A passive is recognized by the past participle, the normal object turned into the subject, the normal subject turned into an optional *by* phrase, and the verb *be* or sometimes *get* (DfE glossary). A verb is not passive just because its meaning feels passive: "He received a warning" is active. *Be* plus an adjective ("The café was full") and the progressive ("Ember was blowing bubbles") are not passive. Some *be* + participle clauses read two ways, as an adjectival state or a verbal passive ("The gate was closed"); items avoid them or add *by* or a time phrase, and the grown-up panel names the ambiguity, which also touches Hollis's "The Sponge is drained". "The brook rose" names no doer and is still active. Tense is kept when flipping: *is printing* becomes *is being printed*.

**Why the passive.** Writers use it when the doer is unknown, obvious or unimportant, or to keep the topic in subject position; Strunk (1918) advised the active voice yet called the passive "frequently convenient and sometimes necessary" and chose voice by what the paragraph is about. The Year 6 curriculum frames the passive as a way "to affect the presentation of information". Leaving out the doer can also hide responsibility; Pullum (2014) shows that many published attacks on the passive misidentify it, and suggests that some critics confuse the grammatical passive with a rhetorical complaint about hidden agency. Fausey and Boroditsky (2010) found that agentive wording ("she tore") led readers to assign more blame and larger penalties than non-agentive wording ("it tore"): wording can shift blame, which is why the pack asks "by whom?" without assuming an answer.

**Mood.** US standards (CCSS L.8.1c) name five moods: indicative, imperative, interrogative, conditional, subjunctive; Marble's target names four, leaving out the interrogative, which 269 teaches as the question sentence type. The DfE glossary does not use "mood"; it lists *would* among modal verbs and treats the imperative as the verb of a command. The pack uses the school names and says in the grown-up panel that grammarians differ. The **imperative** gives a command with "you" understood. The English **subjunctive** survives in few forms, mostly formal: *were* in unreal conditions ("If I were"), the plain form after verbs of asking or requiring ("The school requires that all pupils be honest"), and inverted "Were they to come" (DfE Appendix 2 and glossary). Informal speech commonly uses *was*, and the subjunctive has declined in use (BYU corpus study). The **conditional** with *would* predicts what follows from an imagined condition; CCSS L.8.3a pairs the conditional and subjunctive with "expressing uncertainty or describing a state contrary to fact".

**Terms and effect.** The Year 6 terminology list is subject, object, active, passive, synonym, antonym, ellipsis, hyphen, colon, semi-colon, bullet points; ellipsis is "the omission of a word or phrase which is expected and predictable" (DfE glossary), and the US name *ellipsis* also covers the mark "...". Key stage 3 asks pupils to study "the effectiveness and impact of the grammatical features of the texts they read" and to discuss language "with precise and confident use of linguistic and literary terminology". Myhill and colleagues' 2012 randomized trial (744 students, 31 schools) found a positive effect on writing from grammar taught in context, with explicit talk about choices and their effects (the gain was larger for more able writers); the pack follows that shape (feature, quote, effect) and avoids feature-spotting without effect.

**Public-domain texts.** Kenneth Grahame, *The Wind in the Willows* (1908), chapter 1: the Mole's list of digging verbs. Shakespeare, *Twelfth Night* (written about 1601; performed at the Middle Temple by February 1602), line 1: "If music be the food of love, play on" (subjunctive then imperative). William Strunk Jr., *The Elements of Style* (1918), rule 10, "Use the active voice", itself an imperative.

## The lesson

Jane's **teach -> guided -> practice**: ten teach cards (subject and object; active; passive; the doer can disappear; not every *was*; why the passive; statements and commands; the subjunctive; the conditional; grammar for effect), one idea each with a worked example revealed a step per tap; each set's first item shows 1-3 visible steps. **"I'm stuck" ladder** on every item: hint -> an easier worked case plus how to start this one (the cake eaten by Ember; "The bell was rung"; "Dot posts a letter" flipped; "Shut the gate"; "Stop. Listen. Run.") -> skip for now, no penalty.

**Contrasts:** active vs passive with the same meaning; doer after *by* vs doer left out; *was* + participle vs *was* + adjective vs *was* + *-ing*; a passive vs a doerless active ("The brook rose"); flipping vs swapping meaning; a kept tense vs a drifted one; "if I was" in talk vs "if I were" in a formal letter; an imagined "if" vs a real "if"; command vs prediction; naming a feature vs saying its effect; how a sentence is built vs whether it is true.

**Misconceptions tagged on choices:** `subject-is-doer`, `by-phrase-ignored`, `verb-as-subject`, `object-is-subject`, `place-as-doer`, `be-means-passive`, `past-means-passive`, `no-doer-means-passive`, `swap-without-meaning`, `tense-drift`, `passive-always-hides`, `by-banned`, `passive-is-wrong`, `mood-by-punctuation`, `imperative-no-subject`, `real-for-unreal`, `informal-for-formal`, `subjunctive-is-fact`, `were-tells-nothing`, `subjunctive-is-error`, `were-only-plural`, `vague-praise`, `term-without-effect`, `more-examples-not-effect`, `grammar-settles-truth`, `drop-the-doer`, `conditional-is-certain`, `mood-is-command`, `repetition-is-error`, `effect-unrelated`, `mood-by-word-order`, `mood-order-swap`, `voice-mood-mixup`, `mood-by-topic`.

## The game or activity

The Two Columns and mood benches always show: *Grammar shows how a sentence is built. It can't show whether the sentence is true.* No score, timer, lock or mastery label. Drags have tap equivalents; every sentence card can be read aloud.

- **(a) Who Did It? (Sprout, Seedling; first slice).** Eight practice-sheet sentences: tap the doer, then a tray (*doer first*, *doer after "by"*, *doer left out*). "Left out" asks for a spoken guess, never stored. After page 4, five *Gazette* lines join the stack.
- **(b) Flip It (Seedling, Sapling; first slice).** Tile builder for five sentences across present progressive, past, future and present perfect; a **"by" switch** hides and shows the doer; feedback names tense drift and meaning swaps separately.
- **(c) Two Columns (Sapling).** Tap each verb to color passives and actives. Standalone pair: the baker's notice about a missing pie against Pip's note owning up. Act IV pair: the miller's report and the librarian's note, two short pieces from the *Gazette* issue whose main stories spec 273 owns. Then **What can the grammar show?** (yes / no): where each writer puts the attention (yes), which explanation is true (no), who might have acted (only where a doer is named).
- **(d) If the Wall Were Higher (Seedling, Sapling).** Sort each line by its bold verb into statement, command, what-if or formal ask, and "would". Standalone: five café lines. Act IV: the meeting notice and three posters, one per plan, each with a command and a "would". **Talk or letter?** sets "if I was" beside "if I were" and says both are heard in talk.
- **(e) The Editor's Word List (Sapling).** Tap each Year 6 word, then its example (names only; use is taught in 259, 264 and 278). **Say it precisely:** pick the comment the editor would print.
- **(f) Old Pages, Old Books (Keeper's Desk).** Grahame, Shakespeare and Strunk cards (name, quote, effect), and after page 4 Hollis's "The Sponge is drained and the brook cut straight", with a grown-up "say why".
