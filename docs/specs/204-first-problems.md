## Purpose

Mathematical Thinking has a cluster of pinned "practice" topics for the youngest explorers that no spec teaches: making sense of a problem, choosing a tool, modelling with objects, moving between real life and math, connecting representations, showing and explaining working, finding quicker ways, using structure, and planning a two-step problem. Existing benches practice these habits in passing; none teaches them. This pack takes twelve untouched topics there, mostly at Sprout and Seedling depth.

A child comes to understand that a word problem is a little story to understand before any number is touched; that a puzzle has things we know, one thing we want, and sometimes numbers that do not help; that "we can't tell yet" can be the right answer; that a tool is chosen because it fits; that objects should do what the story does; that math hides in real scenes and a number sentence can become a story; that an answer needs its words ("3 cents change"); that one situation can be shown as counters, a picture, a number line, a number sentence and a bar, with each number keeping its job; that working can be shown with objects or a drawing and justified with "because"; that counting on, swapping the order, jumping a ten and reusing a known fact are quicker routes to the same total; and that a long job is planned, kept in view and checked.

**Builds on, never re-teaches:** spec 210's addition and subtraction within 20, number bonds, symbols and early word-problem types (here: used as the vehicle for sense-making and representation, never taught as facts); spec 194's pattern spotting (here: using a noticed pattern on purpose); spec 53's tens and ones (here: +10 on a hundred square, recap only); spec 38's equal groups and division as an unknown factor (here: one array read as four facts, recap only); spec 50's equals sign (here: a single balance item); spec 63's learner-posed delivery problems (here: telling a story for a given number sentence, no free text); spec 33's twelve footbridge planks (here: the setting of one two-step problem). **Not targeted:** the older multi-step, modelling, precision, tool-choice and justification topics owned by sibling spec 205; number bonds and early word problems owned by spec 210; shape classification topics owned by the geometry packs (F7 uses only the rule "4 sides and 4 square corners" as a structure check, with no claim).

## The knowledge

**Sense-making first.** A word problem describes a situation. Read it as a story; say what is happening and what we want to find out before any number appears. A numberless staged reveal (story, then question, then numbers) keeps the child reasoning about relationships before a number invites a sum. Sort the parts into **we know**, **we want** and **not needed**. Some stories cannot be answered: "we can't tell yet" is a correct answer when a piece is missing, and an incorrect one when the story gives enough. Cue words are never taught ("altogether means add" is a trap).

**Tools and models.** A tool is chosen because it fits the numbers: fingers are quick for small numbers and run out past ten; a ten frame shows how many spaces are left to ten; counters and cubes can be joined and split. The reason is part of the practice. A model must do what the story does: eating is a taking-away, arriving is a putting-together. The answer is read back as something about the story ("3 apples left"), and checked against it (more apples after eating is impossible).

**Life to math and back.** Math can be spotted in a scene (how many, how much, how long, which is more) and a number sentence can become a story in which every number has a job. An answer needs its words and units ("3 cents change", "14 cm long"); a bare number is half an answer. A sense-check catches answers the story rules out.

**Connecting representations.** Counters, a picture, a number line, a number sentence and a bar model can all show one story. Tapping a number lights up its job in every form. The bar model shows structure: the whole on top, the parts beneath, ? where the unknown sits; one bar supports several correct sentences (5 + ? = 12, 12 − 5 = ?). The equals sign means both sides are the same amount (8 + 4 = ☐ + 5 gives 7, not 12).

**Showing and explaining.** Working can be shown with objects, pointing or a drawing; a drawing counts. "How do you know?" is answered by showing what was done, not by restating the answer. At Seedling the child judges a peer's method, not only the answer. At Sapling the child gives a "because" reason, uses a second method that agrees, and finds and explains a slip in a worked example (9 + 5 written as 10 + 5). Spoken explanations are to a grown-up in the room and are never recorded, stored or graded; a quiet child may be reasoning well.

**Quicker ways and structure.** Count all, count on, or use a known fact: same total, fewer counts (6 + 3: nine, three, none). Adding in any order gives the same total, so start from the bigger number (2 + 9 as 9 + 2); this does not work for subtraction. On a hundred square, +10 moves straight down a row and changes only the tens digit (47 + 10 = 57). At Sapling: +100 changes only the hundreds digit (352 + 100 = 452); one array gives four facts (6 × 4 = 24, 4 × 6 = 24, 24 ÷ 4 = 6, 24 ÷ 6 = 4); a square fits the rule "4 sides and 4 square corners", so it belongs with rectangles.

**Longer jobs.** Plan the steps (know, want, what first?), keep the whole picture in view with a bar so the child does not stop after step one, leave out a true number that does not help, then look back by checking another way (9 + 3 = 12).

**Real anchors (see audit).**
- **Pólya's four steps.** George Pólya (born in Budapest in 1887) wrote *How to Solve It* at Stanford; Princeton University Press published it in 1945. It sets out four phases: understand the problem, devise a plan, carry out the plan, look back. Pip's plan card ("Know and want, Plan, Do, Look back") is a child-sized version, credited on card H1.
- **Near Eastern clay tokens.** Small clay counters (cones, spheres, disks, cylinders) were used across the ancient Near East from about 8000 or 7500 BCE to about 3000 BCE, one token for one unit of goods. Later some were sealed in hollow clay envelopes (bullae). Denise Schmandt-Besserat argues the system led to writing; other scholars dispute parts of that, and child text says so. The Nuzi hollow tablet (about 1400 BCE) is `[check]` (sources give 48 or 49 counters) and stays out of child text.

## The game or activity

**(a) The Basket Table, model `basket-card-v1`.** Picture puzzle cards from neighbors. Each card reveals its story, then its question, then its numbers, one tap at a time. Tiles sort into **We know / We want / Not needed**; a **We can't tell yet** button is always present; **Retell it** offers three retellings to choose from. *A made-up model: the cards, neighbors and amounts are authored.* Six cards, including one unanswerable card and one with an extra number.

**(b) The Tool Tray, model `tool-tray-v1`.** Fingers (up to ten), counters, two ten frames and joinable cubes. The child picks a tool, acts the story out and reads the answer back with its words. Fingers visibly run out past ten. A prompt asks "Why did that tool help?" (spoken, nothing stored). *A made-up model: a finite set of tools, each holding at most 20 objects.*

**(c) The Story Window, model `story-window-v1`.** Two painted scenes (the market stall, the pond). **Spot**: tap an object and pick a question starter (How many, How much, Which is more, How long). **Story builder**: for a given number sentence (3 + 4, 5 − 2, 6 + 1), snap tiles for who, what happens and how many, then **Read my story** reads the assembled tiles aloud. No free text. *A made-up model: scenes and story tiles are authored.*

**(d) The Four Ways Board, model `four-ways-v1`.** One story shown as counters, picture, number line and number sentence, plus a bar from Seedling up. Tapping any number highlights the same number in every form with its job ("The 4 is the sacks at first in every way"). Match mode asks the child to pair stories with forms. *A made-up model: stories within 20 and authored forms.*

**(e) The Show-Me Slate, model `slate-v1`.** A 12 × 8 grid with four stamps (dot, ring, jump arc, bar), Undo and **Keep my picture**. **Tell a grown-up** opens sentence starters ("First I... Then I... So... I know because...") and closes; it records nothing. At Sapling a worked example from Pip appears with a slip to find. *A made-up model: the slate stores only stamp codes and grid positions.*

**(f) The Quick-Way Path, model `quick-path-v1`.** A number track to 20 and a hundred square. The child solves the same sum several ways (Count all, Count on, Use a fact, Swap the order, Jump a ten) and a tag says how many hops each took. Sapling adds a three-digit place-value strip (+1, +10, +100) and a 6 × 4 array that shows its four facts. *A made-up model: hops are counted by the game; every way gives the same total.*

No timers, scores, streaks, medals or locks. Every bench is open from the first visit.
