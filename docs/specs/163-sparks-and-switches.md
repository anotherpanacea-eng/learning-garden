## Purpose

[Six-act strands](../six-act-strands.md) lists 28 Marble Energy topics under **S6** with none covered; the circuit topics sit there. The built [Lantern Circuit Bench (spec 98)](98-lantern-circuit-bench.md) at `#workshop/lantern` teaches one thing well: a filament bulb glows only on a complete path from one cell end through both bulb contacts and the filament to the other. It deliberately leaves out switches, materials, diagrams, more than one bulb, static and magnetism. Proposed specs 147 (energy), 154 (magnetic materials) and 146 (thunder count) border it and are linked, not repeated.

A child comes to understand that conductors (mostly metals) let electricity through and insulators do not; that a switch is a gap you can close and open, and "open" means off; that a circuit diagram maps connections with agreed symbols, where position is free and connections are fixed; that on one path (series) a single break darkens every lamp, while separate paths (parallel) let each lamp work alone; that rubbing moves electric charge from one material to another, charged things push or pull across a gap, and a spark (lightning is a huge one) is charge jumping back; and, at older depth, that a coil carrying current around an iron nail makes a magnet you can switch off. Cards on Volta's pile (1800) and Ørsted's needle (1820) show how people found out.

**Safety is a fixed rule; no model or number decides it.** Mains (sockets, plugs, anything that plugs in) is never for experiments; nobody goes near a fallen outdoor wire (the storm setting makes this one essential); experiments use only AA or AAA cells, with a grown-up leading. The exact wording, in the companion, appears on arrival, on every bench and in the Grown-up panel.

## The knowledge

**Revisit, never re-teach:** T1 recalls spec 98's complete path.

**Conductors and insulators.** A conductor lets electricity through; an insulator does not, or hardly at all. Metals (steel, copper, aluminum foil, brass, coins) conduct well. Wood, plastic, rubber, glass, paper, wool, cork and cloth insulate. The test decides, not looks: pencil graphite (carbon, not lead) conducts, less well than metal. A wire is a metal core (conductor) in a plastic coat (insulator) that keeps electricity in the metal and off fingers. Tap and brook water carry dissolved salts and conduct (pure water hardly does), so water is never on the bench.

**Switches.** Two contacts, a gap, and a conducting bridge that closes it. **Closed** completes the path (lamp on); **open** breaks it (off). Everyday "open" means the opposite, so the words are taught as a pair. A switch makes no electricity; it only makes or breaks the path. A break anywhere in one loop stops it, so a switch works at any position; two switches in one loop must both be closed.

**Symbols and diagrams.** A **cell** is a long and a short line (long is positive); a **battery** is two or more cells, drawn as repeated cell symbols; a **lamp** is a circle with a cross; a **switch** is drawn open or closed; a **buzzer** has its own symbol; wires are straight lines. A diagram shows what connects to what, not where parts sit; slide a symbol along the loop and it is the same circuit.

**Series and parallel (on and off only).** In **series**, everything is on one path: a dead or missing bulb, or one open switch, darkens every lamp. In **parallel**, each lamp has its own branch: a dead bulb darkens only its branch, and a branch switch controls only its lamp. Older plug-in holiday strings were wired in series, so one failed bulb could darken the lot; many modern ones add a bypass in each bulb. It stays in a Keeper's note and the Grown-up panel, never an activity, because those lights run on mains. **Brightness is not modeled.** A Keeper's note gives the ideal-cell picture: identical bulbs in series each glow dimmer than one alone; in parallel each glows like one alone, and the cell runs down sooner. Current and voltage are named only qualitatively (the flow round the loop; the push from the cells).

**Static electricity.** Rubbing two different materials moves tiny negative charges (electrons) from one to the other; no charge is made. A balloon rubbed on hair gains electrons (negative); the hair, missing them, is positive. Opposites pull, likes push, both across a gap. A charged balloon also sticks to an uncharged wall by pushing the wall's nearby electrons back a little (Keeper's Desk), so a pull does not prove opposite charge; only a push shows like charges. Charge builds up on insulators; on metal held in a hand it flows away. A **spark** is charge jumping across air; a door-handle shock is one. **Lightning** is the same at enormous size: air normally insulates, but when charge differences grow large enough a discharge crosses it. The National Weather Service gives the air in the channel up to about 50,000 °F (about 28,000 °C); it bursts outward: thunder. Spec 146's count (about 3 seconds per kilometer) estimates distance and is never a safety decision. The National Weather Service rule is used: if you can hear thunder, go indoors.

**Electromagnets (Sapling and Keeper's Desk).** A wire carrying current swings a nearby compass needle: current makes magnetism. Coil insulated wire many turns around a soft iron nail and the nail is a magnet while current flows. Switch off and soft iron drops nearly all its magnetism, so the clips fall; a steel bolt keeps a little. More turns, more current (more cells) and an iron core each make it stronger; a copper rod adds nothing, because copper is not magnetic. Fields and motors come later.

**Real history.** On 20 March 1800, Alessandro Volta (born in Como, professor of physics at Pavia) wrote to Joseph Banks, president of the Royal Society in London, describing a **pile**: copper (or silver) and zinc discs stacked in turns, separated by cloth or card soaked in salt water. A wire from top to bottom gave a steady current, the first battery to do so; the volt is named after him. Franklin had used "battery" earlier for linked jars that stored charge (1748 or 1749). In spring 1820 Hans Christian Ørsted in Copenhagen saw a compass needle swing when current ran through a nearby wire, during a lecture, and published that July; accounts differ on whether it was an accident (his own later account says he planned the test), and the card says so.

## The game or activity

Every bench shows the fixed safety rule and its own model line. No timers, scores, streaks, medals or locks.

**(a) Tester Bench, `gap-tester-v1`.** Spec 98's cell and bulb with two clips and a gap. Tap an object, predict (Glow / Stay dark), then **Put it in the gap**; the bulb glows if the authored table marks the touched part a conductor, and the result drops into a two-column table. Sixteen: steel nail, copper wire, foil, brass key, coin, pencil graphite, a wire at its bare ends, the same wire on its plastic coat, wooden peg, plastic button, rubber band, glass marble, paper, wool thread, Bramble's ribbon, a cork. *A picture of real testers; it tests no real object.*

**(b) Diagram Desk, `loop-four-slots-v1`.** A square loop with four slots; tiles: cell, lamp, buzzer, switch (tap to open or close), wire, gap. A request card asks for a circuit. Rules in order: a gap or open switch gives an **open path**; no cell gives **no push**; cells but no lamp or buzzer **pauses the model** in spec 98's words, plus the fixed line "Real cells joined by bare wire get hot"; otherwise lamps glow and buzzers show a sound mark (no audio). Cells face the same way; the model buzzer works either way round (many real ones do not). **Picture or diagram?** shows a tangled drawing of real wires beside three tidy diagrams to match. Enumerated: 2,401 fillings; 304 work, 1,776 open, 256 no cell, 65 paused. *A made-up model: on or off only.*

**(c) Lantern String, `lantern-string-v1`.** Two to six lanterns, one battery, laid out as **One path** or **Own paths**. Tap a lantern to cycle **working**, **dead bulb**, **missing**; a main switch, plus a switch per branch in Own paths. One path: all glow if every lantern works and every switch is closed, else none. Own paths: a lantern glows if it works and its branch and main switches are closed. The child predicts how many will glow, then lights the bridge. *A made-up model: on or off only.* Its model line adds that real series bulbs share the push, so a long string may not visibly glow: the rule answers whether a path exists.

**(d) Static Corner, `charge-tally-v1`.** Each **Rub** moves 2 minus marks from Bramble's hair to the balloon (cap 5 rubs); the hair shows matching plus marks, with the line "Charges made: 0. Charges moved: {n}." Bring the balloon near: hair (pulls), a second rubbed balloon (pushes), paper bits (pull), the wall (sticks; why at Keeper's Desk). **Touch the door handle** shows a small spark mark and the tallies even out. Marks are "tiny bits of charge" at Seedling and electrons from Sapling. *A simplified picture: real balloons move many millions of electrons.*

**(e) Coil and Nail, `coil-nail-v1`.** Turns 10, 20 or 30; cells 1 or 2; core soft iron nail, steel bolt, copper rod or none. Clips lifted = (turns ÷ 10) × cells × core factor (2 for iron and steel, 0 otherwise): 2, 4, 6 with one cell; 4, 8, 12 with two. **Switch off**: iron drops every clip, steel keeps 1. A compass by the wire swings while on. *A made-up model: real electromagnets are less tidy, and a coil on a real battery gets hot. On screen only.*
