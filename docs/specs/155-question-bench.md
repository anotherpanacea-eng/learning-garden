## Purpose

Spec 49's Act V asks the learner to originate a project: choose an intention, find a knowledge gap, plan an inquiry, make a first version, meet evidence, revise. It also says an open project "cannot stand in for unprovided lessons in ... scientific method". Spec 120's Act V hands the explorer blank pages headed *For the next Keeper*. Nothing built yet teaches the thinking those pages need.

This pack builds on, and does not re-teach, the **Fair Test Bench** (`#workshop/fair`, specs 56 and 60: one factor, confounded pairs, repeats, claims apart from observations), the **Water Evidence Shelf** (`#science/evidence`, spec 82: clue versus route) and **plant study** and the **Field Notebook** (`#living/plants`, `#living/notebook`: tables and two bars). The Fair Test Bench already shows each side's minimum to maximum and says when ranges overlap, so bench (c) recaps that in one line and adds only what is new: range as a number, the mean, an odd reading, and precision versus accuracy. **Repeat a Delivery Block** (`#workshop/repeats`, spec 71) is a programming loop, not repeated measurement; noted so nobody links it by mistake.

Of the pinned Marble Scientific Inquiry domain's 30 topics, three have scoped references; the **meta** habits Act V runs on have none. A child comes to understand that:

1. A wondering becomes a scientific question when it names something you could **watch, try or look up**. Some good questions are not fair tests, and questions of taste are not settled by evidence.
2. In a fair test you name **the one thing you change**, **the thing you measure** and **the things you keep the same**, and can say *why* each is kept the same.
3. One result can mislead. Repeats show the **spread**; overlapping spreads mean "can't tell yet". An odd reading is noted and checked, never quietly deleted.
4. When two things rise together, something else may cause both, the arrow may point the other way, or it may be chance. A fair test, not the pattern alone, can show a cause.
5. A surprise is worth more than a confirmation. You change your **idea**, never the recorded result. Science does this too: continental drift was doubted for decades, then accepted on new evidence. Revisable is different from "anything goes".

## The knowledge

Facts are checked in the [source audit](../source-audits/155-question-bench.md); no `[check]` items remain open. Vocabulary follows the built benches: change, measure, keep the same, observation, explanation, claim.

**Ways to find out.** Watching (sometimes over time), trying (a test), looking for patterns, sorting, and research. Questions of taste ("Which cake is nicest?") are real questions that evidence alone cannot settle. A testable question names something to change or compare and something to notice: "Does bread in the tin stay soft longer than bread on the board?"

**Change, measure, keep.** The thing you change (older readers: independent variable), the thing you measure (dependent), the things you keep the same (controlled). Each kept factor has a reason: if it differed too, you could not tell which difference made the result. The Fair Test Bench already shows a confounded pair; this bench adds naming and justifying roles, and choosing a way to find out so the fair test is not the default.

**Repeats and spread (a made-up model).** Paper spinners from the library balcony, seconds: long wings 2.4, 2.6, 2.5 (range 0.2, mean 2.5); short wings 1.8, 2.0, 2.2 (range 0.4, mean 2.0). Every long time exceeds every short time. Pip's single tries (2.4, 2.2) told him little. Bramble's plain (2.5, 2.1, 2.7) and ribbon-tail (2.4, 2.6, 2.2) spinners overlap: "can't tell yet". An odd reading (2.5, 2.6, 4.1, noted "caught on a shelf") stays in the record, marked, and the try is repeated.

**Precision and accuracy (Keeper's Desk only).** Precision is how closely repeats agree; accuracy is how close results are to the true value (NIST). The baker's 500 g weight: bakery scale 512, 511, 513 g (mean 512, range 2: precise, 12 g heavy); market scale 494, 506, 500 g (mean 500, range 12). Averaging repeated readings can reduce random uncertainty in the mean; they cannot remove a steady offset. No significant figures.

**Together or because (a made-up model).** Puff's café week (table in the companion): 16 to 28 °C, ice creams 9 to 40, sun hats 1 to 8; sorted by temperature, both rise at every step. Three routes to check: a **hidden cause** (warm sunny days), a **backwards arrow** (rain brings umbrellas) and **chance**. A deciding test holds the weather the same and changes only the hats.

**Predict, then look.** Ice cubes on a metal tray and a wooden board at room temperature: the metal, which feels colder, melts its ice first, because metal carries warmth into the ice much faster than wood (Nuffield Foundation, UCSC, NSTA). The bench's minutes (about 9 and 20) are made up; the direction is real. Conduction is named once; no heat lesson is claimed.

**Science can be revised: continental drift (a true thing from science).**
- In January 1912 the German meteorologist Alfred Wegener presented the idea that the continents were once joined and had moved apart; his book followed in 1915 (USGS; Britannica).
- His evidence: the fit of South America's and Africa's coasts (noticed earlier by the mapmaker Ortelius), matching rocks, and the same fossils on lands now an ocean apart, such as the small water reptile *Mesosaurus* (South America and southern Africa) and the seed fern *Glossopteris* (the southern continents and India) (USGS; UC Museum of Paleontology; Britannica). *Mesosaurus*'s habitat is debated, so the text names none.
- Most geologists rejected it for decades, mainly because no force able to move continents was known; the forces he suggested were shown to be far too weak (NASA Earth Observatory; Britannica). Their doubt was reasonable for the evidence of the time.
- Ocean-floor evidence in the 1960s changed that. Harry Hess's seafloor spreading idea circulated from 1959 and was published in 1962; in 1963 Vine and Matthews (and, separately, Morley) explained the seafloor's magnetic stripes as a record of new crust forming at ridges. Seafloor spreading was widely accepted in the later 1960s, and plate tectonics by the late 1960s and early 1970s (USGS; Geological Society of London; Geological Survey of Canada; Britannica).
- Spec 139 carries the plate geology and not the drift history; this pack uses its plate wording and carries only the change of mind. Wegener's death in 1930 is left out.

**Revisable is not "anything goes".** An idea is held because evidence supports it and changes when better evidence arrives, not when someone powerful insists. Old ideas were often reasonable; revision is an upgrade, never a joke at past scientists' expense.

## The lesson

Jane's order throughout: **teach** (one idea, one worked example revealed a step per tap), **guided** (first item shows 1 to 4 steps), then **practice**.

| (b) Change, measure, keep | The baker's drying cloths, each factor into its tray. | First factor placed. | Two changes vs one; the thing you set vs the thing you read. |
| (c) Once is not enough | Long-wing tries placed one by one on a number line. | Lowest and highest of three dots. | Separate vs overlapping spreads (a revisit of spec 56); noted vs deleted odd reading; precise vs accurate (Keeper's Desk). |
| (d) Two things together | The café week sorted by temperature. | "What else could make both go up?" | Hidden cause vs cause; backwards arrow; pattern vs fair test. |
| (e) I changed my mind | The locked ice prediction; then the continental drift panels. | "What was missing?" with the clue shown. | Changing the idea vs changing the record; revisable vs anything goes. |

**"I'm stuck" ladder** on every practice item: a hint; then a worked example of an easier similar case plus how to start this one; then **Skip for now**, with nothing lost or recorded against the explorer. Help stays separate from independent attempts.

**Misconceptions designed against:** guesses or opinions taken for questions, a fair test for everything, swapped or over-changed variables, one result is enough (and its underclaim twin, repeats tell nothing), overlap read as equal, hiding an odd reading, precise taken as accurate, together read as because, the first fit wins, changing the data, wrong means failed, old ideas were foolish, and revised means untrustworthy or anything goes. Each is a named tag on a wrong choice in the companion.

## The game or activity

- **(a) From wonder to question.** Six Wonder Board slips; tap one, then a mat: **Watch it**, **Try it**, **Look it up or ask**, **Evidence can't settle it**. Slips move back freely. The **sharpening frame** takes one "something to notice" chip and one "something to compare" chip and reads the question back; many pairs work.
- **(b) Change, measure, keep.** A question card and three trays. Five factor tokens are tapped then placed; a Keep token flips to "Why?" with three reason chips. (Matching questions to ways of finding out lives in bench (a) and A5, not here.)
- **(c) Once is not enough.** A number line from 1.5 to 3.0 s. **Drop again** reveals the next of three authored tries per spinner (no fourth pretends to be new). Toggles: **Show the spread**, **Show the middle** (Sapling and up). Bramble's pair overlaps; the odd reading carries a note icon. Keeper's Desk adds a target picture for the two scales. Every screen: *A made-up model.*
- **(d) Two things together.** The café table, **Sort by temperature**, and a paired bar chart (patterns and text labels, never color alone). Three **escape-route cards** matched to three cases; then **Which test would decide?**.
- **(e) I changed my mind.** **Lock in my guess** before **Show the result**. A frame ("I guessed... I saw... So now I think...") fills from codes; the record line never changes. Then six continental drift panels tapped open in order, and a two-card sort: "Held because of evidence" or "Anything goes".
- **My question card** (the Act V hand-off). Built from chips and codes: a question (from (a) or 8 authored Act V questions), a way to find out, change/measure/keep tokens for a test, a locked prediction, and **What would change my mind?** (three authored answers or "talk it over with a grown-up"). Kept, edited or removed; never graded.

## Saved work

Optional root `questionBench`, per explorer, absent until the first explicit keep. A sketch for review:

```
questionBench: {
  version: 1,
  cards: [{ id, source, question: code, way: 'watch'|'try'|'lookup'|'taste',
            change?: code, measure?: code, keep?: [code], prediction?: code,
            mindChanger?: code, kept: boolean, at }],                        // latest 12
  attempts: [{ itemId, source, choice?: code, entry?: number,
               support: 'none'|'hint'|'example', skipped: boolean, at }],    // latest 60
  predictions: [{ caseId, source, guess: code, shownResult: code, at }]      // latest 12
}
```

- Codes, numbers and stable ids only; **no free text is stored**. "Say why" answers are spoken to a grown-up. `source` freezes the content version.
- Support, skips and attempts are separate fields; nothing derives mastery or a score. A prediction is saved with the result shown and never edited.
- Never rewrite a saved entry on load. An unknown version or malformed entry stays exact, exportable and read-only; one unreadable item never closes the bench.
- Saves follow an explicit action and the existing progress limits; at a cap the oldest readable entry is replaced only after a disclosed notice; unreadable entries are never evicted. Export, import, reset and explorer switching use shared behavior.
