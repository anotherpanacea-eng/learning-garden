## Purpose

Earth's Systems and Weather & Climate are still thin on the map. [Spec 140](140-weather-station.md) owns instruments, the water cycle and weather versus climate; [143](143-letters-from-the-coast.md) owns tides, waves and currents as causes; [152](152-up-through-the-canopy.md) owns the rainforest's own rain; [147](147-mills-energy.md) owns renewable versus non-renewable; [149](149-orchard-year.md), [151](151-meadow-comes-back.md) and [174](174-breath-and-heartbeat.md) own photosynthesis, matter cycling and respiration. Nobody yet teaches **where Earth's water is and how little is fresh**, **climate zones**, **the atmosphere as a thing with weight and layers**, the **ocean as a climate engine**, **forests and wet soils as carbon stores**, where **fuels come from and what using them does**, or the **carbon cycle** as one balanced system.

This pack builds on 140 rather than re-teaching it: the water cycle is 140's, and here it gains a map (where the water sits), a scale (how much is fresh), and a driver (the ocean). Weather versus climate is 140's, and here it becomes zones on a globe. 140's barometer becomes air with weight.

A child comes to understand that water is in oceans, rivers, lakes, ice and under the ground, and that ice is water; that about 97 of every 100 cups of Earth's water is salty and most of the fresh rest is frozen or underground (shown on a graph the child builds); that climate zones run in bands from the Equator to the poles, bent by height and rain, and that **desert means dry**; that air has weight, presses every way, and wraps Earth in a thin coat whose lowest layer holds the weather; that the ocean stores warmth, feeds the rain and carries warmth in currents; that fuels and other sources come from nature and each has effects; that a forest or marsh stores carbon and **flips** to a source when burned or drained; and, at the Keeper's Desk, that carbon goes round and is never used up, and burning old fuel tips a balance.

## The knowledge

**Where water is (Sprout).** Oceans, rivers, lakes, glaciers and snow, and underground (wells and springs show it); for readier explorers, invisible water vapor in the air (clouds are tiny drops, not vapor). Ice and snow are water in solid form. The oceans hold most of Earth's water. Land-and-water words (ocean, river, lake, glacier) are pinned to pictures inline. Observational at this depth: no numbers.

**Salty and fresh (Seedling to Sapling).** USGS's table (rounded, from a 1993 estimate) gives the oceans about 96.5% of Earth's water and fresh water about 2.5%; with salty groundwater and salt lakes, about 97 of every 100 parts are salty. Of fresh water, about 69% is ice caps, glaciers and permanent snow, about 30% is groundwater, and only about 1% is surface water. The **hundred cups** model rounds this to 97 salty and 3 fresh, of which about 2 are ice, about 1 underground, and fresh rivers and lakes less than a spoonful (about 0.007 of a cup, under half a teaspoon, computed from USGS shares of all water). The child builds a bar graph from a table and reads it; a bar shows a share of the whole, not an amount you could carry. Some lakes are salty, and so is much deep groundwater (USGS lists more saline than fresh groundwater): the model line says so.

**Climate zones (Sapling).** Tropical (hot and wet near the Equator), temperate (mild, four seasons), polar (freezing), arid (very dry) and mountain (cold because high). Bands follow how directly sunlight strikes (a flashlight straight down or tilted), never distance from the Sun. Height bends the pattern: a peak near the Equator can be snowy (Chimborazo); the cold is from height, not ray angle. **Desert means dry:** Antarctica, with very little snowfall, is a cold desert ("largest desert" is common but kept out of child text until checked). A zone is the pattern over many years (140's climate), and zones blend gradually. Each zone's plants, animals and ways of life suit it.

**The atmosphere (Sapling to Keeper's Desk).** Air is matter: a blown-up balloon outweighs a flat one by the squeezed air inside. Air presses in every direction (the card on an upturned glass, on screen only). The atmosphere is a thin shell: on a globe 30 cm (12 in) across, the troposphere (about 10 km; as low as about 7 km over the poles in winter and up to 20 km near the Equator, UCAR) is about 0.24 mm thick, about two sheets of paper (computed). The troposphere holds about 75-80% of the air's mass and almost all weather; temperature falls with height **within** it. Above, the stratosphere's ozone layer absorbs much of the Sun's harmful ultraviolet. A second job: some gases hold warmth near the ground, and ozone is one of them (NOAA, Twenty Questions Q17), so the text separates the two jobs, never two sets of gases. Air flowing from higher to lower pressure is wind (inline support only). At the Keeper's Desk: air near the ground is about 1.2 kg per cubic meter, so a 5 × 4 × 2.5 m room holds about 60 kg; the air above each square centimeter at sea level weighs about 1 kg (1.03, computed).

**The ocean engine (Sapling).** Three jobs, then one system. (1) A store: water warms and cools slowly, so coasts swing less than inland places; the ocean also soaks up heat and carbon dioxide, which slows warming of the air, but the store has limits. (2) A rain feeder: USGS says the oceans supply about 90% of the water that evaporates into the cycle. (3) A mover: currents carry warmth poleward; the Gulf Stream helps keep western Europe milder in winter than eastern Canada at the same latitude (Met Office). Winds and latitude also matter; text never says "only the ocean". No percentages of heat or carbon uptake (held for later study).

**Fuels and their effects (Sapling).** Energy sources come from natural resources. Wood from trees (regrows over years to decades); coal from plants buried millions of years ago; oil and gas from tiny sea organisms buried millions of years ago; flowing water refilled by rain and snowmelt; wind; sunlight. Each card pairs where it comes from with what using it does: burning gives carbon dioxide and smoke; mining scars land; spills harm the sea; a dam's lake floods habitat; turbines and panels need space and work only when the wind blows or the Sun shines. Renewable versus non-renewable is spec 147's sort; this pack revisits it once. Electricity is a carrier (147).

**Forests, wet soils and carbon (Sapling to Keeper's Desk).** A growing tree builds wood from carbon dioxide; a standing forest is a carbon store and its leaves release water vapor that feeds nearby rain (152's loop). Burned, its carbon goes back as carbon dioxide; cleared and left to rot, more slowly. Clearing is a **double hit**: stored carbon released, and no more taken in. Regrowth takes decades. Rainforests are not "the lungs of the planet": a grown forest uses about as much oxygen as it makes (National Geographic, 2025); scientists estimate that roughly half of Earth's oxygen production comes from ocean plankton (NOAA; child text says "about half", hedged). Most forest cleared worldwide is cleared for farming (FAO: almost 9 in 10, crops and grazing). Peat is soil made of plants that rotted slowly in water; peatlands cover about 3% of land and store more carbon than all other vegetation combined (IUCN); drained, they give carbon back.

**The carbon cycle (Keeper's Desk).** Carbon moves between air, plants, animals, soil and old fuel underground by photosynthesis (taking in), feeding, respiration (giving back), decomposition and combustion. It is conserved, never used up. Plants respire too. Natural taking-in and giving-back roughly balance; burning fossil fuel releases long-stored carbon more quickly than plants and sea can take it back, and clearing forests reduces the taking-in. Fast living cycle versus slow fossil store.

**History cards (real, checked).**
- **The Persian qanat.** Gently sloping tunnels carry groundwater by gravity from valley heads to towns; estimates of the oldest run from about 800 BCE to about 3,000 years ago (child text says only "very old"); eleven qanats in Iran were inscribed together on the World Heritage List in 2016, and communal water-sharing still runs them (UNESCO).
- **Périer on the Puy de Dôme, 19 September 1648.** At Pascal's request, his brother-in-law Florin Périer carried the Torricelli tube up the mountain; the mercury stood about 3 French inches (about 8 cm, computed) lower at the summit than in the tube left at Clermont. Pascal read it as the air's weight; not every scholar agreed at first.
- **Humboldt and Bonpland on Chimborazo, 1802.** They climbed high on the Ecuadorian volcano without reaching the top (sources differ on how high); Humboldt's 1807 *Tableau physique* drew the Andes with plants in bands by height.
- **Franklin and Folger's Gulf Stream chart, late 1760s.** Timothy Folger, a Nantucket whaling captain and Franklin's cousin, described and sketched the current (Richardson dates the talk to October 1768; Franklin later recalled "1769 or 70"); the chart was printed about 1769-70; Franklin measured water temperatures on crossings in 1775 and 1776 and published again in 1786.
- **Eunice Foote, 1856.** Two glass cylinders with thermometers in sunlight, one with carbon dioxide and one with air; the carbon dioxide one heated more, and she proposed that more of the gas in the air would give Earth a higher temperature (*American Journal of Science and Arts*, 1856).
- **Charles David Keeling, 1958.** Measurements at Mauna Loa from 1958 (first reading 313 ppm); about 280 ppm before industrialization; above 420 since 2022, with May 2025 above 430 (Scripps, NOAA).

Cultures: Persia, France, the Andes, colonial America and the United States.

## The game or activity

**(a) Where the Water Is.** A picture map of the valley with a far sea and a far glacier, and a spinnable globe. Tap each place to look closer and tag it **Liquid** or **Solid (frozen)**: the sea, Willow Brook, the millpond, snow on the peaks, a glacier, the old well, a spring by the ford. A cutaway shows water in soil and rock below the well. Globe spin: more blue or more land? An optional air card (vapor, with clouds as drops) for readier explorers. No numbers.

**(b) The Hundred Cups, model `cups-v1`.** *A made-up model.* Pour all Earth's water into 100 cups: 97 salty, 3 fresh. **Zoom** enlarges the 3 fresh cups: about 2 ice and snow, about 1 underground, less than a spoonful for rivers and lakes. **Build the graph:** drag four bars to a table's values (97, 2, 1, a sliver); a mismatch asks "How many cups for this one?" Then read a fresh-only graph (ice 69, underground 30, everything else 1; rivers and lakes are only about 0.3 of that 1). Model line: "100 cups is a way to picture shares. The real amounts are rounded, and some lakes and some underground water are salty."

**(c) Climate Bands, model `zones-v1`.** *A made-up model.* Inputs: place `near the Equator`, `in the middle`, `near a pole`; height `low`, `high`; rain `wet`, `dry`. Rule: high → mountain; otherwise dry and not polar → arid; otherwise the band's zone (tropical, temperate, polar). Polar and dry shows the cold-desert line. 12 settings reach all five zones (6 mountain, 2 arid, 2 polar, 1 tropical, 1 temperate; computed). Then match five zone cards to ecosystems and ways of life, and a flashlight card (straight vs tilted). Model line: "Real climates also depend on the sea, the winds and the mountains around."

**(d) The Air Shelf.** Two balloons on a stick balance (tips toward the full one); a squeeze card; the upturned-glass card (on screen only); a **layer board** (drag cloud, rain and airplane into the troposphere; ozone sits in the stratosphere); a **zoom reveal** from Willowbank out to the globe showing the weather layer's thinness; the two-jobs line (ultraviolet shield vs holding warmth, ozone in both); the wind line. Keeper's Desk adds the room-of-air item and Périer's card.

**(e) The Ocean Engine.** Three job cards, then one picture that runs all three. **Coast bench, model `coast-v1`:** *a made-up model.* Two made-up towns the same distance from the Equator: inland 2 °C winter, 26 °C summer; coast 7 °C winter, 19 °C summer (ranges 24 and 12, computed). A current map with warm and cold arrows and the Gulf Stream card. Model line: "Made-up towns and rounded numbers. Real places also depend on winds and mountains."

**(f) Forests, Fuels and Carbon.** Three parts.

1. **Fuel Shelf.** Six sources (wood, coal, oil and gas, flowing water, wind, sunlight), each split into two cards: *where it comes from* and *what using it does*. The child pairs the halves, combining two pieces of information into one source story. A revisit card points to 147's renewable sort.
2. **Forest Flip.** One forest, two panels: **Standing** (carbon in, wood stores it, vapor out toward rain) and **Burned** (carbon dioxide out, no more taken in, less rain nearby); the double-hit line; the why-cleared card; the marsh card linking to Willowbank's wet corner.
3. **Carbon Paths bench, model `carbon-v1` (Keeper's Desk).** *A made-up model.* Five stores start at air 6, plants 4, animals 1, soil 4, old fuel 10 (total 25). Each turn of **Nature only**: plants take in 3; plants breathe out 1; animals eat 1; animals breathe out 1; dead plants rot into soil 1; soil gives back 1. All stores stay level. **Burn old fuel** adds old fuel → air 1 per turn: air 6, 7, 8, 9, 10. **Forest fire, then regrowth**: turn 1 burns 3 plant tokens to air (air 9, plants 1); on each regrowth turn the young forest drops no dead wood, so both rotting arrows rest (nothing new into the soil, nothing out of it); plants gain 1 and air loses 1: air 9, 8, 7, 6, soil stays 4. The bench says this rule on screen. The total shows 25 on every turn. Tap one token to follow it. Model line: "Made-up tokens, not real amounts. The bench shows directions and balance, not how much or how fast."

No timers, scores, streaks, medals or locks. Every activity is open from the first visit.
