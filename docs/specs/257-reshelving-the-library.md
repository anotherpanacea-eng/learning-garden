## The knowledge

Sourced in the [audit](../source-audits/257-reshelving-the-library.md).

**Parts of a book.** The front cover carries the title, usually the author and illustrator, and a picture; the back cover usually carries a short description (a blurb); the title page, inside the front, names the title, author, illustrator and publisher (CCSS RI.K.5). The **author** writes the words and the **illustrator** makes the pictures, and both present the ideas (RI.K.6); one person can do both. A contents page lists parts in page order at the front; headings name each part's focus; a glossary defines a book's special words; an index lists topics alphabetically with page numbers at the back (RI.1.5, RI.2.5; DfE Years 3 to 4 non-statutory guidance: use contents pages and indexes to locate information).

**Kinds of texts** (`mt_gZIo5oiBMt`). Kindergarten standards ask children to recognize storybooks and poems (RL.K.5), and by grade 1 to explain how books that tell stories differ from books that give information (RL.1.5). Here: a story tells what happens to characters; a poem is written in lines and often plays with sound; a fact book tells true things about the world. The categories are working labels for young children; real books mix them, which the grown-up panel notes.

**How fact books are built.** The English national curriculum introduces Year 2 pupils to non-fiction books structured in different ways. Three simple structures are used: time order (*Swallows Come Home*), part by part (*The Busy Hive*) and question and answer (*Ask the Weather*). Comparing structures across texts is 270's.

**Main topic, key details, focus.** The main topic is what a whole text is about; key details are facts that tell more about it (RI.K.2); in a multi-paragraph text each paragraph has its own focus (RI.2.2); key details support a main idea (RI.3.2). A common error is to take one vivid detail, or the first sentence, as the topic.

**Notes and summary.** Years 3 to 4 pupils retrieve and record information from non-fiction and identify main ideas across paragraphs, summarizing them (DfE). Notes keep the important fact in a few words; a table gives each fact a box; a summary covers every part briefly and is not a copied chunk.

**Linking ideas.** Time and order words (first, next, finally), cause words (because, so, as a result) and comparison words (both, but, unlike) show how sentences and paragraphs connect (RI.2.3, RI.3.3, RI.3.8). *Ask the Weather* uses order on page 3, cause and effect on pages 2, 4, 5 and 6.

**Purpose and point of view.** Grade 2 asks for a text's main purpose (RI.2.6, which frames it as what the author wants to answer, explain or describe); grade 3 for distinguishing one's own view from the author's (RI.3.6). "Inform, persuade, entertain" is a common classroom scheme in the US and England, not a standard. It is taught as a starting frame: card 11 says many texts do more than one and asks for the main job, and E1 uses only clear cases. Reasons support an author's point (RI.1.8, RI.2.8). Word choice shows attitude: "a scruffy mess" and "a little meadow" name the same grass.

**Facts inside the books.** Swallows: a deeply forked tail with long streamers; both parents help build a cup of mud, often with grass, lined with feathers (the female usually does more); they eat small flying insects, scarce here in winter; shortening days trigger departure; they cross the sea and the Sahara and winter in Africa south of it; a swallow ringed in Britain was found in South Africa in December 1912 (spec 144 audit S13, S23, S35; Cornell; Songbird Survival). Honeybees: one queen that can lay about 1,500 eggs a day at the early-summer peak; she does not command; tens of thousands of female workers whose jobs follow age, foraging from about three weeks; a few hundred stingless male drones in summer, whose job is mating; the waggle run's direction shows the way and its length the distance (spec 150 audit S6, S7, S17, S27, S28, S33, S38). Weather: rain is caught in a funnel and measured as a depth in millimeters; a gauge under a tree reads low; read at eye level and empty daily; a wind vane's wider tail swings downwind so the arrow points into the wind, and winds are named for where they come from; water evaporates, rises, cools and condenses into cloud, and drops join and fall; rivers keep rising after rain because water takes time to reach them (spec 140 audit).

## The lesson

**Contrasts:** a story about animals vs a fact book about animals; author vs illustrator, and one person doing both; front cover vs title page; contents (parts, in page order) vs index (words, A to Z); a heading vs a glossary entry; one page's focus vs the whole book's topic; a key detail vs a true but unrelated fact; a note vs a copied chunk; order words vs cause words; a fun fact book vs a text written to entertain; an author's view vs a fact; the author's view vs the reader's.

## The game or activity

Every book screen offers read-aloud and the three lengths. No score, timer, lock or mastery label. Drags have tap equivalents; nothing is typed.

- **(a) The Wet Shelves (Sprout).** Seven covers onto three shelves: stories, poems, fact books. Then **Who did which job?**: tap the author, then the illustrator, on each fact-book cover.
- **(b) Cover to Index (Sprout, Seedling).** *Swallows Come Home* as a turnable book. Six questions, each answered by tapping a part (cover, back cover, title page, contents, heading, *Words to know*, index). **Why is it there?** matches four parts to their jobs.
- **(c) What's It Mostly About? (Seedling, Sapling).** *The Busy Hive*: tap the main topic, then two key details; at Sapling, match each page to its focus.
- **(d) The Notes Table (Sapling).** Fill a swallow table from six supplied note cards (three fit); a second card does the bees. No free text.
- **(e) Linking Words (Sapling).** *Ask the Weather*: tap the link words on pages 3 and 4; order the gauge steps; match causes to effects.
- **(f) Why Did They Write It? (Sapling).** Six texts into three purpose trays; the two notices: each author's view and reasons, word choice, then "which do you agree with?" spoken to a grown-up.
