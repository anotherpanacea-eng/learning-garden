## Purpose

The game has a minibeast key (spec 116), garden life stages and food links (specs 65, 66) and a body-clues key (spec 62). It has nothing yet on **Animals of the World**, **Polar Regions** or **Rainforests** in the Marble science taxonomy: no biome, no journey, no animal living far from the garden. Strand S3 (adaptation and classification) has local organisms but no far-away ones, and S4 (biomes) is empty.

This pack makes the explorer a volunteer bird counter at a fictional high pass above Willowbank. The real western Pyrenees lie on a major autumn flyway, and volunteers count migrating birds at passes in the French Basque Country such as Organbidexka (counted since 1979) and Lindus (S4, S5). Through one shared scene (a morning at the pass), a child comes to understand:

- **Migration** is a regular journey there and back, usually yearly, driven by food, daylight for raising young, and breeding places. Cold alone is not the reason.
- **Soaring birds** ride rising warm air and avoid long sea crossings, so they funnel over mountain passes and narrow straits such as Gibraltar. **Flapping birds** can cross open sea.
- Birds find their way with several cues (the Sun, the stars, Earth's magnetic field, landmarks). Some of how this works is still being studied.
- On the way, birds pass through **different habitats**: an Arctic summer, temperate valleys, desert, savanna and tropical rainforest. Each card says one true reason why that place matters to a bird.
- **Body features help** (long broad wings for soaring, pointed wings for fast flapping, fat stored before a long flight), and these features came about over many generations.
- **Birds are one group of vertebrates.** Flying does not make an animal a bird: bats are mammals and butterflies are insects. A penguin is a bird that cannot fly.
- **Counting is science.** A tally becomes a table and a bar chart; a key identifies by features; and one morning's count cannot show whether a population is rising or falling.

## The knowledge

Source numbers in brackets (S1 to S40) refer to the [source audit](../source-audits/144-ways-of-the-birds.md). They were checked from web-search excerpts on 2026-10-25; pages were not opened in full, so the subject review should still read the key papers.

**What migration is.** A regular, usually yearly, journey between two places, there and back. A one-off move is not migration. Migration differs from hibernation: a hazel dormouse stays put and hibernates from about October to April (S38); a migrant travels. Many kinds of animal migrate: birds, mammals such as humpback whales and wildebeest, fish such as salmon, and insects (S41). More than 17 million insects a year, mostly flies such as marmalade hoverflies, were counted passing one narrow Pyrenean pass, Bujaruelo (S18). Painted lady butterflies travel between Europe and Africa over several generations: no single butterfly makes the whole round trip (S19).

**Why birds go.** Food is the big reason. Swallows eat small flying insects, plentiful in a European summer and scarce in winter (S13, S23). Many birds can cope with freezing weather if they have enough food, so cold alone is not the driver (S23). Long summer days give birds more hours to find food (S23), and Arctic breeders time their nesting to a short, huge burst of insects that their chicks depend on (S24). Birds do not plan the trip the way a person would. The urge to migrate is inherited, and shortening days are the main trigger (S23).

**How they find the way.** Birds have a Sun compass (read with their internal clock), a star compass (night migrants read the pattern of stars) and a magnetic compass, and they use landmarks; how these combine is still debated (S20). The magnetic sense seems to work through the eyes and light; a molecule called cryptochrome is the leading idea, but exactly how it works is not yet known (S20). Young cuckoos make their first journey alone, on an inherited program, and never meet their parents (S21). Young cranes stay with their parents through their first autumn and winter, and they are thought to learn the route from them (S21).

**Soaring and flapping.** Over sunny land, warm air rises in columns called **thermals**. Broad-winged birds such as storks, honey buzzards, kites and eagles circle up in a thermal, then glide to the next one, saving energy. Rising air is weak or missing over open sea, so these birds avoid long sea crossings and gather at short ones such as the Strait of Gibraltar and the Bosporus (S22). In crosswinds, kites crossing at Gibraltar fly lower and flap harder, and some drown (S22). The Strait is about 13 to 14 km wide at its narrowest; references differ (S17). Flapping birds such as swallows pay for every wingbeat and cross the Mediterranean and the Sahara (S13). Satellite-tracked white storks averaged about 220 to 290 km a day on migration, depending on the region, and on some days more than 500 km (S3). Cranes do both: powerful flapping in lines or V shapes, with long glides and soaring (S32).

**Real counts.** Volunteers began counting at Organbidexka in 1979, when a group of naturalists rented a hunting pass on the ridge for observation (S5). In autumn 2020 the Organbidexka counters logged about 32,400 black kites, 19,100 honey buzzards, 31,200 wood pigeons and 6,500 cranes (S4). Lindus is a second Basque watch point run with the LPO; in 2025 its counters logged about 5,600 white storks from July to September (S5). These real numbers appear only in Keeper's notes, never as the made-up mornings.

**Timing.** In the western Pyrenees, honey buzzards pass mostly from mid-August to mid-September, with black kites at about the same time (S7). Cranes pass later: the 2020 Organbidexka crane peak was on 5 November (S4), and crane passage has moved about 20 days earlier over 30 years (S7). Wood pigeons pass mostly from mid-October to mid-November (S12). That is why the pack has two made-up mornings, one in early September and one in late October.

**Record holder.** The Arctic tern breeds in the Arctic summer and spends the southern summer near Antarctica. Light loggers on terns from Greenland and Iceland showed an average of about 70,900 km a year (range about 59,500 to 81,600 km), along winding routes with an Atlantic stopover (S1). Terns from the Netherlands flew about 90,000 km (S2). Earth's circumference at the equator is 40,075 km, so a year's trip is roughly one and three-quarters to two and a quarter times around the planet. The oldest known Arctic tern was 34; many live 20 years or more (S36). The pack's lifetime item uses "a tern that lives 30 years" as a could-be case.

**Habitats on the way** (one postcard each in the companion): **polar summer** (north of the Arctic Circle the Sun stays up at midnight in summer (S25); a short burst of insects feeds chicks (S24); the Arctic is mostly ocean ringed by land and Antarctica mostly land ringed by ocean (S25); penguins live almost entirely in the Southern Hemisphere, and none in the Arctic (S26); feathers slow the loss of body heat and do not make heat); **temperate valley** (mild summers, cool winters; migrants stop to rest and feed); **desert** (defined by dryness, usually less than 250 mm of rain a year, not by heat; there are cold deserts (S27)); small songbirds cross the Sahara in different ways: many fly at night and rest in the day, and some fly on into the day or for 40 to 60 hours without stopping (S28); **savanna** (grass with scattered trees south of the Sahara; white storks eat grasshoppers and locusts there and gather in thousands at grass fires and locust swarms (S29)); **tropical rainforest** (near the equator, warm and wet all year; layers from forest floor through understory and canopy to emergent trees, with the most species in the canopy and only about 2% of sunlight reaching the floor (S30); honey buzzards winter at its edges and in moist woodland nearby (S8)).

**Adaptations:** long, broad wings suit soaring; slim pointed wings suit fast flapping. Before the Sahara, garden warblers fatten from about 16 to 18 g to as much as 37 g, roughly doubling their weight (S16). Wing shape and the urge to migrate came about over many generations, because birds with features that suited the journey survived and raised more young. A single bird does not grow new wings because it needs them.

**Classification:** birds are vertebrates (animals with a backbone). Birds have feathers and beaks and lay hard-shelled eggs; not every bird flies (penguins, ostriches). Bats are mammals: they have fur and feed their young on milk (S39; standard biology, but the excerpts came from weak sites, so the reviewer should cite a textbook). Butterflies are insects: invertebrates with six legs and three body parts as adults.

**How scientists know:** **ringing** puts a numbered ring on a bird's leg; when it is found again, the two places tell part of its journey (S13). **Light loggers** record day length and the time of local noon, which give latitude and longitude, roughly, about twice a day (S37); **GPS tags** on larger birds show detailed routes. **Counts** at fixed watch points, done the same way for many seasons, show change over time (S6). **Radar** can detect birds the eye misses; a radar study ran at Lindus in 2013 (S5).

**What one day's count cannot tell you.** Weather and wind shift routes and timing (S22); the peak may fall on another day (S4); some birds now winter in Europe instead of Africa, so fewer crossing need not mean fewer birds (S6, S14). Even long series can disagree about trends: Organbidexka wood pigeon counts fell from the early 1980s, while other networks found no clear trend, and some birds may cross further west, uncounted (S12). A trend needs the same method at the same place across whole seasons and many years.

## The lesson

Jane's order is followed: **teach** (one idea and a worked example), **guided** (first item with 1 to 4 visible steps), **practice**.

1. **Teach:** "Migration is a journey there and back, every year." Worked example: a swallow's year shown as two map arrows and two season labels.
2. **Teach:** "Birds go where the food is." Worked example: a swallow's food in a Willowbank summer, then in a Willowbank winter.
3. **Teach:** "Soaring birds ride warm air, so they avoid the sea." Worked example: a stork rising in a thermal over land, then the thermal fading over water.
4. **Teach:** "A key names a bird by asking about features." Worked example: the key walked through for one crane.
5. **Guided:** tally the first eight birds of the made-up September morning, with visible steps (look, use the key, add a mark, every fifth mark crosses the four).
6. **Practice:** the rest of the count, the bar chart, Map the journey, Why go? and the Habitat postcards, then the problem sets.

**"I'm stuck" ladder,** on every practice item and activity step: (1) a hint; (2) a worked example of an easier similar case, plus how to start this one; (3) skip for now, with no penalty and nothing recorded against the explorer.

**The contrast** that marks the boundary: a stork (soars, avoids the sea, funnels over the pass) beside a swallow (flaps, can cross the sea and the Sahara). For classification: a bat beside a swallow (both fly; only one has feathers). For migration: a migrating stork beside a hibernating dormouse (one travels, one stays and sleeps; S38).

**Top misconceptions designed against** (each tagged on at least one wrong choice in the companion):

- `cold-only`: birds leave just because they feel cold.
- `any-journey`: any long trip is migration, there and back or not.
- `only-birds`: only birds migrate.
- `just-know`: birds "just know" the way, with no cues.
- `plan`: birds decide and plan the route like a person.
- `flying-means-bird`: anything that flies is a bird (bat, butterfly).
- `swims-means-fish`: a penguin is a fish because it swims.
- `penguins-north`: penguins live in the Arctic too.
- `fur-makes-heat`: feathers make heat (they slow heat loss).
- `lifetime-adapt`: a bird grows the wings it needs in its own life.
- `desert-hot`: a desert is any hot place.
- `one-day-trend`: one day's count shows a population trend.
- `bar-items`: a bar chart shows each bird, not each kind's total.
- `local-counted`: every bird in the sky counts as a migrant.

## The game or activity

### (a) The count at the pass

A made-up morning at the High Notch plays as a sequence of **sightings**. Each sighting shows a lifelike drawing of a real species in flight (silhouette against sky, plus one behavior animation: circling, gliding straight, flapping in a line, darting) and a one-line field note ("calling loudly", "silent", "a flock of about 40"). The child:

1. Taps **Use the key**. The key asks two-way questions about features, one per screen, with pictures for each answer and **Go back** at every step (pattern from spec 116). **I can't tell** explains why that feature is hard to see and offers Go back or **Not sure** (recorded as not sure; never marked wrong).
2. When the key reaches a name, the child taps **Add a mark** on that species' row of the counting board. Marks draw as tally marks; every fifth mark crosses the four before it.
3. A sighting can be a **flock**: the field note gives the flock size and the child adds that many with **+1**, **+5** and **+10** buttons (each press draws its marks). The Sprout sequence has single birds only. Big wood pigeon flocks are counted the way real counters estimate, in blocks of ten ("about 12 tens").
4. After the last sighting, **Make the table** turns the tallies into numbers; **Make the bar chart** asks the child to set each bar height (in steps of 1, against gridlines labeled every 5 in September or every 10 for October cranes and red kites; a bar may stop between gridlines). The 410 wood pigeons are too many for that chart; a note says so and keeps them in the table, which teaches that a chart's scale must fit its data. Axes, title and the scale are always labeled.

**Two made-up mornings** (fiction, labeled on every screen: *A made-up morning at a made-up pass. The birds are real; the numbers are invented.*):

| Morning | Species and invented totals | Total |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Early September | honey buzzard 36, black kite 24, white stork 15, barn swallow 45 | 120 |
| Late October | common crane 72, wood pigeon 410, red kite 8 (plus 5 griffon vultures circling that are local birds, not migrants, and are not counted) | 490 |
| Nuria's late October tally (Vadoalto side, fiction) | common crane 64, wood pigeon 380, red kite 9 | 453 |

The griffon vultures teach that counters record **passing migrants**, not every bird in the sky; most Pyrenean griffons stay all year, though some young birds move south (S15). The short morning (written for Sprout) is 12 single sightings from the September species, with its own totals in the companion; the full September morning is written for Seedling and the October morning for Sapling. Any explorer can open any morning. The sequence is authored and fixed, so totals are checkable.

**The key (`key-v1`)**: eight two-way questions about visible features (neck and legs stretched out; calling; slim swept-back wings; plump gray flock; forked tail; deep fork with pale wing windows; pigeon-like head; huge broad wings circling low), reaching seven counted species, the griffon vulture (local, not counted) and **Not sure**. Full wording is in the companion. Features follow S31 to S35; a field-guide reviewer should still confirm they are fair at a distance (the honey buzzard head is hard to judge far off, so I can't tell is always offered).

### (b) Map the journey

A **simplified map** (made-up picture, labeled as such): the Pyrenees with the High Notch marked, Spain, the Mediterranean, the Strait of Gibraltar, the Sahara, the Sahel and savanna band, the tropical forest band, and an inset arrow pointing on toward southern Africa. Rounded distances are printed on the map as **model numbers**: pass to the Strait 900 km; Strait to the Sahel 2,700 km; the open-sea shortcut across the Mediterranean 600 km. Line: *A simplified picture. Real routes bend with wind, weather and food, and real distances vary.*

The child picks a **stork** or a **swallow**, then taps waypoints to draw a route. When the stork route crosses open sea for more than the Strait, the bench shows a thermal fading over water and asks *Can a soaring bird find rising air out here?* The route stays editable; nothing is refused. The swallow can cross the sea, and the bench says so when it does. A **Compare** card sets the two routes side by side with their model distances. Sapling adds a third bird (honey buzzard to the forest band) and a tern inset (Arctic to Antarctic on a whole-Earth sketch).

### (c) Why go? cards

Eight reason cards; the child sorts each into **A real reason** or **Not the main reason**, and must tap a **because** chip for each card placed (from three supplied stems). Real reasons: food runs out in winter; long summer days for feeding chicks; returning north to breed; insects to eat in Africa in our winter. Not the main reason: "they just go to get warm"; "they are bored"; "the leader decides"; "they want a holiday". Each card has feedback in the companion. After sorting, a short card names the cold-only idea and why it is not enough (many birds cope with frost if they can find food).

### (d) Habitat postcards

Five postcards (polar summer, temperate valley, desert, savanna, rainforest), each with a picture and one short card. The child matches a bird's line ("I came for insects and the Sun that never sets", "I need shade by day and fly by night") to the right postcard, then reads the card. Sapling adds two contrast postcards (Arctic and Antarctic) and asks which has penguins.
