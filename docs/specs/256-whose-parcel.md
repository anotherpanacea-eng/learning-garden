A child learns: a contraction is two words with letters left out, read by saying it long and spelled with the apostrophe where the letters were (not where the words meet); the apostrophe can change a word (ill, I'll); 's shows that something belongs to one owner; a plural owner ending in s takes the apostrophe after the s, and a plural without s takes 's (children's, mice's); an s that only means more than one takes no mark, as the label on the parcel for the Keeper of the Ways shows; and, at Sapling and Keeper's Desk, it's against its, who's against whose, and 's that means is.

## The knowledge

Sourced in the [audit](../source-audits/256-whose-parcel.md). Child text uses American terms; the grown-up panel names England's.

**Contractions.** In a contraction the apostrophe shows where a letter or letters would be if the words were written in full (DfE English Appendix 1, Year 2: *can't*, *didn't*, *hasn't*, *couldn't*, *it's*, *I'll*). England's Year 1 word reading asks children to read contractions such as *I'm*, *I'll*, *we'll* and understand that the apostrophe represents the omitted letters; Year 2 spells them. The Common Core asks for "an apostrophe to form contractions and frequently occurring possessives" in grade 2 (L.2.2c). *Can't* contracts the single word *cannot*; *won't* means *will not* but does not keep its letters, so both are taught as exceptions. Several contractions spell a different word when the mark is dropped (*ill*, *well*, *shell*, *were*). The common error places the mark at the join (*did'nt*). French also marks a dropped vowel with an apostrophe (*je* + *arrive* > *j'arrive*), which scene 4 shows.

**Possession, one owner.** England's Year 2 adds the apostrophe for singular possession (*the girl's name*; spelling examples *Megan's*, *Ravi's*, *the girl's*, *the child's*, *the man's*). The DfE glossary notes that the relation a possessive expresses "goes well beyond ordinary ideas of possession", so child text says "belongs to or goes with". Common Core grade 1 uses possessive nouns (L.1.1b); grade 3 asks children to "form and use possessives" (L.3.2d). *Of* can express the same relation (*the Keeper of the Ways*).

**Possession, many owners.** England's Years 3-4 spelling guidance: the apostrophe goes after the plural form; no s is added if the plural ends in s (*girls'*, *boys'*, *babies'*), and 's is added if it does not (*children's*, *men's*, *mice's*). Year 4 grammar: apostrophes to mark plural possession (*the girl's name*, *the girls' names*). A noun whose plural matches its singular (*sheep*) gives *sheep's* for one or many, so the written word alone cannot tell; C5 tests that.

**Plural or possessive.** Year 4 grammar names "the grammatical difference between plural and possessive -s". In speech *hens*, *hen's* and *hens'* sound the same, so only meaning tells the writer which to spell; Bryant and colleagues (1997) studied children's apostrophe spelling as a matter of grammatical understanding. A plain plural takes no apostrophe; an apostrophe in a plain plural on a shop sign is a well-known error, sometimes called the greengrocer's apostrophe (grown-up panel only). The parcel's label has one s-word, *Ways*, a plain plural: "not every s needs a mark."

**It's and its, who's and whose.** *It's* means *it is* or *it has*; DfE Year 2 adds that *it's* "is never used for the possessive". *Its*, like *his* and *hers*, takes no apostrophe (Merriam-Webster). *Who's* is *who is* or *who has*; *whose* means belonging to whom (DfE Years 5-6 homophone list). The "say it long" test separates each pair, and also separates 's meaning *is* (*Dot's here*) from possessive 's (*Dot's hat*), which is the Marble target's "distinguishing the two uses". The *has* reading (*It's been raining*) is named on card 11 and kept out of items.

**Names ending in s.** Style guides differ: the *Chicago Manual of Style* adds 's (*James's*), the AP Stylebook adds only the apostrophe to proper names ending in s (*James'*), and England's spelling note uses 's (*Cyprus's*). One Keeper's Desk card shows both; no item keys either, and every keyed singular owner ends in a letter other than s.

## The game or activity

Every screen: *The apostrophe is a small mark with a big job. Try any tile. Nothing here is scored.* No score, timer, lock or mastery label. Drags have tap equivalents; tiles are at least 44 px; every word and label has read-aloud.

- **(a) Long and Short (Sprout, Seedling).** **Fold it up:** eight word pairs; tapping the letters that drop out folds the strip and drops the apostrophe in their place; *won't* is shown after them as the one that doesn't fold. **Dot's door notes** (Sprout): tap a short word to open it into its long words. **Ill or I'll?:** Pip reads four markless notes aloud as written; the child places the mark and Pip rereads.
- **(b) Whose Parcel? (Seedling, Sapling).** Eight parcels, each with a picture of who it is for (one hen, five hens, the children, Hob's flock). The child builds the label from the owner word in both forms, an apostrophe tile and an s tile. The sheep parcel accepts *the sheep's gate* and explains that it fits one or many.
- **(c) The Label Bench (Seedling, Sapling).** Ten labels onto three shelves: **Just more than one**, **Belongs to one**, **Belongs to many**, with per-card feedback. **Mend the sign:** four market signs, three wrong and one right, to leave or fix. A read-only card ends the shelf: the label on the parcel for the Keeper of the Ways, and why it needs no mark.
- **(d) Say It Long (Sapling, Keeper's Desk).** **Short or belonging?** (six lines), **It's or its?** (four gaps), **Who's or whose?** (two gaps), and at Keeper's Desk **Dot's busy note** (E5: find and fix three marks).
