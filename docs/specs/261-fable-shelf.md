## The knowledge

Sourced in the [audit](../source-audits/261-fable-shelf.md); unconfirmed claims were cut or kept out of child text.

**Fable, moral, message.** A fable is a short story, usually with animals acting like people, that conveys a useful truth (Merriam-Webster: "a narration intended to enforce a useful truth", especially with animals acting as people); the moral is that truth stated, traditionally at the end. A message can be shown without being stated, as in the Cherokee terrapin story, which ends with no moral line. Common Core RL.2.2 asks children to determine the central message, lesson or moral, and RL.3.2 adds explaining how key details convey it; RL.4.2 and RL.5.2 ask for theme and summary.

**A moral is a teller's choice.** The goat-in-the-well fable ends "Look before you leap" in Townsend's English Aesop, "Never trust the advice of a man in difficulties" in Jacobs (1894), and "En toute chose il faut considérer la fin" in La Fontaine (III, 5, 1668). The three tellings share a pattern, not identical events. In Townsend a fox stuck in a well praises the water until a thirsty goat jumps down; in Jacobs the stuck fox claims a great drought is coming; in La Fontaine fox and goat travel together and both go down into the well to drink, and only then does the fox propose the way out. Every time the fox climbs out on the goat's back and horns and leaves the goat below (in Townsend and La Fontaine after promising to help it out). One pattern, different details, three morals. Opposite morals in two fables: the goat leaps too fast (look first); the fox in "The Cat and the Fox" deliberates too long while the cat acts (Jacobs: "Better one safe way than a hundred on which you cannot reckon"; compare the proverb "He who hesitates is lost").

**Theme and topic.** A topic is what a text is about (a race); a theme is a broader idea it develops (boasting can make you careless). Recurring theme here: pride or boasting before a fall (the hare twice, the fox with a hundred tricks, Léon's tortoise). Conventions: talking animals, a stated moral at the end, a boaster beaten by a smaller rival, a trick.

**Same pattern, different place.** The race of a slow animal against a fast one is folktale type ATU 275 and relatives. In Aesop the tortoise wins by steadiness while the hare naps. In Grimm's "The Hare and the Hedgehog" (KHM 187) the hedgehog's wife, who looks just like him, waits at the far end; Grimm states the moral that nobody should mock someone lesser. In the Cherokee "How the Terrapin Beat the Rabbit" (told by John Ax and Suyeta, printed by Mooney, 1900) the boastful terrapin, "a great warrior", asks his lookalike friends to wait on the ridges of a four-ridge race to stop the rabbit's boasting; the tale states no moral and ends by explaining why a tired rabbit cries "mi, mi, mi". Mooney notes a Deer variant and Creek, African American and European parallels. It is presented as a Cherokee story, not as an Aesop-style fable, and no item asks for its moral. The talkative tortoise, by contrast, traveled by translation: in the Sanskrit *Panchatantra* (date uncertain, often placed around 300 CE), translated into Middle Persian by Borzuy in the 500s and into Arabic by Ibn al-Muqaffa' about 750; the Castilian *Calila e Dimna* was translated from the Arabic in 1251 for the future Alfonso X (some sources say 1252), where the tale of the turtle and two ducks is a nested story told by a female bird; La Fontaine retold it as "La Tortue et les deux Canards" (X, 2, printed 1679 with Books IX-XI). His road did not run through the Spanish: the second collection credits Pilpay, and its usual source is Gaulmin's *Livre des lumières* (1644), a French translation of a later Persian telling. So the companion shows the journey as one line from India to the Arabic, then two roads, and D4 orders the tellings by date. Each telling stresses a different message: in the frame, heeding good advice; La Fontaine's moral opens "Imprudence, babil, et sotte vanité" (rashness, chatter and foolish vanity are close kin).

**Chapter, scene, stanza.** CCSS RL.3.5 asks children to refer to parts of stories, dramas and poems using chapter, scene and stanza, and to describe how each part builds on earlier sections; RL.5.5 asks how a series of them fits together. Working definitions: a chapter is a main division of a book; a scene, a division of a play or show, usually one place and time; a stanza, a group of lines set off as a unit of a poem. "How Doth the Little Crocodile" has two quatrains in chapter II of *Alice's Adventures in Wonderland*; the first shows the crocodile tending its shining tail and pouring Nile water over its golden scales; the second turns to its grin, its neatly spread claws and the "gently smiling jaws" that welcome little fishes in, so the shining creature of stanza 1 turns out to be a hunter. It parodies Watts's 1715 four-stanza poem, whose first stanza praises the busy bee.

**Summary.** An objective summary gives the key events in order, briefly, without personal opinions or minor details (CCSS RL.5.2; RL.6.2 adds that the summary is distinct from personal opinions or judgments; the English national curriculum's Years 5-6 reading asks for summarising main ideas, and Years 3-4 for identifying themes and conventions).

## The lesson

Jane's **teach -> guided -> practice**: nine teach cards, one idea each with a worked example revealed a step per tap; each set's first item shows 1-3 visible steps. **"I'm stuck" ladder** on every item: hint -> an easier worked case plus how to start this one (the mouse and the cheese; one story where rushing fails and one where waiting fails; a song's verses; two smallest-wins stories; one-sentence dog summary) -> skip for now, no penalty.

**Contrasts:** what happens vs what it shows; message vs detail; topic vs theme; one moral vs opposite morals; one pattern, three tellings with different details and morals; trick vs steadiness; a story that traveled vs similar stories in separate places; chapter vs scene vs stanza; a later part that carries on vs one that turns; summary vs opinion vs small detail.

**Misconceptions tagged on choices:** `moral-is-summary`, `moral-is-topic`, `detail-not-message`, `moral-always-stated`, `one-true-moral`, `opposites-cant-both`, `moral-is-rule`, `pattern-means-copy`, `same-pattern-same-message`, `different-place-different-story`, `one-place-only`, `only-one-cause`, `stanza-is-line`, `stanza-is-scene`, `scene-is-chapter`, `later-part-unconnected`, `theme-is-topic`, `opinion-in-summary`, `every-detail-in-summary`.

## The game or activity

Every fable card shows **A story people told** and its source line; read-aloud on every line; scene tickets reuse the Story Loft stage art read-only. No score, timer, lock or mastery label. Drags have tap equivalents.

- **(a) What Is the Message?** Read a fable as three scene tickets; sort three cards (*what happens*, *the message*, *what it's about*); tap two moments that show the message.
- **(b) Two Morals.** Match three morals to three fables; Bramble's question ("they can't both be right"); at Sapling, **One story, three tellers** (three tellings of the goat story: one pattern, different details and morals).
- **(c) Chapter, Scene, Stanza.** Sort five parts; count the crocodile poem's stanzas; **Builds on**: stanza 1 then stanza 2.
- **(d) Same Pattern, Different Place.** A pattern table for three races (who is fast, who is slow, who boasts, how the slow one wins, is there a moral); at Keeper's Desk, the tortoise's journey map and the two tellings side by side.
- **(e) Say It Plain.** Sort main-event, small-detail and opinion cards; build Nuria's tale in three cards; opinion cards go in Bramble's envelope, allowed but kept apart.

**Cherokee fallback:** the terrapin story ships only after a Cherokee cultural reviewer clears the retelling. Without it, card 6, D1 and D2 use the two races only (wording in the companion's review note), and the pattern table has two rows.

## Saved work

Optional root `fables` per explorer. An explicit Start freezes only the selected visit's complete working source; Keep freezes that source if no visit has started. New visits retain earlier visits. No snapshot of every catalog item is required.

The sketch uses `Saved<T>` only as shorthand for an inline first/latest record, not a shared runtime helper or a source registry. `FullSource` is the complete selected authored source: passages and provenance labels; instructions, teaching and examples; models/data and captions; every selected question, ordered choice text, key and feedback; help and read-back text or asset references. It contains authored prose; only the response payload `T` uses codes.

```

Snapshot<T> = {
  source: FullSource,
  shown: { question: text, choices: [{ id, text }], key, feedback },
  answer: T | null,

  helpBefore?: ObservedHelp,
  at

Saved<T> = { first: Snapshot<T>, latest?: Snapshot<T> }

fables: {
  v: 1,
  visits: { [visitId]: {
```

**Frozen source and first response.** Every `Snapshot` above embeds its own full source, shown question/instruction, ordered choices, key, feedback, response and original outcome. For a bench with no supplied choices or key, preserve its instruction and use an empty choice list and null key; an ungraded keep uses `kept`. The working source is frozen before the visit begins and is never replaced by today's catalog. Waiting work and saved responses render only from their own sources. `first` is write-once at the first recorded response/keep; later attempts have a separate `latest`, and later skips/help never change `first`. Explicitly starting revised content creates another visit and retains earlier sources and work. `answer: null` is allowed only when no selection exists and the outcome is `skipped` or `shown`; otherwise retain the actual response. No original spoken or written explanation is graded.

**Observed help before reveal.** During a newly tracked visit, `observed: true` with empty arrays means no help request was recorded in that visit so far, not demonstrated independent performance. Commit concept-help codes (hint, guided step, example, shown answer, meaning or slot scaffold), with the affected task/item id, to working help before their first reveal. Commit read-aloud/prompt-audio codes and the affected task/item id separately to `reading` before playback. Copy the observation into each response's `helpBefore` at the response time. A later request changes working help and the next response only. Missing older observations remain unobserved: never infer them from a later response or turn them into an empty list, false or “none.” Help requests are not proof that help was used; reading/audio data establish neither independent reading nor independent listening.

**Validation and preservation.** Validate structural bounds, ids, counts, outcome codes and consistency with the save's own source/choices; never require today's catalog membership, bench sizes, wording or answer key. A missing item is absent. Never recompute a saved result. Unknown versions and unreadable entries remain exact and exportable; only the affected visit/bench becomes read-only, and one bad item never closes the pack. Response payloads contain no free text; say why is spoken and unstored. No field holds a real learner's identity, age, grade or coursework. The `loft` root (specs 36, 37) is untouched; spoken E6 is ungraded and unstored.

**Atomic saves and capacity.** Every mutation, including source copies and help-before records, must pass the actual host's pretty-notes serialization/import preflight and shared limits before committing only this root. Refusal preserves the whole earlier state and visible pending work; do not rerender, navigate or change explorer in a way that silently loses unsaved words/selections. Offer the existing notes download and an explicit confirmed restore of saved words where needed. No silent truncation, history eviction or replacement of saved writing. Export, import, reset and save-off use the shared store; preview/render/suggestion reads are save-neutral. Existing source/language/cultural/rights checks remain open until separately cleared.
