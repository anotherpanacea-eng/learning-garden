A child learns: a fable shows a message through its events, and details can be pointed to; a moral is the message put into words, and two fables can point opposite ways; one story can carry different morals from different tellers; a theme is a big idea, a topic is a thing; far-apart traditions share story shapes, sometimes because a story traveled (the talkative tortoise, from the *Panchatantra* to *Calila e Dimna* and La Fontaine) and sometimes not (three races); books have chapters, shows scenes, poems stanzas, and later parts build on earlier ones; a summary keeps main events and drops opinions and small details.

## The knowledge

Sourced in the [audit](../source-audits/261-fable-shelf.md); unconfirmed claims were cut or kept out of child text.

**Fable, moral, message.** A fable is a short story, usually with animals acting like people, that conveys a useful truth (Merriam-Webster: "a narration intended to enforce a useful truth", especially with animals acting as people); the moral is that truth stated, traditionally at the end. A message can be shown without being stated, as in the Cherokee terrapin story, which ends with no moral line. Common Core RL.2.2 and RL.3.2 ask children to determine the central message, lesson or moral and explain how key details convey it; RL.4.2 and RL.5.2 ask for theme and summary.

**A moral is a teller's choice.** The goat-in-the-well fable ends "Look before you leap" in Townsend's English Aesop, "Never trust the advice of a man in difficulties" in Jacobs (1894), and "En toute chose il faut considérer la fin" in La Fontaine (III, 5, 1668). Same events, three morals. Opposite morals in two fables: the goat leaps too fast (look first); the fox in "The Cat and the Fox" deliberates too long while the cat acts (Jacobs: "Better one safe way than a hundred on which you cannot reckon"; compare the proverb "He who hesitates is lost").

**Theme and topic.** A topic is what a text is about (a race); a theme is a broader idea it develops (boasting can make you careless). Recurring theme here: pride or boasting before a fall (the hare twice, the fox with a hundred tricks, Léon's tortoise). Conventions: talking animals, a stated moral at the end, a boaster beaten by a smaller rival, a trick.

**Same pattern, different place.** The race of a slow animal against a fast one is folktale type ATU 275 and relatives. In Aesop the tortoise wins by steadiness while the hare naps. In Grimm's "The Hare and the Hedgehog" (KHM 187) the hedgehog's wife, who looks just like him, waits at the far end; Grimm states the moral that nobody should mock someone lesser. In the Cherokee "How the Terrapin Beat the Rabbit" (told by John Ax and Suyeta, printed by Mooney, 1900) lookalike friends wait on each of four ridges, and the tale also explains why a tired rabbit cries "mi, mi, mi"; Mooney notes Creek and European parallels. The talkative tortoise, by contrast, traveled by translation: in the Sanskrit *Panchatantra* (date uncertain, often placed around 300 CE), translated into Middle Persian by Borzuy in the 500s and into Arabic by Ibn al-Muqaffa' about 750; the Castilian *Calila e Dimna* was translated from the Arabic in 1251 for the future Alfonso X (some sources say 1252), where the tale of the turtle and two ducks is a nested story told by a female bird; La Fontaine retold it as "La Tortue et les deux Canards" (Book X; the second collection credits Pilpay). Each telling stresses a different message: in the frame, heeding advice; in La Fontaine, "Imprudence, babil, et sotte vanité."

**Chapter, scene, stanza.** CCSS RL.3.5 asks children to refer to parts of stories, dramas and poems using chapter, scene and stanza, and to describe how each part builds on earlier sections; RL.5.5 asks how a series of them fits together. Working definitions: a chapter is a main division of a book; a scene, a division of a play or show, usually one place and time; a stanza, a group of lines set off as a unit of a poem. "How Doth the Little Crocodile" has two quatrains in chapter II of *Alice's Adventures in Wonderland*; the first sets a cheerful mood, the second keeps the smile and turns it ("gently smiling jaws"). It parodies Watts's 1715 four-stanza poem, whose first stanza praises the busy bee.

**Summary.** An objective summary gives the key events in order, briefly, without personal opinions or minor details (CCSS RL.5.2; RL.6.2 adds that the summary is distinct from personal opinions or judgments; the English national curriculum's Years 5-6 reading asks for summarising main ideas, and Years 3-4 for identifying themes and conventions).

## The lesson

Jane's **teach -> guided -> practice**: nine teach cards, one idea each with a worked example revealed a step per tap; each set's first item shows 1-3 visible steps. **"I'm stuck" ladder** on every item: hint -> an easier worked case plus how to start this one (the mouse and the cheese; one story where rushing fails and one where waiting fails; a song's verses; two smallest-wins stories; one-sentence dog summary) -> skip for now, no penalty.

**Contrasts:** what happens vs what it shows; message vs detail; topic vs theme; one moral vs opposite morals; one story, three morals; trick vs steadiness; a story that traveled vs similar stories in separate places; chapter vs scene vs stanza; a later part that carries on vs one that turns; summary vs opinion vs small detail.

**Misconceptions tagged on choices:** `moral-is-summary`, `moral-is-topic`, `detail-not-message`, `moral-always-stated`, `one-true-moral`, `opposites-cant-both`, `moral-is-rule`, `pattern-means-copy`, `same-pattern-same-message`, `different-place-different-story`, `one-place-only`, `only-one-cause`, `stanza-is-line`, `stanza-is-scene`, `scene-is-chapter`, `later-part-unconnected`, `theme-is-topic`, `opinion-in-summary`, `every-detail-in-summary`.

## The game or activity

Every fable card shows **A story people told** and its source line; read-aloud on every line; scene tickets reuse the Story Loft stage art read-only. No score, timer, lock or mastery label. Drags have tap equivalents.

- **(a) What Is the Message?** Read a fable as three scene tickets; sort three cards (*what happens*, *the message*, *what it's about*); tap two moments that show the message.
- **(b) Two Morals.** Match three morals to three fables; Bramble's question ("they can't both be right"); at Sapling, **One story, three tellers** (the goat story's three morals).
- **(c) Chapter, Scene, Stanza.** Sort five parts; count the crocodile poem's stanzas; **Builds on**: stanza 1 then stanza 2.
- **(d) Same Pattern, Different Place.** A pattern table for three races (who is fast, who is slow, who boasts, how the slow one wins, is there a moral); at Keeper's Desk, the tortoise's journey map and the two tellings side by side.
- **(e) Say It Plain.** Sort main-event, small-detail and opinion cards; build Nuria's tale in three cards; opinion cards go in Bramble's envelope, allowed but kept apart.

**Cherokee fallback:** the terrapin story ships only after a Cherokee cultural reviewer clears the retelling. Without it, card 6, D1 and D2 use the two races only (wording in the companion's review note), and the pattern table has two rows.
