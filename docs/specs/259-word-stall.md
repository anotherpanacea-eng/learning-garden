**Ladder rule (binding):** a shades-of-meaning ladder uses only words whose order most speakers agree on. A child learns: words sort into kinds, and the sorting rule is a choice; opposites are the other end, some with steps between and some depending on which meaning is meant; a new word is something to ask about, look around and link to a word already known, then try where it fits; near-synonyms differ in how much, and some are too close to rank; a definition names a kind and a feature that sets the thing apart; synonyms are close, not the same, so a thesaurus is used meaning first and sentence second; and a vivid word shows the reader something exact and must still be true.

## The knowledge

Sourced in the [audit](../source-audits/259-word-stall.md).

**Kinds and rules.** Sorting objects (kindergarten) and then words (Grade 1) into categories is a CCSS standard (L.K.5a, L.1.5a); explaining why items belong is one of the topic's own evidence lines. The same items sort differently under a different rule (kind, color). A category depends on its rule: plant scientists class the tomato as a fruit, cooks use it as a vegetable, and in *Nix v. Hedden* (1893) the US Supreme Court treated it as a vegetable under a tariff law, by its ordinary meaning.

**Opposites.** The DfE glossary defines antonyms as words with opposite meanings and gives *light–dark* and *light–heavy*: one word, two meanings, two opposites. Some opposites have intermediate terms (hot, warm, cool, cold); some are two sides of one exchange (buy, sell). The pack names the first kind as "steps between" and the second as "two sides of one swap", without linguistic terms.

**New words.** Young readers ask and answer questions about unknown words and use context and pictures (CCSS L.K.4 to L.5.4a, RL.K.4); linking a new word to a known one ("enormous means really, really big") is one of the topic's own evidence lines. Teaching words with student-friendly explanations and many uses in context is the approach associated with Beck, McKeown and Kucan's *Bringing Words to Life* (2nd ed. 2013; characterization unchecked, see audit). Using words acquired from reading and conversation is CCSS L.K.6 to L.3.6.

**Shades of meaning.** CCSS asks children to tell apart verbs of the same action by manner (walk, march, strut, prance) and adjectives by intensity (L.K.5d, L.1.5d, L.2.5b). Semantic gradients, words placed along a line from least to most, are a classroom routine described by Greenwood and Flanigan (2007). Ladders here: warm, hot, scorching; damp, wet, soaked; whisper, talk, shout; walk, jog, sprint; drizzle, rain, downpour; big below huge, enormous and gigantic, which may share a rung in any order (learner's dictionaries define huge and enormous alike and list each as the other's synonym).

**Defining.** A formal definition names a superordinate category and a distinguishing attribute ("a duck is a bird that swims"; CCSS L.1.5b). Snow (1990) found formal definitions develop through the school years and track school language experience. A first feature often fits other members of the kind (a swan also swims), so a better definition adds one that sets the thing apart (a duck's short neck; Merriam-Webster and Britannica both list a short neck).

**Synonyms and the thesaurus.** The DfE glossary defines synonyms as words with the same or similar meanings (*talk–speak*, *old–elderly*). Exact synonyms are rare: near-synonyms differ in degree, manner, connotation or formality, which is why CCSS L.4.5c describes synonyms as similar but not identical. A thesaurus groups words by meaning; Peter Mark Roget's *Thesaurus of English Words and Phrases* (London, 1852) grouped them by idea, not alphabet. *Thesaurus* comes, through Latin, from Greek *thēsauros*, treasure or storehouse. The English national curriculum asks Years 5 and 6 to use a thesaurus, and Year 6 to learn how words relate as synonyms and antonyms. The stall's *run* entry is newly written for this pack and is labeled a made-up model.

**The vivid word.** Choosing words and phrases to convey ideas precisely (CCSS L.4.3a) and for effect (English national curriculum, UKS2 composition) means asking what the reader should see or feel, then checking the word is still accurate.

## The lesson

Jane's **teach -> guided -> practice**: thirteen teach cards, one idea each with a worked example revealed a step per tap; each set's first item shows 1-3 visible steps. **"I'm stuck" ladder** on every item: hint -> an easier worked case plus how to start this one (a cherry; up and down; a sign saying *pears* over pears; a puddle and a lake; "a cat is an animal that purrs"; nibble and gobble) -> skip for now, no penalty.

**Contrasts:** kind vs color as a sorting rule; a different word vs an opposite; steps between vs two sides of one swap; a light room vs a light box; guessing vs asking; a word that fits vs a word stuck anywhere; a stronger word vs the matching one; a ranked pair vs a too-close pair; a description vs a definition; a feature every member shares vs one that sets it apart; close vs the same; the first word in a list vs the word that fits; vivid vs plain; vivid and true vs vivid and false.

**Misconceptions tagged on choices:** `kind-mixed-up`, `sort-by-letters`, `one-rule-only`, `rule-ignored`, `opposite-is-any-different`, `past-the-ends`, `opposite-ignores-meaning`, `synonym-for-opposite`, `ignore-the-clues`, `guess-without-clues`, `skip-new-words`, `new-word-anywhere`, `shade-order-reversed`, `shade-wrong-scale`, `one-right-order`, `stronger-is-better`, `kind-missing`, `example-not-definition`, `feature-not-special`, `synonyms-exact`, `wrong-meaning`, `first-listed-word`, `vivid-wrong-effect`, `vivid-not-true`, `plain-word`.

## The game or activity

Every bench shows: *Words can fit more than one way. Tap* Why? *to see the reason.* No score, timer, lock or mastery label. Drags have tap equivalents. Words are not bought: the stall's rule is take one, leave one.

- **(a) The Crates.** Twelve word cards into four crates (fruit, bread, tools, clothes), with a "why together?" line per crate. **Odd one out** (three sets) asks for the card and then the reason. **Two rules** (Sapling): nine of the cards re-sorted by color into crates the child labels; the tomato note.
- **(b) The Opposites Hooks.** Eight pairs to hang. **Steps between**: hot to cold, full to empty, early to late. **Two sides of one swap**: buy and sell. **Which meaning?**: light (heavy, dark) and right (wrong, left). At Act III, ABIERTO and CERRADO hang beside OPEN and CLOSED.
- **(c) The Shades Ladder.** Five three-rung ladders, the size ladder (big below a shared top rung) and, at Sapling, one four-rung ladder (cool to scorching). **Side by side**: huge, enormous and gigantic share the top rung above big, in any order. **Which walk?**: tiptoes, marches, stomps, strolls, each chosen by its sentence.
- **(d) The Label Table.** Three new words (*damsons*, *skeins*, *colossal*) with an ask card first: what does it mean, look at the picture, read around it, link it to a known word. **Try it out** taps the thing that fits. **Build a label** from a kind tile and a feature tile. **So is a...?** (Sapling): a swan, an apple, a trowel also fit the first label, so the child adds a feature.
- **(e) The Sign Board (Sapling).** Three plain signs, each with the effect wanted (look fresh-baked, sound crunchy, feel quiet) and a three-word bank. The child says the sentence aloud with each word before choosing. **True as well as vivid**: Bramble's GIGANTIC BUNS.
- **(f) The Stall Thesaurus (Keeper's Desk).** The stall's own entry for *run* (three meanings, four to six words each). **Pick the meaning** for three sentences, then **Pick the word** for three more. **Close, not the same** cards (jog and sprint, trickle and pour, whisper and announce), the *thesaurus* word history and Roget's 1852 book.
