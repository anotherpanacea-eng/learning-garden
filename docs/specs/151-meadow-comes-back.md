No built encounter covers living/dead/never alive, food webs, matter cycling, energy loss or changing environments in Marble's Ecosystems & Habitats domain. Spec 143 brings an ocean web; this pack brings the land one, and the game's first lesson on **change over time in a habitat**.

A child comes to understand that living, dead and never alive are told apart by growing and feeding, not moving; that a food web is chains sharing living things, and removing one kind may change others up and down; that decomposers return dead matter to the soil, so dead is not empty; that energy enters as sunlight and leaves as heat, with roughly a tenth passing up each step (older depth); that a flooded meadow comes back in a sequence (pioneers, then grasses and flowers) and would turn to scrub without hay cutting; and that communities can help.

## The knowledge

**Living, dead, never alive.** Living things grow, feed, respire, respond, reproduce. Dead things were once alive (rotten branch, feather, wooden post, empty shell); never-alive things never were (stone, water, glass). Movement is not the test. Mushrooms are fungi: living, and not plants (no chlorophyll; they digest food outside the body and absorb it).

**The fallen willow.** Willows resprout strongly after being cut or broken (coppicing does not kill them) and their branches root very readily in damp soil, so a fallen willow can stay alive and sprout while its broken end rots: one object, both "living" and "dead". Summer-shed willow seed stays viable only days to a few weeks and needs moist bare ground at once; floodplain studies find willow seedlings in bands where the ground was wet and open when seed fell.

**The meadow food web** (simplified; real diets are wider). Producers: grass. Links used: grass → field vole (grass is the field vole's main food, Mammal Society); grass → meadow grasshopper (grazes grasses, The Wildlife Trusts); field vole → barn owl (about 45% of British barn owl diet, Barn Owl Trust); field vole → kestrel (voles dominate in northern Europe); meadow grasshopper → kestrel (grasshoppers are the bulk of nestling diet in eastern Spain); meadow grasshopper → common frog (Orthoptera recorded in alpine common frog diets, a minor item among many insects); common frog → grass snake (frogs are principal prey in Britain); dead leaves → woodlouse (detritivore, RHS); woodlouse → common frog (frog diets include woodlice). Character species are kept out of the web on purpose: no fox (Pip), heron (Hollis), otter (Nuria), kingfisher (Léon) or ladybug (Dot), so no friend appears as food or hunter.

**Arrow convention, shared with specs 66, 88 and 143.** Food to eater, with spec 66's legend ("Arrow: food → eater. Food supplies energy to the eater."), read aloud as "is eaten by" as in spec 143. Decomposer links use the same arrow; the matter loop uses a visibly different round arrow with its own legend. Removal outcomes always say "may".

**Decomposers and matter cycling.** Fungi digest dead plant material, including wood (some can break down lignin, the toughest part), returning carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus to soil and air. Woodlice break up fresh plant debris so smaller organisms and bacteria can continue. Earthworms pull leaves into their burrows (Darwin). Dead wood feeds fungi and beetle grubs and shelters woodlice; conservation guidance is to leave fallen wood where it falls when safe. Matter loops; energy flows one way and leaves as heat. The loop the child builds: leaf → woodlouse → fungi and bacteria → soil → grass → vole → owl → pellet rots → soil.

**Energy between levels (older depth).** The classroom "10 percent rule", usually credited to Lindeman (1942), says roughly a tenth of the energy at one level reaches the next. It is a rule of thumb: real figures run from under 1 percent to around 40 percent. The rest leaves mostly as heat from respiration and movement, or in waste and uneaten parts (transferred, not destroyed), which is why pyramids are wide at the base and chains are short.

**Changing environments and succession.** Secondary succession is recolonization after a disturbance such as a flood that leaves soil, roots and seeds behind (Britannica); primary succession starts where there was no life. Pioneers are fast, often weedy colonists, later crowded out. Flood silt replenishes the nutrients a hay crop removes (Floodplain Meadows Partnership), though extreme floods can waterlog soil or oversupply nutrients. A hay meadow is held at the grassland stage by cutting or grazing: with low grazing, floodplain grassland near seed sources is colonized by willow, then alder and ash (Knepp report). Pyrenean studies agree in general: where meadows and pastures are abandoned, shrubs and forest replace them, and moderate grazing limits encroachment.

**Human impact and communities.** Natural change (the flood) and human-caused change (the straightened brook in the story; an invasive plant in real life) are both taught, without "all change is bad". Himalayan balsam is a real invasive on European riverbanks, long recorded along French Pyrenean rivers and reported new to the upper Segre and Llobregat basins of the Catalan Pyrenees in a 2021 floristic paper: pods burst, rivers carry seeds downstream, it dies back in winter and leaves bare banks to erode; volunteers hand-pull it before seeding, often starting upstream. Green hay transfer moves seed from a species-rich donor meadow to a bare site; not every species transfers, and further seeding may be needed. Leaving dead wood is the third example, giving the three contrasting community actions Marble's research asks for.

**Biodiversity and resilience (older depth).** Tilman and Downing (1994) found that in long-studied Minnesota grassland plots, productivity in more species-rich plots resisted a severe drought better and recovered more fully; a published comment questioned causation, and later experiments followed. The pack says "more likely", never "certain".

## The game or activity

**Survey (`Survey`).** A top-down picture of the 10 × 10 survey square for each of five visits (tap a visit tab), plus the lawn square beside it. Each visit shows bare small squares shaded, a "kinds of plant" count and a "kinds of small animal" count, from the authored table below. The child taps a visit, then answers read-the-survey prompts. **A made-up model** label and the model line on every view.

| Visit | Bare small squares (of 100) | Kinds of plant | Kinds of small animal | Lawn kinds |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | 85 | 2 | 1 | 4 |
| 2 | 40 | 6 | 4 | 4 |
| 3 | 15 | 9 | 6 | 4 |
| 4 | 5 | 14 | 9 | 4 |
| 5 | 2 | 18 | 12 | 4 |

Model: one 1 m square of 100 small 10 cm squares; "kinds" are kinds the explorer can tell apart, not identified species; same place and method each visit. The numbers are invented to show a plausible shape; one square is a small sample.

**Sort (`Sort`).** Ten cards, three trays (Living, Dead (once alive), Never alive). Tap a card, then a tray. Each card has **Look closer**. Any placement is accepted; an unexpected one gets a second-look prompt tied to its misconception, never "wrong".

**Web (`Web`).** Nine cards on a board. Tap food, then eater, to draw an arrow; the bench reads it back ("This says the grass is eaten by the field vole. Is that right?"). Only the nine authored links become solid, with their evidence line; other proposals stay dashed and editable (spec 88's pattern). Steps grow the web from chain one to the decomposer link. **What if?** grays a tapped card and lights every arrow touching it, up and down; a short prompt asks what may happen along each lit arrow. Accessible list view of all arrows, as spec 88 requires.

**Loop (`Loop`).** Eight loop cards placed on a ring (tap a card, tap a ring slot; drag optional). When the ring closes, the "back to the soil" line shows. A **What if nothing rotted?** button shows a pile-up picture. Beside it, at older depth, an energy strip: sunlight in, wavy warmth arrows out of every stage.

**Energy (`Energy`, older).** A four-level pyramid with made-up units, 10,000 → 1,000 → 100 → 10. Tap an arrow for "where the rest went". A slider offers 5, 10 or 20 percent only; levels recompute as `level[n] = level[0] × rate^n`, rounded to whole units. Model: one straight chain, one fixed rate for every step, energy in arbitrary units; real transfers vary by level and organism. Label: **A made-up model**.
