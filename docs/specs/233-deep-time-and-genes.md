## The knowledge

Sourced in the audit. Facts that could not be confirmed stay out of child text.

**Layers (Sprout).** Sediment settles in layers; in an undisturbed stack the bottom settled first (spec 139's superposition, revisited; folding can flip the order, a Sapling note).

**Pompeii (Sprout to Seedling; loss at older depth).** In 79 CE Vesuvius erupted; ash and pumice buried the Roman town of Pompeii several meters deep. It was not a lava flow. The fast burial kept streets, walls, ovens and wall writing in place, which is why the town is evidence of daily Roman life. Digging began in 1748 and continues; part of the town is still unexcavated. Pliny the Younger, who watched from across the bay, later described the eruption cloud in two letters to Tacitus, comparing it to a pine tree. Many people escaped; some did not, one gentle Keeper's-note sentence. **Plaster casts and victims are not shown**, following specs 139 and 157 (owner default). The eruption's month has been debated (August or autumn; Renne and colleagues' 2026 argon study fits late August); the pack says only 79 CE.

**Branching trees (Sapling).** A cladogram groups kinds by **shared derived features**, features that first appeared in an ancestor and were passed to its branch. Relatedness is read by following two tips down to the **join they share first**; tips that sit side by side on the page may be distant (the trap layout puts the kingfisher beside Triceratops). Spinning a branch about its join changes the layout, not the relationships. Ancestors sit at the joins; no tip descends from another tip; no tip is "more advanced". A feature every tip shares (laying eggs) splits no one. **Hip groups, inline:** dinosaurs are traditionally split into the "lizard-hipped" saurischians (sauropods such as *Diplodocus*, and theropods such as *T. rex* and birds) and the "bird-hipped" ornithischians (*Stegosaurus*, *Triceratops*), marked also by the predentary, an extra beak bone at the jaw tip. Birds descend from the lizard-hipped side; the bird-like ornithischian hip is convergent. The pack's tree, (crocodile, ((Stegosaurus, Triceratops), (Diplodocus, (T. rex, kingfisher)))), uses five characters: eggs (all), a hole through the hip socket (dinosaurs), the predentary (ornithischians), air pockets in the backbone (saurischians; postcranial pneumaticity, not found in ornithischians, sourced in the audit), the wishbone (theropods including birds). Willi Hennig set out the method in 1950. A grown-up note says some researchers have proposed other arrangements of the main dinosaur groups.

**Radiometric dating (Sapling to Keeper's Desk).** Some atoms (radioactive isotopes) change into others. One half-life is how long it takes half of what remains to change; after n half-lives, (½)ⁿ remains, never cleanly zero. The half-life is essentially constant, which makes the clock reliable; the number changing each year falls as the parent runs down. Dating measures how much change has built up in the sample; it is not a stopwatch. A **rock clock** such as uranium-lead compares parent atoms left with daughter atoms made. **Carbon-14** works differently: labs compare the carbon-14 left with stable carbon, against the level in living things (the nitrogen it turns into is not counted), which gives the **share** of the original remaining (T10, the carbon readout, C2). Pick the clock that fits the age: **carbon-14** (half-life about 5,730 years; once-living material; up to about 50,000 years; calibrated with tree rings and other records) and **uranium-lead** in zircon crystals (uranium-238 half-life about 4.47 billion years; millions to billions of years). A fossil is usually dated by the ash beds that sandwich it. **Check against history:** in 1997, argon dating of sanidine crystals in the eruption's pumice (sampled at nearby Oplontis) gave about 1,925 ± 94 years, against 1,918 from the calendar (Renne and colleagues, *Science*); a 2026 study reports a much tighter result. Libby developed radiocarbon dating in the late 1940s (Nobel 1960); Patterson's 1956 meteorite lead gave Earth's age as about 4.55 billion years.

**Reconstructing a lost world (Keeper's Desk).** Each line of evidence answers its own question and has limits: **bone growth rings** (age, at least, and growth rate; early rings can be lost), **tooth chemistry** (oxygen for seasons and water; strontium against local rock for movement), **coprolites** (what was eaten), **fossil plants** (habitat and climate). Agreement between independent lines raises confidence; a clash is a clue (a diet shift, a season, reworking), not automatically a mistake. Real cases: a woolly mammoth's tusk sampled along its length showed wide roaming across Alaska over a life of about 28 years (Wooller and colleagues 2021); T. rex growth marks show growth speeding up and then slowing (Erickson and colleagues 2004 put full size near 20; Woodward, Myhrvold and Horner's 2026 *PeerJ* study of 17 specimens favors about 35-40, so child text gives no age); about 75-million-year-old duckbill coprolites from Utah's Kaiparowits Formation hold rotting conifer wood and crustacean shell (Chin and colleagues 2017). The ford duckbill and its readouts are made up.

**DNA and genes (Keeper's Desk).** DNA is a double helix whose rungs are base pairs, A always with T, C always with G, so one strand specifies the other. A gene is a stretch of DNA, present whether or not it is being read. The central dogma is a flow of **information**: DNA → mRNA (T read as U) → protein, where the ribosome joins **existing** amino acids in the order the codons give, from a start codon to a stop codon. Proteins do most of a cell's work. The pack's model gene (ATG GAA AAA TGG TAA) reads Met-Glu-Lys-Trp-stop by the standard code. **CRISPR** cuts DNA at a chosen spot; Francisco Mojica described the repeats in salt-pond microbes from 1993; Emmanuelle Charpentier and Jennifer Doudna made the editing tool in 2012 (Nobel 2020). The ethics board uses a plant case only: a CRISPR-edited, slow-browning mushroom that the US Department of Agriculture said in 2016 fell outside its usual GMO rules. **Partial by design:** somatic therapy, germline editing and "designer babies" are left to families (wave 5's rule on human reproduction and illness); the grown-up panel says so.

**Mutation (Keeper's Desk).** A mutation is a chance change in the order of DNA bases, caused by UV or ionizing radiation, some chemicals, or copying errors (kept separate from mutagens). Most have no effect that matters (a silent change such as GAA→GAG, or a change outside genes); some harm; a few help, and "helpful" depends on the surroundings. Changes arise by chance, whatever the need; selection sorts afterward. Only changes in the cells that make the next generation are passed on. Examples: the peppered moth's dark form came from a transposable-element insertion in the *cortex* gene, dated to about 1819 (van't Hof and colleagues 2016), linking to spec 161's selection story; nectarines are peaches carrying two copies of a recessive allele of one gene (*PpeMYB25*), which arose from a single mutation (a 2014 PLoS ONE study). Motoo Kimura's 1968 neutral theory is the history card.

**Inheritance (Keeper's Desk).** The ladder gene → allele → genotype → phenotype comes first. Each parent plant passes one allele per gene in a pollen grain or egg cell; the square's headers hold single alleles. Mendel's peas: yellow (Y) dominant over green (y); pea color belongs to each pea (the seed, the next generation), so the square's boxes are peas and E8 compares two peas; Yy × Yy gives 1 YY : 2 Yy : 1 yy, three yellow to one green. His real count was 6,022 yellow to 2,001 green (3.01 : 1), published 1866 and rediscovered in 1900. Ratios are chances over many offspring; each seed is an independent draw (four seedlings from Gg × Gg give exactly one smooth with probability 27/64 and none with 81/256). A hidden recessive allele can pass down quietly and show again (Hob's green peas, Nuria's smooth peaches).

**Extinction and rapid change (Keeper's Desk).** Natural selection (restated inline from spec 161's moth): heritable variation, more young for those who fit, change across generations. Extinction is a **race of rates**: when a place changes more quickly than a population can adapt, the population falls behind; individuals cannot adapt on demand. Short generations, high variation and broad diets help; specialists with slow breeding are vulnerable. Background extinction is steady and low; mass extinctions are rare pulses (spec 173's five) with recovery over millions of years. Historical case: the ice-age giants; contemporary case: the **Bramble Cay melomys**, a rodent of one low island in the Torres Strait, recognized as extinct by Australia in 2019 after storm surges and rising seas destroyed most of its food plants (a population elsewhere was not ruled out); a Keeper's Desk card tells it before F2. Today's losses are told calmly on one card: many scientists describe the present rate as faster than background, driven mostly by people's changes to land and climate, with people also working to protect living kinds.

**Megafauna and de-extinction (Keeper's Desk).** What ended the Pleistocene giants is still argued: hunting, climate, or both, with the balance varying by region. Australia is the most disputed case: most dating studies put the losses after people arrived, but how long the two overlapped, and the climate's part, are argued region by region (dates `[check]`, kept off the strip); North America's mammoths, camels and ground sloths disappeared about 13,000 to 11,000 years ago, when people were spreading and the climate swung cold (the Younger Dryas); woolly mammoths survived on Wrangel Island until about 4,000 years ago. Ancient DNA reveals relatedness and population change but breaks into short pieces; the oldest recovered is about 2 million years (Greenland sediment, 2022), so no DNA survives from non-bird dinosaurs (child text says "the big dinosaurs" and notes birds have their own). A "mammoth" could at best be an edited, cold-adapted elephant in a changed habitat. **Science question and ethics question are kept apart.** The bucardo (Pyrenean ibex) is the local case: the last one died in January 2000; a clone from her saved cells was born in 2003 and lived only minutes. Spec 166 names the bucardo on its Red List shelf; this card adds only the cloning attempt, at Keeper's Desk.

**(b) The Branching Tree, model `tree-v1`.** Six tips on the fixed tree above, drawn in the trap layout (crocodile, Diplodocus, T. rex, kingfisher, Triceratops, Stegosaurus). **Trace** two tips: the path lights to their join. **Spin this join**: tips swap sides; a line confirms the joins did not move. **Show features**: five chips light the branch each marks. **Add a fossil**: tick features on a new card (hip-socket hole and beak bone) and place it; only the ornithischian branch accepts it, any other placement asks "Which branch shares those features?". Model line: *A real tree uses hundreds of features and many more animals.*

**(c) The Halving Clock, model `halving-v1`.** 64 gold beads; each **One half-life** tap turns half of the gold to gray: 64, 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1, then the end line. A ratio bar shows gold to gray. At Keeper's Desk a carbon readout gives n × 5,730 years (0 to 34,380). A **clock shelf** sorts four things to two clocks (the dinosaur bone goes to "date the ash"). A **bracket bench** slides two ash ages; the fossil's window follows. Model line: *Real atoms change at random; big samples halve very evenly.*

**(d) The Case Board, model `duckbill-v1`.** Four trays, each with a "can tell" and a "can't tell" line, and fixed made-up readouts: 9 rib rings (wide then narrow); a tooth signal that swings three times, has a warm-rainy water signal, and moves from hill-rock to coast-rock values; six droppings, mostly conifer wood, two with shell; ferns, conifers and palms. Five claim cards each show *Lines that agree: n* (2 for the warm wet plain: plants and the tooth's water signal; 1 each for movement, age and rotting wood; 0 for color). A clash card. Three real cards (mammoth tusk, T. rex rings, Kaiparowits droppings). Model line: *The ford duckbill is made up; its clues copy real kinds.*

**(e) The Pea Bench.** **DNA ladder** with A/T/C/G tiles and a zoom strip (pea plant, leaf cell, nucleus, chromosome, DNA, gene: spec 175's zoom, with a plant). **Gene to protein** on the fifteen-letter model gene. **Change a letter**: six fixed changes give two same, two changed, two early stops (checked against the standard code); then a **twenty-change tally** (made up: 14 no effect, 5 trouble, 1 helps here). **Pea square, model `pea-v1`:** parents YY, Yy or yy (six pairings); headers take single alleles only; boxes read genotype and look. **Grow 4** shows a fixed batch (2 green, so not one in four); **Grow 400** a fixed batch (296 : 104); the Mendel card gives 6,022 : 2,001. **Snip a gene:** the mushroom card and a two-sides board of six chips, unjudged.

**(f) The Weighing Table.** **Rate race, model `race-v1`:** change 1, 2 or 3 steps a decade; adapting 1 (slow breeders) or 2 (fast breeders); gap = max(0, gap + change − adapting) each decade for ten decades; at 4 the population falls behind. Of six settings, three keep up; (2,1) and (3,2) fall behind in decade 4, (3,1) in decade 2. Then background and mass cards, the **melomys** card, a calm **today** card, the **which kinds keep up?** trio, **the giants' timeline** (three strips and a balance with no marked answer), the ancient-DNA card, the bucardo card, and the **can we / should we** board. Calm line on arrival. Model line: *Real living things can also move or hold on in corners.*
