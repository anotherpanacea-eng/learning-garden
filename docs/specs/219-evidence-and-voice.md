# 219 — Evidence & voice

## Intended outcome

A usable first English sequence at `#learn/english`: five open lessons around one complete original story, **The Clock Above the River**. Mature literary questions, optional read-aloud and concrete help are separate from reading-load choices. This is a first sequence, not a course-completion or reading-level claim. Paris and Story Loft remain untouched.

The text below is the canonical original text. Its four chapters remain available in a whole-story drawer in every lesson, including later chapters: staged reading is a suggestion, never an access lock. Every paragraph has a stable chapter/paragraph anchor. Each paragraph can be chosen for a free note marked literal, inference, or question. Notes retain the exact paragraph, anchor, and original words.

## Five lessons

1. **Before the bell** — read chapter 1; learn explicit statement versus inference. Annotate an exact paragraph. Check whether the narrator explicitly knows why Ivo puts away the brass key. Fitting choice is position 2: she reports the action but not the motive. Overclaim: he intends sabotage. Underclaim: she sees nothing that morning. Keep an optional first interpretation in original words.
2. **Two accounts** — read chapter 2; compare Mara's chapter-1 account with Ivo's account of the same encounter. Both remember Mara asking whether the clock would run; they differ in access to the key's purpose. Fitting choice position 1: Ivo reports a repair and his own thought, while Mara infers concealment from the key. Other responses: both describe identical motives; neither account shares any event. Annotate a second passage and keep a comparison in original words. Teach that first-person access to thought does not prove a narrator's claim true.
3. **What the later page changes** — read chapters 3–4; explicitly revisit chapter 1 paragraph 3 beside chapter 3 paragraphs 1–2. The repair ledger gives an earlier date for the defect, weakening Mara's first theory of deliberate damage without proving every part of Ivo's account. Check strongest support for the narrower claim that a fault was reported before opening day; fitting choice position 3 is the dated ledger, alternatives are the stopped hands and the crowd's silence. Check the remaining limit; fitting choice position 1: the ledger doesn't show who read it. Other choices: nothing about earlier reporting is known; it proves Ivo's complete innocence. Keep a changed-or-retained interpretation and reason, ungraded.
4. **Write for a reader** — a fictional editor asks: “How should the town describe the silence of the clock?” Teach claim / exact detail / reasoning using an unrelated library-window example. A planning check identifies reasoning, fitting position 2: the earlier date weakens a theory of damage during preparations. Alternatives merely repeat a quote or invent council motives. Provide optional separate claim/detail/reasoning fields, plus a fully original paragraph textarea; do not assemble supplied prose as the learner's own work. Keep the first draft snapshot exactly, including blank writing if chosen; ordinary edits remain a draft until explicitly kept.
5. **A fair objection, then a revision** — show the learner's exact first draft alongside the whole text. Teach a serious objection (“A repair note doesn't establish who knew about the fault”), and a response that narrows a claim rather than dismissing an opponent. Keep an original objection, revised reader-facing paragraph, and optional change note. First kept revision and latest kept reader-facing piece remain separate, with exact earlier versions accessible. Human review asks what claim the reader understood, which detail supported it, and whether the objection changed the wording. No prose score, keyword grader or automatic claim of completion.

Every lesson has original Learn, Guided walkthrough, Hint, Different example, Read aloud, Working together and Skip controls. Use of supplied teaching material is recorded as support. Structured checks record first response and later attempts, whether help preceded each response, and frozen feedback/outcome. Skipping records skipped rather than correct. Visits, text exposure, reported independent reading, supplied support and structured outcomes are separate. All lessons can be revisited directly.

## Complete original story: The Clock Above the River

### Chapter 1 — Mara's notebook

**c1-p1** On opening morning, the observatory clock showed seventeen minutes past six. It had shown that time when I arrived, carrying the paper cups, and it still showed it after I had swept the steps. Below us, the river was the color of an old spoon. People would climb the hill at noon to hear the restored clock strike. I had written that promise on thirty posters. At the bottom of each, in my smallest letters, I had put my name.

**c1-p2** Ivo was standing beneath the clock with a brass key in his palm. When he saw me looking, he closed his fingers and slipped it into his coat. “Will it run?” I asked. He looked toward the river instead of toward me. “The doors will open at noon,” he said. That was an answer to a different question. I set the cups down harder than I meant to. One rolled across the stone and came to rest against his shoe.

**c1-p3** Until that moment, I had thought the clock was merely broken. Now I thought someone might want it to stay broken. Ivo had opposed the opening date at the last meeting; I remembered how he had folded the schedule without reading it aloud. He picked up my cup and set it beside the others. “There are things a clock shouldn't be asked to promise,” he said. I wrote that sentence in my notebook. It sounded like something I would need later.

### Chapter 2 — Ivo's account

**c2-p1** Mara asked if the clock would run. I should have answered her. Instead, I told her when the doors would open, because that was the only part I could guarantee. The key in my hand belonged to the glass case around the mechanism. Its small teeth caught the lining of my coat as I put it away. I had just locked the case after taking out the cracked wheel. From the steps, she could not have seen the empty space inside.

**c2-p2** I had asked for another week at the meeting. The new wheel had arrived with one tooth bent, and the old one sometimes caught at seventeen minutes past six. The schedule was folded because I had carried it in my pocket through the rain. I remember Mara watching my hands. Perhaps she thought I was impatient with her posters. I was impatient with myself. I had seen the damaged replacement three days earlier and still hoped I could straighten it.

**c2-p3** That hope is the part I left out when I spoke to the committee. I said that the clock needed care. I did not say how much care, or that my best plan had failed. A clear warning would have made the meeting longer. I wanted to leave it. When Mara's cup touched my shoe, I thought of telling her everything. Instead, I straightened the cups. That is an easier thing to put right. I cannot tell you what she believed when she walked away.

### Chapter 3 — The book under the bench

**c3-p1** At eleven, Mara went inside to fetch the guest book. The observatory smelled of dust and orange peel. Someone had brought flowers in a jam jar, and a wet ring was spreading beneath it. Looking for a cloth, she pulled a shallow box from under the workbench. Inside lay a repair ledger. Most pages held numbers she could not follow, but one sentence, dated the previous Thursday, was plain: “Replacement wheel damaged on arrival; opening strike uncertain.” Beneath it was Ivo's narrow signature.

**c3-p2** She found Thursday on the folded schedule: it was the day before she had printed the posters. The fault had been reported in the ledger before her promise appeared around town. Yet there was no record of who had read that page. Beside the sentence, in a different pencil, someone had drawn a question mark. Mara ran her finger along it without touching the paper. A question mark could mean doubt, or a question waiting for an answer. It did not come with a name.

**c3-p3** Ivo found her holding the book. “You wrote it down,” she said. “Here,” he answered. Neither spoke for a while. Through the open door they could hear the first visitors on the path. Mara wanted to ask who was responsible. Instead, she asked why the ledger had stayed in a box. Ivo looked at the little flowers. “I knew where it was,” he said. “I thought that meant people could find it.” Mara closed her notebook without crossing out what she had written.

### Chapter 4 — Twelve without a bell

**c4-p1** At noon, Mara stood on the steps with the guest book. The crowd watched the clock. A bird landed on its minute hand, making a tiny movement that brought several people to their feet. Then the bird flew away. Ivo carried the cracked wheel outside on a cloth. He put it where people could see it and explained which part had failed. He did not mention the posters. Mara did not mention the key. For a moment, each omission seemed generous to the other.

**c4-p2** A visitor asked whether the clock would work next week. Ivo began, “It should—” and stopped. He asked Mara for a blank page. Together they wrote what had been tried, what still needed testing, and where the next notice would be posted. Mara pinned the page at eye level beside the open door. It was less beautiful than her posters. People stopped to read it longer. One visitor said she was glad they had opened the building anyway. Another said the promised bell had been the reason he came.

**c4-p3** That evening, Mara added a line beneath the sentence about promises in her notebook: “I heard a warning as a threat because I was afraid my own promise would fail.” She left the first sentence visible. Ivo asked if she would help write the new notice when the wheel was ready. “When it has been tested,” she said. The clock above them still showed seventeen minutes past six. Down at the river, the lamps were coming on one at a time. No one on the hill could hear their switches click.
