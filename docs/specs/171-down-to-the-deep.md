A child comes to understand that sea animals need food, shelter and the right water, as land animals do; that tiny animals build reefs full of variety; that the sea floor has plains, mountains, a ridge and trenches; that the dark, cold deep is full of life that makes its own light, some of it running on chemicals; that one change in an ecosystem ripples; that most harm to the sea starts on land and people help; and that people measured, mapped and went down, with most of the deep still unseen.

## The knowledge

Checked against search excerpts (NOAA, WHOI, US Navy, Columbia, NOC, MBARI, papers); review pass in the audit.

**Needs.** Ocean animals need food, shelter and the right water (oxygen in it, a temperature that suits them); fish take oxygen through gills. These are the same kinds of needs land animals have. Different parts of the sea suit different animals: a warm reef fish would not do well in the cold deep.

**Coral reefs.** Coral is an animal. Reefs are built by colonies of tiny polyps (mouth, tentacles), each building a hard limestone cup. Algae living inside many corals make food from sunlight and share it, so reef corals need clear, sunny, shallow water; corals also catch tiny food. Reefs cover less than 1% of the ocean floor; NOAA pages say they support about 25% of known marine species, but reef scientists note that figure lacks a clear origin, so it appears only at Keeper's Desk as an estimate. "Rainforests of the sea" means variety plus a structure that gives others homes.

**The sea floor.** Shelf, slope, wide abyssal plains with seamounts, a mid-ocean ridge (the longest mountain range on Earth, nearly 65,000 km, more than 90% underwater, NOAA) and narrow trenches, mostly near ocean edges. Average depth about 3,700 to 3,800 m (sources vary). Deepest known point: the **Challenger Deep**, Mariana Trench, about **10,935 m** (NOAA, from a 2021 study; other measurements about 10,900 to 11,000 m). Everest: **8,848.86 m** (China and Nepal, 2020), so its peak would sit about 2,100 m underwater. Plates belong to spec 139.

**The deep.** No sunlight below about 1,000 m (spec 143's midnight zone). Pressure rises about one atmosphere per 10 m (NOAA): about 100 times surface at 1,000 m, about 380 times on an average-depth floor (WHOI). Deep animals are mostly water, which barely compresses, with little or no gas space (secondary sources; Seedling wording kept simple). Cold, dark, little food.

- **Bioluminescence:** light made by a chemical reaction in the body, used to lure food, find mates, or startle or hide from hunters. An anglerfish's lure glows from bacteria living inside it. Martini and Haddock (MBARI, 2017), 17 years of video off California, surface to 3,900 m: 76% of the open-water animals seen could make light; fewer bottom animals. Used as a source-reading item, never as "three-quarters of all ocean animals."
- **Adaptations by challenge:** no light (own light, huge eyes or none); scarce food (big mouths, stretchy stomachs); pressure (soft watery bodies).
- **Marine snow:** dead plankton, animals and waste sinking from the sunlit water, some flakes for weeks; many deep animals eat it, and bacteria decompose the rest in the floor mud (NOAA, Smithsonian). Most deep life still depends on sunlight.
- **Vents:** in **1977** scientists diving in **Alvin** at the Galápagos Rift found hot springs crowded with tube worms and giant clams (WHOI). Bacteria make food from chemicals in vent water (**chemosynthesis**, older depth); snails graze bacterial mats and tube worms host bacteria inside them; the energy is chemical, not heat. Some life does not depend on sunlight.

**Ecosystems.** Living things (producers, consumers, decomposers) plus non-living parts (light, temperature, saltiness, currents). One change ripples in steps. Worked case: muddy water from land blocks light; coral's algae make less food; coral grows less; fewer hiding places; there may be fewer small fish (sediment cutting coral photosynthesis: reef-science sources in audit). Small changes may pass; big or long ones can shift a system. Outcomes say "may."

**Harm and help, calmly.** Litter and plastic (lasts, breaks into small pieces), mud and farm chemicals in runoff, fishing too much, oil spills (rarer). Most ocean pollution starts on land and arrives by drains, rivers and air. "About 80% from land" is widely attributed to NOAA and the UN, but a 2025 paper calls it an old estimate with a weak origin: child text says "most"; Keeper's Desk shows "about 80%, an old rough estimate." Help: river planting, waste-water treatment, beach and river cleans, protected areas, fishing limits, coral replanting. No death counts, named disasters or habit questions.

**How we know (history).**

| Card | What happened | Evidence vs interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| HMS Challenger, Dec 1872 to May 1876 | Sounded with a weighted line. On 23 March 1875, between Guam and Palau, 4,475 fathoms (about 8,184 m; the report table gives 4,475 or 4,476), the deepest of nearly 500 soundings; the Deep is named for the ship. Thousands of new species (sources: about 4,000 to 4,700). | Rope soundings were rough; echo soundings later found nearly 11,000 m nearby. |
| Echo sounding | Depth = sound speed × two-way time ÷ 2, with an assumed 1,500 m/s; real speed varies (NOAA NCEI). | A model with a stated assumption. |
| Marie Tharp, 1950s | At Lamont (Columbia) she turned soundings, mostly collected by Bruce Heezen and others, into profiles and maps; women were kept off the ships (her first cruise: 1968). Around 1952 she noticed a valley along the Mid-Atlantic Ridge crest; Heezen doubted it until earthquake records lined up. North Atlantic diagram published 1957. | Accounts of the discovery differ; the pack says "she noticed" and links to spec 155. |
| Trieste, 23 January 1960 | Piccard and Walsh reached the Challenger Deep floor: about 5 h down, 20 min on the bottom, about 3½ h up (Walsh, 2010; other accounts give 4 h 47 min and 3 h 15 min). | Depth reading later corrected; the cracked window stays out. |
| Alvin, 1977 | Vents, above. | The animals were the surprise. |
| How much seen? 2025 | Whole floor mapped coarsely from space; just over a quarter mapped to modern standards (Seabed 2030: 23.4% in 2022, 27.3% on 21 June 2025). A 2025 *Science Advances* study: less than 0.001% of the deep floor seen, about the area of Rhode Island. | Mapped roughly, mapped in detail, seen. |

**Tools:** sonar ship, satellite, crewed submersible, ROV on a cable, diver; light and radio do not travel far in water, sound does.

**Bramble's claim:** partly right (Moon maps are more complete; little deep floor seen), but it mixes mapping, seeing and understanding. Its origin is not asserted.

## The games

**Sounding bench (model `sound-v1`).** *Line:* tap **Let out line** in 500 m steps until the weight goes slack on a made-up floor; a Sapling **slack** card says long lines drifted. *Echo:* tap **Ping**, read a stopwatch in half seconds, and work depth = 1,500 × t ÷ 2 in three visible steps. Floors per card: 1,500, 4,500 and 6,000 m; 10,935 m at Keeper's Desk. Every view: *A simplified picture. Real sound speed changes with temperature, saltiness and depth.*

**Profile table.** Shore-to-trench strip, vertical 1 cm = 1,000 m, horizontal squeezed (stated). **Drain the sea** replaces Pip's flat line with the real shape; tap-to-name six features with land cousins; optional **Drop Everest in** leaves about 2.1 cm (2,100 m) of water over the peak. Sapling **Tharp step:** plot five depths per cross-section for three sections and spot the repeated central dip.

**Dark water.** A near-black scene: taps reveal animals by their own light (anglerfish, jelly, lanternfish), each asking "What is this light for?" with three jobs. Then a challenge-to-adaptation match, **Follow the snow** from the surface to a sea cucumber, and at Sapling a vent chain (bacteria, vent snail, crab) beside a sunlight chain (phytoplankton, zooplankton, fish), with spec 143's arrow ("is eaten by"); sunlight and chemicals are labels on the first card, never arrow nodes.

**Who lives where? tray.** Pick clownfish, shore crab, anglerfish or sea turtle; give food, a safe place and the right water from three cards each; a calm "settled" picture appears when all are met. A wrong home says "This water is too cold for me. Where else could I go?" No score. Second stage: zoom reef, branch, polyp; polyp-and-snail contrast; reef versus bare-sand variety count.

**Ripple board.** Reef cards (sunlight, warm water, currents, coral, coral's algae, small fish, reef shark, decomposers). Flip a change card (muddy water, cooler water, fewer small fish) and tap "and then?" at least two steps, with "may" outcomes. **Land to sea** strip from a made-up hill farm and town to the reef; **People help** row of six helper cards to place along it. Every view: *A made-up model. Real reefs change in many ways at once.* It never says a reef will die and never asks what the child does.
