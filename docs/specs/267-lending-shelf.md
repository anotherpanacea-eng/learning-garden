## Purpose

All five targets are "not yet mapped" in `curriculum/topic-coverage.csv` and named in no spec on `main`: Discussing Texts as a Group (`mt_thsY1ZesaU`, ages 5-10), Recommending Books (`mt_0MfpLj0Uhb`, 9-10), Comparing Books (`mt_sUVOS2jH3J`, 10-11), How Authors Treat Similar Themes (`mt_jO0gHMk7Ti`, 10-11) and Wide Independent Reading Across Genres (`mt_9k1qcpvVi_`, 11-14). Strand **E3** (`docs/six-act-strands.md`) asks for comparing accounts (E3-III), theme (E3-V) and, at **E3-VI**, "independent reading separately observed"; strand **E5** asks for talk with an adult (E5-I), collaborative talk (E5-II) and comparing viewpoints (E5-III).

Built work reads and talks about single texts: spec 59 pairs two accounts of one event, the Story Loft composes shows, and spec 80's bookstall reads one almanac aloud. Nothing teaches how to take turns in talk about a book, how to build on or kindly disagree with another reader, how to recommend a book to fit a reader with a reason, how to compare two books, how two authors treat one topic differently, or how to choose a wide reading diet across kinds, times and places.

A child learns: book talk takes turns and listening comes first; saying back an idea before adding one; two readers can see a book differently and each points to the book; a recommendation starts from the reader's likes, and a reason names something in the book; "if you liked this, try that" works when both share what the reader liked; two books are compared with "both" and "but"; two stories can share a topic (being new) and differ in theme, seen through the problem, what the character does, the ending and whether the idea is stated; reading wide means kinds, periods and places; a stretch book is a bit harder, not too hard; and taste is stated with a reason.

## The knowledge

Sourced in the [audit](../source-audits/267-lending-shelf.md); unconfirmed claims were cut.

**Talk about books.** The English national curriculum asks Year 1 and 2 pupils to take part in discussion about what is read to them, taking turns and listening to what others say, and Years 5-6 pupils to take part in discussions about books, building on their own and others' ideas and challenging views courteously. Common Core asks kindergartners to engage actively in group reading (RL.K.10, RI.K.10) and to follow agreed rules for discussion (SL.K.1), and grades 1 and 2 to build on others' talk (SL.1.1, SL.2.1). Teresa Cremin and colleagues' reading-for-pleasure research (Open University, 2014) describes informal "inside-text" talk and children's own recommendations as part of a reading community. Here: one speaker at a time, say back, then add with a reason; disagree kindly with a reason from the book.

**Recommending.** Years 5-6: recommending books they have read to their peers, giving reasons for their choices. A recommendation starts from the reader; a reason names a feature in the book (kind, character, setting, what happens); "if you liked X, try Y" works when both share what the reader liked. Marble's evidence for this topic includes keeping a reading log; this pack leaves logs out by the owner's rule.

**Comparing books.** Years 5-6: making comparisons within and across books. Common Core RL.5.9: compare and contrast stories in the same genre on their approaches to similar themes and topics. A topic is what a story is about; a theme is what it says about it (as 261 and 272 teach). Fern and Bea share a topic and genre (short animal stories about a newcomer) and differ in four ways the companion can point to: the problem (a bell in deep water; a post office in a busy town), what the character does (dives alone; asks a snail and they go together), the ending (the pond cheers; a quiet end in which tomorrow she will know one snail), and whether the idea is stated (Fern's last line says it; Bea's story leaves it to the reader).

**Reading wide.** KS3 asks pupils to read a wide range of fiction and non-fiction, including whole books, short stories, poems and plays across genres, periods, forms and authors, with pre-1914 literature, two Shakespeare plays and seminal world literature, and to choose and read books independently for challenge, interest and enjoyment. Common Core RL and RI 6-8.10 ask students to read and understand grade-level literature and nonfiction, with scaffolding as needed in grades 6-7 and independently by the end of grade 8. A stretch book is a bit harder than usual, never so hard it stops making sense. In a randomized study of second and third graders, a mandatory reading log lowered interest in reading for fun compared with a voluntary one (Pak and Weseley, 2012), which supports the owner's rule.

**The old books** (public domain in the US and UK; dates on each card): Kenneth Grahame, *The Wind in the Willows* (1908); Johanna Spyri, *Heidi* (1880-1881, German); Lewis Carroll, *Alice's Adventures in Wonderland* (1865); Robert Louis Stevenson, *A Child's Garden of Verses* (1885); William Shakespeare, *A Midsummer Night's Dream* (first printed 1600) and *Twelfth Night* (first printed 1623); Jean de La Fontaine, *Fables* (from 1668, French); Gilbert White, *The Natural History of Selborne* (1789). *The Secret Garden* (272's second book) is left off this shelf (see Review asks).

## The lesson

Jane's **teach -> guided -> practice**: twelve teach cards, one idea each with a worked example revealed one step per tap; each set's first item (A1, B1, C1, D1, E1) shows its steps first. **"I'm stuck" ladder** on every item: hint -> an easier case plus how to start this one (Pip's nap said back; Puff and the dragon book; an apple and a pear; Pip's nap as a problem; a card that says "A play") -> skip for now, no penalty.

**Contrasts:** talking over against listening to the end; "you're wrong" against "I see it differently, because"; your taste against the reader's; "it's good" against a reason from the book; a fact book about animals against a story with an animal in it; a weak link (water, the same author) against the shared thing the reader loved; both against but; topic against theme; Fern alone against Bea together; a loud ending against a quiet one; stated against left to the reader; three novels against a list with a play; old against first written in another language; a stretch against too hard; others' taste against your own with a reason.

## The game or activity

Every screen: *Pick a book, talk about a book, or help a friend find one. Nothing here counts what you read.* No score, timer, lock or mastery label. Drags have tap equivalents. Every item shows the book cards or both stories it asks about, with read-aloud, so it can be done at any reading length.

- **(a) Book Talk (every depth; spoken).** Choose any book both people know. **Pass the lantern** shows whose turn it is and counts and keeps nothing. Five talk starters and a listening card. Nothing is stored.
- **(b) Find a Book For... (Seedling, Sapling).** Six friend cards (Ember, Hob, Dot, Bramble, the orchard keeper, the apprentice), each with three book cards; pick the book whose card has both likes, then the best of three reasons. Fitting books sit first, second, second, third, first, third; fitting reasons first, second, third, first, second, third.
- **(c) Side by Side (Sapling; Keeper's Desk for the plays).** Three pairs (the two fact books whose makers swap jobs; Fern and Bea; Shakespeare's two plays), six fact cards each into *this book*, *both*, *that book*; then a "Both... but..." sentence from the placed cards.
- **(d) Two Stories, One Idea (Sapling, Keeper's Desk).** Both stories side by side; eight cards into a four-row table (problem, what the character does, ending, what it says about being new) under Fern or Bea; then a spoken view on which ending the child likes better, both accepted.
- **(e) The Lantern Night List (Sapling, Keeper's Desk).** Five slots (story or novel, poems, play, true book, long ago or far away) plus an optional stretch slot; each placement is checked only against the card's stated kind and date. The list shows no tick, count, date read or time, and it is not stored.
