Three message forms per scene, exactly18 messages:

1. **Identifying detail:** `{noun} {clause} {predicate}`. The main statement refers to the scene’s matches-list. Its property status is fits: every included member has the stated feature in these authored cases. Explain: **“The detail identifies which {couriers/parcels/books} the message talks about: {matching labels}. It says they {action with will}. It does not say that {the full label of the left-out figure} will not {action}.”** Produce natural family-specific wording from the packet below, not a naive strip parser.
2. **Extra detail:** `{noun}, {clause}, {predicate}`. The noun group is already established by the scene: all three. The main statement refers to all A/B/C and adds the clause’s property about that whole group. Mixed scenes: the detail conflicts with the pictured exceptions; name them. All-share scenes: it fits all three. Never label this form ungrammatical merely because its extra claim is false. No claim that “commas always mean everyone” outside this fixed, established plural-group context.
3. **No detail:** `{noun} {predicate}`. Main statement refers to all three; it says nothing about their bag/label/cover. Removing an identifying detail can broaden reference; removing extra detail keeps the already established group but removes information. “The sentence still works” is not a test that meaning stayed the same.

Family-specific action phrases for interpretation: couriers **“will get route cards”**, other-inference **“get route cards”**; parcels **“will go on the cart”**, other-inference **“go on the cart”**; books **“will go on the display table”**, other-inference **“go on the display table.”** Do not duplicate “will” or lose agreement. Joined label lists are A and C, B and C, A and B, or A, B and C with ordinary English conjunctions, always full figure labels in narration. Avoid “only these get cards”; “only these are explicitly included in this main statement” is a scope distinction, not an assertion that others cannot receive cards.

The left-out-figure sentence is used only for mixed scenes. In all-share scenes, omit it entirely and instead explicitly say: **“Both versions include all three here. One identifies the group by its feature; the other adds that information about the group already identified.”** Both forms can be grammatical and fit; do not force a stylistic winner. The no-comma use of which is grammatical; some US style guides prefer that for identifying things. The game does not grade that style preference. This increment offers who/which as prepared words, not a pronoun-choice quiz. Whose/whom/where/when/why, object-relative omission, coordinated relatives, ambiguous real-world contexts and free-text parsing remain missing.

### Exact five teaching cards

1. **Start with a sentence.** “The lanterns will hang by the door.” names a group and says something about it. It starts with a capital and ends with a period. We can join two sentences: “The lanterns will hang by the door, and they will light the entrance.” List commas have another job: “The shelf holds a map, a pencil, and a bell.” The pair we try next brackets extra information.
2. **A detail identifies which ones.** Our separate scene has Lantern A with a silver handle, Lantern B with a wooden handle, and Lantern C with a silver handle. “The lanterns which have silver handles will hang by the door.” identifies A and C. “Which have silver handles” is a relative clause about the noun group here, not a complete main sentence. Earlier we used that for some identifying details. That also works without commas. With a pair of commas, use who for people or which for things.
3. **Remove the identifying detail.** “The lanterns will hang by the door.” now talks about all three lanterns. It is still a sentence, but the group changed. The earlier message did not say that Lantern B will not hang by the door. Removing words can change meaning even when the sentence still works.
4. **Add both commas.** “The lanterns, which have silver handles, will hang by the door.” talks about the established group of all three and adds that they have silver handles. Lantern B’s wooden handle conflicts with that detail. The sentence is grammatical, but its extra detail does not fit this scene. Both commas bracket the clause; a comma is not just a place to breathe. Grammar names: identifying or defining clause; extra-information or non-defining clause.
5. **Another scene: all silver.** In this different scene all three lantern handles are silver. Both versions can fit: “The lanterns which have silver handles will hang by the door.” and “The lanterns, which have silver handles, will hang by the door.” Both include all three, but the detail has a different job. Removing the extra detail keeps that group and leaves the handle information out. Return to your waiting message.
