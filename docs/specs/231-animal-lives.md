## Purpose

Animals of the World and Ocean Life are among the largest untouched science domains. Drafts already cover where animals live and migration by air ([144](144-ways-of-the-birds.md)), homes, signals and endangered species ([166](166-valley-animal-book.md)), mammal-or-fish, food webs and depth zones ([143](143-letters-from-the-coast.md)), the deep sea ([171](171-down-to-the-deep.md)), vertebrate groups and keys ([175](175-sorting-life.md)) and social insects ([150](150-bee-meadow.md)). Nobody yet teaches animal records, how scientists test what animals can do, symbiosis, the argument for living in groups, the invertebrate split in the ocean, or ocean migrations.

A child comes to understand that each record names its own **kind of most**, best understood beside something familiar; that an ocean animal's group is decided by **features** (backbone, then gills, then milk), and that most ocean animals have **no backbone**; that living in a herd, pack or colony helps **each animal**, with a cost to weigh, and how to argue it with **claim, evidence and reasoning**; that **symbiosis** is a close, lasting life between different kinds, sorted by what each partner **gains or loses**; that many animals think and learn, and we know it only through **tests that rule out simpler explanations**; and that ocean migrations are **round trips** between seasonal food and safe places for young.

## The knowledge

**Records are kinds of most (Sprout).** Biggest, tallest, fastest and smallest are separate records, each with its own holder. No animal ever found is bigger or heavier than the **blue whale** (NOAA: up to about 110 feet); a big one is longer than two school buses end to end. A few long-necked dinosaurs might have matched its length; none known was as heavy. The **giraffe** is the tallest animal alive today (up to about 18 feet: tall enough to look in an upstairs window); some long-necked dinosaurs stood taller, so "alive today" goes on the tallest and biggest-on-land records wherever the dinosaur card is in play. The **cheetah** is the fastest on **land**, for a short dash (Smithsonian: about 64 mph, faster than a car on a town street); a **peregrine falcon** in a dive goes faster. The **bee hummingbird** is the smallest **bird**, not the smallest animal: about 2 g (lighter than a nickel), small enough to perch on a pencil. The **elephant** is the biggest animal on land today. Size and weight comparisons stay separate. No numbers on Sprout screens.

**The ocean key (Seedling).** Groups are decided by features, never by habitat, size, shape or name. Three questions in order: **backbone?** (no: **invertebrate**: jellyfish, octopus, crab, starfish); **gills?** (yes: **fish**: shark, cod, seahorse, whose skin covers bony plates instead of the usual scales); **milk?** (yes: **marine mammal**: seal, dolphin); otherwise **another group** (the sea turtle is a reptile, 175's group). Most kinds of ocean animals are invertebrates. Egg-laying is never used as a test.

**Groups help each animal, at a cost (Seedling to Sapling).** Living in a herd, pack or colony can raise each member's chance of surviving: **staying safe** (more eyes; izard whistles, meerkat lookouts), **getting food** (wolves hunting elk together; bees' dance, from 150) and **raising young** (an ant colony's workers). Explanations name what the group does for each animal, not feelings, not orders and not kindness "for the species". Costs are real: a bigger group shares the same food. The argument is built as **claim, evidence, reasoning**. Disease spread, a cost the research names, is left out under wave 5's no-illness rule (owner question 3).

**Symbiosis (Sapling).** A close, long-lasting relationship between different kinds of living things. Decide the kind from each partner's outcome: **mutualism** + + (clownfish and anemone; ants and aphids; coral and its algae), **commensalism** + 0 (remora and shark; a bird nesting in an oak), **parasitism** + − (tick and deer; mistletoe and apple tree). Predation (owl and mouse) and same-kind squabbles are not symbiosis. Plants and algae can be partners. "0" means no clear change: some scientists think sharks gain a little from remoras, so "?" (not enough evidence) is accepted on the borderline cards. Mutualism is not a deal; each partner does what helps itself.

**How do we know what animals think? (Sapling to Keeper's Desk).** Seeing is not knowing. Look for a simpler explanation, then design a test. Clever Hans (Pfungst, 1907) read his askers' movements, not sums. The **mirror mark test** (Gallup, 1970, chimpanzees; Reiss and Marino, 2001, dolphins) asks whether an animal uses a mirror to inspect a mark placed on itself; it tests one ability, not intelligence in general. Magpies passed in 2008 and wild-caught magpies did not in 2020: not settled. Tool use: chimpanzees fishing for termites (Goodall, 1960), Betty the crow's wire hook (2002, with the later caveat), rooks raising water with stones (2009). Octopuses unscrew jar lids in aquariums. Meerkat helpers give pups dead or stingless scorpions before live ones, and practiced pups learn faster (2006): teaching by a stated definition. Elephants examine and touch the bones of dead elephants; whether that is grief is open (Keeper's Desk only). Intelligence takes many forms; there is no one ladder with people on the top rung.

**Ocean migrations (Sapling to Keeper's Desk).** A migration is a round trip on a seasonal timetable. North Pacific humpbacks feed in cold Alaskan water in summer, when the water blooms with food and krill swarm, and store blubber; in winter they swim to warm Hawaiian water, about 3,000 miles (about 4,800 km), sometimes in 28 days, where calves are born; NOAA says blubber stored in summer sustains them through the winter. Some humpback groups travel about 5,000 miles. Female sea turtles return to nest on the beach where they hatched, or very near it; they seem to steer by Earth's magnetic field (loggerhead studies). Carr's tagging at Tortuguero, Costa Rica, from 1955: about 12,000 nesting females tagged by 1976, more than a thousand seen nesting there again, none reported elsewhere. Navigation is wonder only; the assessed core is why (food and young) and scale.

**History and story cards.** *A true thing from history:* de Bary's broad use of *symbiosis* (1879); Pfungst and Clever Hans (1907); Goodall at Gombe, Tanzania (1960); Gallup's mark test (1970); Carr at Tortuguero (from 1955). *A story people told*, each paired with what we know now: Herodotus's crocodile bird eating leeches (Greek writer, about Egypt, about 430 BCE; never photographed); Pliny's ship-stopping remora (Roman, about 77 CE; *remora* is Latin for delay); Aesop's crow and pitcher, tested with rooks in 2009.

## The game or activity

**(a) Record Parade (Sprout).** Six animal cut-outs and five familiar things (two school buses, an upstairs window, a car in town, a pencil, a nickel). Slide a thing beside an animal; a matching pair says the comparison aloud. A **kind of most** sorter (biggest, tallest, fastest, smallest) catches the giraffe-is-biggest and cheetah-is-fastest-of-all slips. A dinosaur card answers the dinosaur question. No numbers on screen.

**(b) Ocean Key (Seedling).** Léon's ten cards run through three yes/no questions in order. Tap spots on each picture show the evidence (backbone drawing, gills, blowhole, calf with mother, scales, bony plates). The turtle exits at "another group". A count line ends the run: four of ten have no backbone.

**(c) Lookout Meadow, model `lookout-v1`.** *A made-up model; the rules are on screen.*

- Inputs: herd `1`, `2`, `5`, `10`; for a herd of 1 only, look-up minutes `2`, `6`, `10` (in a herd, each izard looks up 2).
- Rules: each izard looks up its minutes in every 10, at different times from the others; minutes watched = min(10, look-up × herd). The patch has 20 bites; grazing gives half a bite a minute; bites each = min((10 − look-up) ÷ 2, 20 ÷ herd). Each izard needs 4.
- Outputs (computed): alone 2/10 watched, 4 bites; alone looking up 6: 6/10, 2 bites; alone looking up 10: 10/10, 0 bites; herd 2: 4/10, 4; herd 5: 10/10, 4; herd 10: 10/10, 2. Only the herd of 5 has full watching and enough grass.
- Model line: "Real izards look up for different lengths of time, and herds change size through the year. This bench uses four herd sizes and one patch of grass."

The **Claim Builder** (Sapling) offers three claims, four evidence cards and three reasons, with feelings and "always better" cards as tagged distractors. Three "ways a group helps" cards follow (meerkats, wolves and bees, ants).

**(d) Partners Grid (Sapling).** Nine pairs, each marked +, 0, − or ? for both partners, then named by a decision path. Two impostors (owl and mouse; two blackbirds and a worm) teach "not symbiosis". The remora and oak cards accept ? with a note.

**(e) How Do We Know? (Sapling to Keeper's Desk).** Eight case files, each with three tabs: what was seen, a simpler idea, the test and what it showed. A mirror mark diagram sits on the dolphin file. The elephant file is at Keeper's Desk only. A **kinds of clever** panel has four shelves (makes tools, solves puzzles, knows itself, teaches) and no ladder.

**(f) Ocean Year, model `ocean-year-v1`.** *A made-up model: one simplified year of one North Pacific humpback.* A twelve-month dial moves the whale among cold north (June to September, feeding), traveling (October to December; April and May) and warm south (January to March, calves born, living on stored blubber). A food-bloom layer greens the north in summer. The turtle panel is four picture steps and a magnetic-field note. Model line: "Real whales leave at different times, and not every whale goes south every year. This dial shows one simple year."

No timers, scores, streaks, medals or locks. Every bench is open from the first visit.
