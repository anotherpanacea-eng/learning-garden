## Purpose

The Energy domain is the largest science gap in the map: [six-act strands](../six-act-strands.md) lists 28 Marble Energy topics under **S6** with **none** covered. The built [Lantern Circuit Bench (spec 98)](98-lantern-circuit-bench.md) teaches a complete path through a bulb and deliberately refuses energy movement; the [Fair Test Bench (56/60)](56-fair-test-bench.md) teaches comparison design on a ramp; the proposed Clockwork Mill (spec 123, draft PR #128) owns gears, mechanisms and programming the cart Trundle. Nobody yet answers Pip's question: *where does the go come from, and where does it go?*

A child comes to understand that energy is what makes things happen and moves from one thing to another; that the mill's energy comes from the Sun lifting water into the hills; that faster water carries more energy; that energy is never used up or destroyed, only spread out (mostly as warmth) so it becomes less useful; that friction is a force that warms things; that no machine gives out more than it takes in, so no wheel can run itself; at older depth, that efficiency is useful out over total in, shown in a Sankey diagram; and that renewable sources are refilled by nature, which is different from free or harmless.

## The knowledge

**Energy and what it does.** Energy is what makes things happen: things move, warm up, light up, make sound. It is never made from nothing and never destroyed. It moves from one thing to another and, in every real process, some spreads out into the surroundings.

**Two vocabularies, and how they relate.** Younger cards use everyday "types of energy" words, as Marble's *Naming types of energy* does: movement energy, stored energy, heat, light and sound energy. Following that record, they are sorted into three bins: energy **waiting** in something (water high up, grain, wood), energy a **moving** thing has, and energy **traveling** between things (light, sound, heating). Older cards use the stores-and-pathways model now used in English KS3 and taught by the Institute of Physics: **stores** (kinetic, gravitational, thermal, chemical, among others) and **transfers** (mechanical working such as water pushing paddles, heating, radiation such as sunlight, and electrical working). Light, sound and electric current are ways energy travels, never stores. A matching panel (companion T8) lines the two vocabularies up so the younger words never need unlearning.

**The mill chain (a map, then one step at a time).**
1. Sunlight warms seas, lakes and wet ground; water evaporates and rises as vapor, which cools and condenses into clouds. Rain and snow fall on high ground. Water high up has a **gravitational store**. Hydropower's energy therefore comes from the Sun, which drives the water cycle. (The child text says only this much. It does not try to say what share of the Sun's energy ends up in high water.)
2. Water runs downhill: the gravitational store empties and the **kinetic store** of the moving brook fills (with some warming by friction with the bed).
3. The brook pushes the paddles (a force). Energy moves into the kinetic store of the wheel and axle.
4. The axle turns the millstones, which grind grain. Grinding, friction at the axle and gears, splashing and noise all end with the energy in the **thermal store** of the stones, flour, machinery and air, plus a little sound that also ends as warming.
5. **The bread contrast.** The energy stored in bread came from sunlight, captured by the wheat plant and stored in the grain (a chemical store). The wheel's energy ground the grain and then spread out; it did not go into the flour. (The baker's oven warms the dough from its own fuel.)

The Marble *Energy stores and transfers* record warns against long energy chains. The spec uses the full path once, as a story map of the valley, then at Sapling depth asks for start and end stores **one step at a time**, with the machinery (paddles, axle, gears) explaining the middle.

**Speed and energy.** For the same amount of water, faster water has more kinetic energy; the evidence is what it does (pushes harder, turns the wheel faster, grinds more). Mass is held constant in every speed comparison, as the record asks. Grinding more grain per hour needs more useful energy per hour.

**Energy and force.** A force is a push or pull acting now (the water on a paddle). Energy is what is transferred while the force moves the paddle. A moving thing *has* energy; it does not carry a stored push that runs out.

**Conserved but spread out.** Energy is never used up; it spreads out (dissipates), mostly warming the surroundings, and becomes harder to use. What gets used up is a **fuel**, such as coal or wood. Friction is a force between surfaces that opposes sliding and warms them (rubbing hands is the felt example). Friction can also help: feet grip, and millstone faces are cut with grooves (furrows) that shear the grain and carry it outward, with flat lands between that grind it. Millers cut the furrows partly to keep the stones from overheating, which is good evidence that grinding warms the stones.

**No perpetual motion.** No machine gives out more energy than goes in, and every real machine spreads some out as heat and sound. Both reasons are needed (the Marble conservation record says so explicitly): a perfectly frictionless self-filling wheel would still only break even, and real ones lose a little every turn and stop. Historical anchor: designs for self-pumping water mills and overbalanced wheels go back centuries. Overbalanced wheels are the oldest and most common kind: Bhāskara II described one in the 12th century, and Villard de Honnecourt sketched one around 1225-1235. Robert Fludd published a closed-cycle water mill in 1618; Britannica notes he wrongly thought the falling water would give more energy than an Archimedes screw needs to lift it back. Sources disagree on whether his lifter was a screw or a bucket chain, so the child text just says "pumps".

**Efficiency and Sankey diagrams (Keeper's Desk).** Efficiency = useful energy out ÷ total energy in, as a fraction or percentage; it can never exceed 100%. In a Sankey diagram, the arrow in splits into useful and spread-out branches whose widths, drawn to a stated scale, add up to the input. All numbers here are illustrative and labeled so.

**Real anchor: overshot and undershot wheels.** In an undershot wheel the stream pushes paddles at the bottom (mainly the water's kinetic energy); in an overshot wheel water fills buckets at the top and its weight turns the wheel as it falls (mainly the gravitational store). John Smeaton's model experiments, published in 1759, found overshot wheels about twice as efficient as undershot wheels, against common opinion of his day. Modern figures depend heavily on design: one study reports about 85% for well-designed overshot wheels and about 75% for undershot wheels, while field tests of simple undershot wheels often measure far lower. The original draft's "20 to 30% undershot" range was not supported and has been cut. Item 3.8 now uses 60 and 30 as illustrative values matching Smeaton's "about twice".

**Power from flow and fall.** The energy a stream delivers each second is proportional to the flow of water and the height it falls (hydro power = efficiency × water density × g × flow × head). The bench's made-up rule copies that proportionality on purpose.

**Renewable and non-renewable.** A source is renewable if nature refills it quickly enough for people to keep using it (sunlight, wind, flowing water, regrown biomass such as replanted firewood); non-renewable if it is a finite stock used much faster than it forms (coal, oil, natural gas, uranium for nuclear power). Two separate questions are kept apart, as the Marble record requires: *will it run out?* and *what does it emit or harm?* Replanted firewood is renewable but burning it makes smoke and carbon dioxide; uranium is non-renewable but low-carbon. Electricity is a carrier, classified by its source. Renewable is not "free" (machines, repairs and labor cost) and not "harmless" (dams change rivers and fish movement). A modern small hydro station uses a turbine and generator instead of a millstone; a run-of-river scheme takes part of the flow through the turbine. Leaving enough water in the river and getting fish past are recognized problems, and the evidence on how well current measures work is limited. The child card says only that the river needs water left in it and fish need a way past. No real project is named or endorsed.

**"I'm stuck" ladder** on every item: (1) the item's hint; (2) a worked example of an easier similar case plus how to start this one (a ball falling off a shelf; water 1 on the bench; a swing slowing down); (3) **Skip for now**, with no penalty and nothing recorded against the child.

**Contrasts:** energy vs force (the push on the paddle and what the push transfers); "used up" vs transferred and spread out (the stopping wheel; fuel vs energy); renewable vs "free" (the brook costs nothing, the wheel costs a lot); the wheel's energy vs the bread's energy (two jobs of the Sun); renewable vs low-carbon (firewood and uranium); too little energy vs too much (Act VI's sluice gate).

**Top misconceptions designed against:** energy gets used up; friction destroys energy; a machine or generator makes energy; a moving object carries a stored push (impetus); faster and slower water have the same energy if the amount is the same; sound, light and electricity are stores; grinding puts energy into flour; efficiency can exceed 100%; renewable means free or harmless; low-carbon means renewable; electricity is a source.

## The game or activity

**(a) Follow the energy.** Six station cards (Sun, hills, brook, wheel, millstones, end). *Sprout:* order the cards by drag or tap-to-place, then drag one token along. *Seedling and up:* ten tokens; from station 4, three labeled pots (*splash and spray*, *warm axle and squeak*, *warm stones and hum*) take tokens the child chooses to drop (at least one at stations 4 and 5). A counter always shows the total of ten. At station 6 every token is shown as warmth and sound, and the copy explains that grinding also warms. A **wheat branch** opens afterward. *Sapling:* tap each step to reveal its start and end stores.

**(b) The wheel bench, model `mill-energy-v1`.** *A made-up model; the rule is on screen.*

- Inputs: water `w` ∈ {1, 2, 3, 4} (sluice notches); fall `f` ∈ {1 low, 2 middle, 3 high}; axle grease `g` ∈ {fresh 20%, old 40%, dry 60%} spread out.
- Energy in per hour `E = 10 × w × f` illustrative units; spread out `S = E × g`; useful `U = E − S`; flour `U ÷ 2` scoops per hour; efficiency `U ÷ E` (Keeper's Desk only).
- Every one of the 36 settings gives whole numbers; flour ranges from 2 to 48 scoops.
- Display: readouts, a Sankey strip at stated scale (1 square = 10 units), a cartoon wheel whose speed rises with `E` (labeled as a cue, not a measured speed) and a warm, squeaking axle at the dry setting.
- Assumptions stated: all waste is lumped as axle friction; real wheels also lose energy to spills, splashing and leaks; real speed depends on wheel size and load; the proportional rule copies how flow and fall set a stream's power.
- No wheel-type control: overshot and undershot appear only as an illustrative item (3.8).

**(c) Perpetual-motion detective.** Three made-up machines (Bramble's self-filling wheel, Ember's fan-mill, a tipping-weights wheel). Each has a machine card, one 3-choice question with tagged wrong answers, and an optional *say why* for a grown-up. Case 1 shows an authored token view: 10 in, 7 passed on, 3 lifted back.

**(d) Sort the sources.** Nine cards into three bins: *Renewable*, *Non-renewable*, *Not a source (it carries energy from one)*. Placement shows the card's feedback and can be changed freely. Afterward, two cards (renewable isn't free; a modern small hydro comparison) and a *say why* prompt.

No timers, scores, streaks, medals or locks. Every activity is open from the first visit.
