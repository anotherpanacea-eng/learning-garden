## Meaningful challenge and reader changes

Three depths alter the reasoning task, teaching and structured checks, not text length or an age label:

1. **Notice and infer**: distinguish an observed act from an inferred motive; identify what a second account contributes; choose a later detail that warrants a narrow update. Teaching explicitly models action versus motive and a proportionate claim.
2. **Compare and qualify**: distinguish shared events, unequal access and corroboration; choose what a dated document supports and what it leaves open. Teaching models why agreement about an event does not prove a motive and why partial records can still be useful.
3. **Answer a fair objection**: compare two plausible explanations against earlier and later details; identify an objection that exposes a claim’s real limit and a revision that addresses it. Teaching models claim scope, an alternative explanation and the strongest remaining uncertainty. A fitting answer must explain the relation between evidence and a claim, rather than merely repeat an observation.

Every depth includes five structured questions with varied fitting positions, plausible alternatives (including an evidence-specific underclaim where suitable) and explanatory feedback. Every question includes an authored story-specific near miss with comparable scope and tone: a swapped actor/action, a nearby but wrong second-account purpose, a record misread as completed work, or an uncertainty about something the text already settles. Fitting responses name concrete accounts, omissions and evidence too; being the only qualified or moderate-sounding answer must not reveal the key. The shared question constructor capitalizes all supplied labels uniformly. Complete alternatives require substantive source review rather than a mechanical length test. Original prose, annotations, amount written and kept drafts are never automatically graded or used for suggestions.

Reader choices change the actual communicative purpose in both writing and revision prompts: **a curious newcomer** needs the event and one explained detail; **a skeptical discussion partner** needs a fair alternative and a justified limitation; **a decision-making committee** needs a recommendation proportionate to uncertainty. Their objections differ: missing context, rival explanation, or acting under incomplete evidence. They are fictional readers; no real learner data enters sources. Optional planning fields remain distinct from original paragraphs. The revision screen shows the exact first draft, the selected reader’s request, and an optional relevant objection; a learner may retain a view with reasons.

## Canonical original texts

### The Last Seat

#### Chapter 1 — An empty place

**c1-p1** I had arranged every chair in the little hall before the musicians arrived. Seven rows, eight seats in each, with an aisle so straight I could see the back door through it. My aunt had taught me that a room should look ready before people asked whether it was ready. I was helping with my first public rehearsal, and I wanted the room to answer that question for me. The printed programme lay on the front seat, folded to show my name beside “hall assistant.”

**c1-p2** When I returned from the kitchen, one chair was missing from the end of the back row. Tomas was rolling it toward the cupboard. He stopped when he saw me. “That place was reserved,” I said. He glanced at the little square of paper on the floor, then kept moving. “There will still be room,” he answered. I picked up the paper. It said RESERVED in my careful blue capitals. I thought he had decided that my careful work did not matter because I was new.

**c1-p3** I could have fetched another chair. Instead, I laid the sign on the table where everyone would notice it. Through the open door I heard the strings testing one bright note over and over. Tomas came back carrying a wooden wedge. He slipped it under the back door and pushed the door open farther. The wind lifted a corner of my programme. I put a cup on it. A room could look untidy very quickly if everyone made private improvements.

#### Chapter 2 — Tomas’s errand

**c2-p1** I saw the reserved sign only after I had started moving the chair. The singer had telephoned to say her father was coming in his wheelchair, and I had promised to leave space near the door. I did not know who had written the sign. When Ada asked about it, I looked down because I was trying to remember whether the singer had wanted one space or two. I should have said that. Instead, I said there would still be room, as if counting chairs answered her question.

**c2-p2** Last month the back door had swung shut during a delivery. The person carrying the amplifier had bruised a hand against its edge. I brought a wedge because I remembered the bruise, not because I wanted to spoil the straight aisle. The door opened into the hall, narrowing the space beside that last chair. I tested the opening with the empty trolley. A trolley is not a wheelchair, and I knew it. It was simply the thing I had at hand.

**c2-p3** Ada watched me from the kitchen table. The blue sign was beside her cup. I could have asked whom she was expecting, but the conductor called for the music stands and I went to find them. I told myself an explanation could wait until after rehearsal. That was comfortable for me because I already knew my explanation. It was less comfortable, I imagine, for someone who had only seen me take a chair away.

#### Chapter 3 — The plan on the wall

**c3-p1** While looking for tape, Ada found a pencilled floor plan pinned behind the cupboard door. A wide rectangle near the back entrance was labelled CLEAR SPACE. Beside it was last week’s date and the singer’s name. It showed that the space had been requested before Ada arranged the chairs. It did not show whether anyone had told her. In the margin someone had written, “Check door swing.” The words were underlined twice, but the plan gave no measurement.

**c3-p2** She carried the plan to Tomas. “This should have been on the wall outside the cupboard,” she said. He nodded, then asked about the reserved sign. Ada’s aunt was coming late and had asked for a seat near an exit because she might have to leave early. They looked at the doorway together. Neither request had been imaginary. Neither could be met simply by restoring the original row. Ada fetched two strips of tape, and Tomas moved the trolley away so they could see the floor.

**c3-p3** When the visitors arrived, the back row bent a little, with a chair beside a clear space and the door safely held open. Ada’s aunt sat in the chair. The singer’s father stopped beside her, and they argued cheerfully about which piece should come first. After rehearsal, Ada put the blue sign in her pocket. She drew the changed row on the old plan but left the first drawing visible. Above both she wrote, “Ask what a place is for before deciding it is empty.”

### The Map in the Drawer

#### Chapter 1 — The missing path

**c1-p1** The new map of the island was spread across our shop counter when the first visitor asked how to reach the western beach. I traced the road with my finger and stopped. The narrow path behind the orchard was missing. My grandfather had taken me along it every spring, past a stone trough where the birds drank. Without it, the map sent walkers around the long road. In the corner, beneath the legend, was the surveyor’s signature: Leena Vale.

**c1-p2** Leena was outside measuring the shop doorway. I carried the map to her. “You left out the orchard path,” I said. She did not look surprised. “This edition has a particular purpose,” she answered. “There will be a notice.” Then she stepped aside to let a delivery cart pass. I heard an excuse where I had expected a correction. Our shop sold the maps, and I had already told three visitors that this was the best one. A missing line could make my own advice look careless.

**c1-p3** I pencilled the path onto a spare copy, making the line neat and confident. I remembered every turn, or thought I did. At the trough the path forked: one branch reached the beach and the other ended at a locked orchard gate. I could not remember which fork the gate stood beyond. I put a question mark there, then rubbed it out. It spoiled the map’s clean look. When Leena returned for her measuring case, I slid my copy under the till.

#### Chapter 2 — What the map promised

**c2-p1** The shopkeeper’s assistant looked angry before I had explained anything. I had been asked to map routes suitable for the island’s small maintenance carts, not every route a person might walk. The orchard path crossed a footbridge with a limit that no loaded cart could safely ignore. I left it off the cart layer. The public walking layer was supposed to be printed on the reverse, but the copies on the counter had a blank back. That was the first printing error I had seen.

**c2-p2** I said there would be a notice because I had written one that morning. I should have asked whether it had arrived. Instead, I measured the doorway, finished the delivery survey and carried my case away. A task can feel urgent merely because its instructions are in your own pocket. The assistant had no reason to know those instructions. I saw his pencilled map later, when he lifted the till to find a receipt. The line was lovely. It went directly through the old gate.

**c2-p3** I almost laughed, then remembered my own first survey. I had drawn a bridge from an old photograph and discovered the next day that it had been removed. I asked whether he would walk the orchard route with me. He said the path was still there. I said I believed him. What I wanted to check was what a reader of either map would expect the line to promise. He came, but he kept the folded paper in his hand instead of giving it to me.

#### Chapter 3 — Two kinds of blank

**c3-p1** At the shop that evening, they found the printer’s notebook in the delivery drawer. An entry dated before the maps arrived said, “Reverse walking layer not supplied on this batch; hold public sale.” It established that the missing layer had been noticed before the sale. It did not name the person who received that instruction. Beneath it was a record of a replacement delivery, scheduled for Friday. Friday was two days away, and visitors would arrive on both days.

**c3-p2** On their walk, the gate had been open. Beyond it, the footbridge still stood, with a new weight-limit sign and one loose rail. The assistant had remembered the path but placed the fork too early. Leena had measured the road correctly but sold no maps herself and had not checked the public copies. Each could point to something the other had missed. Neither discovery made the island simple. They decided that a blank back and a missing route needed different explanations.

**c3-p3** They took the maps off the sale rack and pinned one beside a handwritten notice. The notice explained the cart routes, the printing error and the footbridge’s limits. The assistant added a separate sketch of the walk, with a question mark where repairs might change access. A visitor asked why it was less tidy than the printed map. “Because you might use it,” he said. Leena sharpened his pencil. Outside, the road and the footpath disappeared into the same evening trees.

### A Light Across the Water

#### Chapter 1 — A signal unanswered

**c1-p1** From the harbour office I could see the lamp on the far pier, a small yellow square above the dark water. Our volunteer group was testing a new signal: three short flashes meant that a walker at the far shelter wanted the ferry office to telephone for assistance. I was writing a report for the harbour meeting. If the test worked, the group hoped to keep the lamp through winter. If it failed, we would have to explain what kind of failure we had actually seen.

**c1-p2** At eight o’clock the far lamp flashed three times. I looked toward the ferry office. Its window stayed dark. Beside me, Neri closed the little testing box and put it in a bag. “You saw it,” I said. He nodded. “Someone should answer,” I said. He checked his watch. “We have another minute.” The water slapped the steps below us. I wrote, “The office ignored the signal,” and underlined ignored. It seemed a stronger word than missed, and the meeting needed strong evidence.

**c1-p3** A minute later the office window lit. Someone moved behind the glass and vanished again. No ferry left the landing. Neri asked me to write the times as well as the conclusion. I did, but I thought he was protecting the equipment he had helped install. The testing box had worked for him all afternoon. It was easy to trust a thing when your own hands had fitted its wires. Across the water, the far lamp was steady now, as though nothing had happened.

#### Chapter 2 — In the ferry office

**c2-p1** I was in the store room when the signal came, looking for the telephone list. Our instruction sheet said the signal must be acknowledged by turning on the office window light, then calling the harbour contact. It did not tell us to launch the ferry. I heard the buzzer at eight, found the list and switched on the light at one minute past. The first number rang without answer. I called the second number, and the volunteer at the shelter answered immediately.

**c2-p2** On the telephone she said that she was running a test. She had a flask of tea and no one needed help. I wrote TEST beside the time in the log, then noticed that my watch and the wall clock differed by two minutes. I used the wall clock because the earlier entries used it. That seemed sensible inside the office. From another building, anyone comparing my entry with a watch might think the order of events had changed. I did not think of that until later.

**c2-p3** When Neri asked how it had gone, I said, “We answered.” I could hear how final those words sounded. They left out the missing telephone list, the unanswered first call and the two clocks. I had followed the sheet, but I had also left it in the store room where nobody at the harbour window could read it. I was tired of hearing that volunteers were unreliable. I wanted one clean sentence to stand between that accusation and the muddled evening.

#### Chapter 3 — What the log could show

**c3-p1** Next morning, the report writer opened the office log. Before the test entry was a note from the previous afternoon: “Window light is the acknowledgement; telephone contact follows.” Below it were two telephone entries. The written procedure showed that an acknowledgement did not require a departing ferry. The entries supported the office worker’s account of attempted calls. But the notebook did not settle the exact minute of each event, and the first number was crossed out without an explanation.

**c3-p2** They carried both clocks to the harbour office and set them side by side. The difference was real. So was the uncertainty in the old times; setting the clocks now could not repair yesterday’s observations. Neri suggested writing that the system had succeeded. The report writer opened the testing box and closed it again. “Some parts worked,” she said. “That is not the same sentence.” The office worker nodded, then asked that the unanswered number remain in the report as something to fix.

**c3-p3** At the meeting, their report described the flashes, the light, the calls and the uncertain timing. It proposed another test with shared instructions and synchronized clocks. One member complained that the report had no simple verdict. Another asked whether someone alone at the shelter would know to watch for the office light. No one had tested that. The writer added the question beneath her recommendation. Across the water, the lamp was invisible in daylight, but its pole cast a long, ordinary shadow across the pier.
