## Purpose

The strand map lists "material properties, mixtures and reversible/irreversible changes" as **missing** (strand map §14), and S5 (Matter and materials, 40 source topics in the pinned Marble map) has only the Water Lab's three guided visits (melt, warm, evaporate) and the Cold Window condensation bench (spec 102). Nothing in the garden lets a child change a material, try to change it back, weigh it, or separate a mixture.

The Baker's Window fills that gap with one bench of supplied cases and simplified pictures. A child comes to understand:

- solids keep their shape, liquids take the shape of their container and keep their amount, and gases spread out to fill the space;
- melting and freezing, evaporating and condensing are changes of state, and the material is the same stuff afterwards;
- dissolving is different from melting: the solid is still there, spread through the water in pieces too small to see;
- some changes can be undone (melted chocolate sets again) and some cannot (baked bread will not become dough), and the test is whether a new substance was made, not whether heat was used;
- in a closed jar, the total mass stays the same when something dissolves or melts;
- mixtures can be separated by choosing a method that fits a difference between the parts (sieve, filter, evaporate);
- at older depth, a particle picture is a model that explains these changes.

## The knowledge

Plain adult statements. Each claim is tied to a source in the [audit](../source-audits/145-bakers-window.md) (fact-check pass by search excerpts, 2026-10-25); none remains.

**Materials and objects.** An object (a spoon) is made of a material (wood, metal). This bench names materials but does not teach the distinction; it links to the materials topics and leaves them to another bench. No claim.

**States of matter.** A solid keeps its own shape. A liquid flows, takes the shape of its container and keeps the same amount (volume) when poured into a different container. A gas spreads out to fill the space it is in. Powders such as flour, sugar and salt pour, but each grain is a solid that keeps its shape. Water vapor (the gas) is invisible; the white cloud above a kettle or a hot oven is tiny drops of liquid water made as the vapor cools, and the mist on a cold window is liquid water too. The everyday word "gas" (fuel) is a different meaning.

**Melting and freezing.** Warming can melt a solid into a liquid; cooling can freeze (set) a liquid into a solid. They are the same change in opposite directions. Pure water melts and freezes at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C at normal sea-level air pressure. Higher up, where air pressure is lower, water boils at a slightly lower temperature. Butter and chocolate are mixtures and soften and melt over a range of temperatures, not at one sharp point. Melted chocolate sets again when it cools; if it is not cooled carefully it can look dull or streaky, and it is still chocolate.

**Evaporation and condensation.** Evaporation is liquid becoming invisible gas (vapor). It happens at the surface and at everyday temperatures, below boiling; warmth usually makes it faster. Boiling (older depth) happens when a liquid reaches its boiling point and vapor bubbles form throughout the liquid. Condensation is vapor becoming liquid, often on a cool surface such as a window. The water on a cold window came from the air on that side, and did not pass through the glass. **The drying pages are evaporation:** water leaves the paper as vapor into the air. Paper does not melt; far too much heat would scorch or burn it, which is a different, irreversible change.

**Dissolving.** When sugar or salt dissolves in water, the solid breaks into pieces too small to see and spreads through the water, making a solution. It has not disappeared and has not melted. Melting needs only one substance and warmth; in this bench, dissolving spreads a solid through a liquid. Other substances, including gases, can dissolve too. Stirring and warmer water usually help sugar dissolve faster. Not everything dissolves (sand does not), and there is a limit to how much will dissolve in a given amount of water. About 36 g of salt dissolves in 100 g of water at 20 °C, so the bench's 20 g fully dissolves. **Tasting is never part of any activity.** A dissolved solid is shown to be present by weighing and by evaporating the water to get it back.

**Reversible and irreversible changes.** A change is reversible when the original material can be got back: melting, freezing, evaporating, condensing, dissolving, mixing things that can be separated. A change is irreversible when new substances are made and the original materials cannot easily be got back: baking dough into bread, cooking an egg, toasting, burning. Heat is involved in both kinds; heat is never the test. When yeast in bread dough is kept warm, it makes carbon dioxide gas, and the bubbles make the dough rise. Baking then sets the dough into bread with a new crust, smell and texture; it cannot be made into dough again. Burning makes new substances, including gases and ash; the matter is not destroyed. It appears in this bench only as a picture, never as an action.

**Clues to a new substance** (older depth): a gas made when nothing is boiling, a color change, a new smell, heat or light given off, a new solid, and failing to get the original back. These are clues, not proof. Bubbles from a fizzy drink are gas that was already dissolved in it. A color change can happen without a new substance (a wet page looks darker and dries back to its color).

**Conservation of mass.** In a closed jar, the total mass stays the same when a solid dissolves or melts: 100 g of water and 20 g of salt make 120 g of salt water. In an open container, water can evaporate and the reading goes down; the missing mass went into the air as vapor. Mass adds up; volume need not (salt water takes up slightly less space than the water and salt did apart); this is an older-depth note only.

**Separating mixtures.** Choose the method from a difference between the parts. **Sieve:** separates solids of different sizes (raisins from flour). **Filter:** separates an insoluble solid from a liquid (sand from water); a dissolved solid passes straight through the filter. **Evaporate:** gets a dissolved solid back from a solution (salt from salt water); the water leaves as vapor. Some mixtures need steps in order: sand, salt and water are filtered first, then the salty water is evaporated. Getting salt back by evaporating is the bridge that shows dissolving can be undone.

**Particle picture** (Sapling and Keeper's Desk only, always labeled as a model). Everything is made of particles far too small to see. In a solid they are close together in a regular pattern and vibrate in place; in a liquid they are close but jumbled and slide past one another; in a gas they are far apart and move quickly in all directions, filling the container. The particles themselves do not change size, color or number between states; only their spacing, pattern and movement change. The space between gas particles is empty, not air. On melting, particles stay close and lose their pattern; they do not spread far apart.

## The lesson

Jane's **teach -> guided -> practice** order for each of the four parts:

1. **Teach** one idea with a worked example revealed one step per tap: "A liquid takes its container's shape" (water poured from a jug into a tray), "Dissolving is not melting" (ice cube melts by warmth; sugar dissolves by stirring in water), "Can it be undone?" (chocolate melts and sets; dough bakes and stays bread), "Weigh the whole jar" (100 g + 20 g = 120 g).
2. **Guided:** the first item of each part shows 1-4 visible steps before the choice.
3. **Practice:** the bench itself, then the problem sets.

**"I'm stuck" ladder** on every item: hint -> a worked example of an easier similar case plus how to start this one -> "skip for now", with no penalty and nothing recorded as a miss.

**Contrasts** the bench is built around: melting vs dissolving; evaporating vs boiling (older); reversible vs irreversible; "disappeared" vs "dissolved"; a closed jar vs an open jar on the scale; filtering vs evaporating.

**Top misconceptions designed against** (tagged on choices in the companion): `dissolve-is-melt` (dissolving is melting); `gone-when-invisible` (dissolved or evaporated stuff has disappeared); `mass-lost-dissolving` (the solution weighs less); `heat-means-irreversible` (any change with heat cannot be undone); `melting-irreversible` (melting cannot be undone); `all-reversible` (anything can be undone if you try); `powder-is-liquid` (pouring means liquid); `steam-cloud-is-gas` (the white cloud is the gas); `window-leak` (window water came through the glass); `filter-catches-dissolved` (a filter takes salt out of salt water); `evaporating-destroys` (evaporating destroys the salt); `particles-change` (particles melt, grow or change color); `air-between-particles`; `one-clue-proves` (one clue proves a new substance).

## The game or activity

Every screen shows the line: *These are simplified pictures. Real materials can behave differently.* The bench has no score, timer, lock or mastery label.

### (a) The window bench

Seven case cards sit on the counter; four action spots sit beside the window: **Warm** (the sunny sill), **Cool** (the cold larder), **Stir**, **Bake** (the oven, pictured with its door shut; only the baker uses it). The child drags or taps a case, then an action. Each case accepts two or three actions; any other gets the baker's "Not for this one" line. Results are authored pictures with one sentence, never a simulation.

| Case | Accepts | Authored result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ice cube | Warm, Cool | Warm: melts to a puddle of water. Cool (after melting): freezes to ice again. |
| Butter | Warm, Cool | Warm: softens, then melts. Cool: sets solid again. |
| Chocolate | Warm, Cool | Warm: melts. Cool: sets again, a little duller. |
| Sugar and tea | Stir, Warm | Stir: the grains get smaller and vanish from sight. Warm: they vanish faster. |
| Bread dough | Warm, Bake | Warm: rises, full of tiny bubbles. Bake: becomes a loaf with a brown crust. |
| Egg | Bake, Cool | Bake: the clear part turns white and firm. Cool: stays cooked. |
| Wet page | Warm, Cool | Warm: dries; tiny marks in the air stand for invisible vapor (labeled). Cool: stays damp longer. |

After two cases have been changed, **Sort it** opens: two baskets, **Can be undone** and **Can't be undone**, and the child places each case they have tried with one or two evidence chips (`back-when-cooled`, `same-stuff-new-shape`, `get-it-back-by-evaporating`, `new-smell-color`, `gas-made`, `could-not-change-back`, `still-water-in-the-air`). Feedback is per case and kind. Sugar's correct basket is supported by a link to "Get it back". Dough and egg are "can't be undone"; dough enters the sort only after it has been baked (its card shows the loaf), so the child never has to judge rising alone. The page is "can be undone" (the water changed state; it could be wetted again), with a note that the paper may stay wrinkled. Nothing grades the evidence chips; mismatched chips get a gentle question.

### (b) The scale

A simplified pan scale with a digital display in grams. **Model:** the scale was set to zero with the empty jar and lid on it, so it shows only what is inside; readings are exact whole grams; a real scale can wobble by a gram or so. Steps: jar of 100 g water and a dish of 20 g salt on the pan (120 g); tip the salt in, lid on, stir (still 120 g); the salt is out of sight and the reading is the same. A second case, **the open jar on the sill**, shows a salt-water jar left uncovered for many days: the reading falls from 120 g to 100 g, and the salt stays; the 20 g that left is water vapor. A third case (Keeper) shows 50 g of chocolate melting in a covered bowl: still 50 g. Labeled: *A made-up model with exact numbers.*

### (c) Get it back

Four supplied mixtures in jars: flour and raisins; sand and water; salt water; sand, salt and water (Keeper's Desk). Three tools: **sieve**, **filter** (a funnel with filter paper), **evaporating dish on the sunny sill** (slow; no heat is used). The child chooses a tool or a sequence. A mis-chosen tool visibly fails in an authored picture (salt water through a filter comes out still salty, shown by a drop drying to a white crust on a dish, never by tasting). For the three-part mixture the order is checked: filter first, then evaporate. Labeled: *Simplified pictures. In real life an adult helps with any heating.*

### (d) Particle pictures (Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

Three boxes of identical dots. **Sapling:** match each box to solid, liquid, gas and choose the property it explains (keeps shape, flows, fills the space). **Keeper's Desk:** a blank box and twelve identical dots; arrange "before" and "after" for melting ice and for sugar dissolving in water (two colors of dots, one for each substance), then pick an annotation. The builder checks only that the dot count and size are unchanged and the pattern category (regular / jumbled-close / spread out) fits; the drawing is never scored. Labeled: *A particle picture is a model. Real particles are far too small to see, and there are far more of them.*
