## Purpose

All eight targets are "not yet mapped" in `curriculum/topic-coverage.csv` and named in no spec: In-Depth Character and Setting Analysis (`mt_Eehl12cSnN`), Comparing Characters, Settings and Events (`mt_WRlJ0-hAOG`), How Parts Build a Whole Text (`mt_PetJM-AYz9`), Plot Structure and Character Development (`mt_Hqz5y_tWz2`), Analysing Text Structure (`mt_II6iw4BmJI`), Tracing Theme Across a Text (`mt_wGxq92Na5g`), Critical comparison across texts (`mt_1GF5MeNZPA`) and Using and Evaluating Textual Evidence (`mt_dmFnJzxKwz`). Strand E3 (`docs/six-act-strands.md`) asks for quoting supporting evidence (E3-III), evaluating relevant evidence (E3-IV) and, above all, whole texts, theme development and structure (E3-V).

Built and proposed work reads short texts only: Two Windows (59) combines two small accounts; the Story Loft (36, 37) composes scenes; the build-out's 261 names chapter, scene and stanza on fables and one poem, and 273 reads narrators in short scenes. Nothing reads a whole book: how a character changes over twelve chapters, how an early line sets up a late one, how a writer's chapter order makes a reader wait, how a theme grows, or which line is the strongest evidence for a claim about a book.

A child learns: a character is known by what they say, do and think and how others treat them; a setting's words carry a mood; two characters or places are compared with a detail for each, and one event can cause the next; a character's change shows in how the narrator describes them early and late; early chapters plant questions later ones answer; structure (chapter order, threads taking turns, a repeated image, a run of short paragraphs, a last chapter that moves elsewhere) shapes what a reader feels; a topic is a few words and a theme is what the book says about it, traced through moments; a summary leaves out opinion; strong evidence fits a point closely and a line must be read beside its neighbors; a quotation sits inside a sentence that still reads; a critical comparison asks how two writers carry an idea differently.

## The knowledge

Sourced in the [audit](../source-audits/272-long-story.md); unconfirmed claims were cut or marked `[check]` there and kept out of child text.

**The books and their status.** Kenneth Grahame (1859-1932), *The Wind in the Willows*, 1908, twelve chapters (Project Gutenberg #289). Frances Hodgson Burnett (1849-1924), *The Secret Garden*, serialized in *The American Magazine* from November 1910 to August 1911 and published as a book in 1911, twenty-seven chapters (Project Gutenberg #113). Both were published before 1931, so they are public domain in the US, and both authors died more than seventy years ago, so they are public domain in the UK and EU.

**Character and setting in depth (RL.4.3).** A character is shown through words, actions, thoughts and how others respond. In chapter 1, the Mole flings down his brush and says "Hang spring-cleaning!", knocks over a rabbit as "the impatient and contemptuous Mole", is bewitched by his first river, grows jealous of the Rat's rowing ("his pride began to whisper"), grabs the sculls and upsets the boat, and is pulled out by the Rat, who is laughing. A setting's words carry mood: the river is "chasing and chuckling"; in chapter 3 the Wild Wood goes "very still" and the light drains "like flood-water". In *The Secret Garden* chapter 9, Mary finds the garden still and says she is "the first person who has spoken in here for ten years."

**Comparing (RL.5.3).** Mole has never been in a boat; the Rat says boating is "the only thing". In chapter 18 Colin says he "can't bear people"; a few lines later Mary says Ben Weatherstaff thinks she has his "nasty tempers", that Colin is like him too, and that she no longer feels "as sour as I used to". In chapter 5, Mole's loyalty causes him to follow the Rat away from his home, and the sob that follows makes the Rat turn back.

**How parts build a whole (RL.5.5) and plot and change (RL.6-8.3).** Early lines plant later chapters: the Rat's "We don't go there" (ch. 1) before Mole goes into the Wild Wood alone (ch. 3); the Rat's "I'm never going" to the Wide World (ch. 1) before he nearly leaves for the South and Mole stops him (ch. 9); Toad's "O poop-poop!" (ch. 2) before he takes a car and is imprisoned (ch. 6); in *The Secret Garden*, Martha's "It's th' wind" (ch. 5) before the crying leads Mary to Colin (ch. 13). The narrator's words for Mole move from "contemptuous" and "triumphant" (ch. 1) to "tactful" (ch. 9). The narrator says the moor air helped Mary feel "less 'contrary'" (ch. 8): setting shapes character.

**Structure (RL.6-8.5, KS3).** From chapter 6 to chapter 10 Toad's chapters and Mole and Rat's take turns: chapter 6 ends with Toad locked in prison, chapter 7 leaves him there, chapter 8 returns to him. The threads meet at the end of chapter 10, when Toad, swept down the river, sees a face in a hole in the bank, "Brown and small, with whiskers", which echoes the Rat's first appearance in chapter 1 ("A brown little face, with whiskers"). In chapter 3 three one-line paragraphs ("Then the faces began." and so on) build fear. *The Secret Garden*'s last chapter leaves the garden for Mr. Craven abroad ("While the secret garden was coming alive and two children were coming alive with it") and ends with the household watching him come home across the lawn.

**Theme and summary (RL.6-8.2).** A topic (home) differs from a theme (home can pull you back after adventures). Home recurs: Mole bolts from his (ch. 1), it calls him back (ch. 5), the Rat nearly leaves his (ch. 9), the friends win Toad Hall back (ch. 12). *The Secret Garden*'s narrator states its idea in a rhyme: "Where you tend a rose, my lad, / A thistle cannot grow." These are offered as one well-supported reading, never "the" theme. An objective summary gives events in order without judgment.

**Evidence (RL.6-8.1).** RL.8.1 asks for the evidence that "most strongly supports" an analysis. In chapter 12 Toad promises to be "a very different Toad", then sings a song about himself twice behind a locked door, then gives modest answers to his guests while feeling he is "an object of absorbing interest to every one", refuses to sing at the banquet, and takes "the greatest satisfaction" in Badger's and Rat's astonishment; only then does the narrator say "He was indeed an altered Toad!" Lines on both sides exist, so the pack asks for the strongest line for a stated point and for reading a line beside its neighbors, and never keys whether Toad changed. A short quotation is embedded in a sentence that still reads, in US double quotation marks.

**Critical comparison (KS3 "making critical comparisons across texts").** Both books set a character against a new place (Mole and the river; Mary and the moor). Burnett states her idea outright as a saying; Grahame shows his through what Mole feels. Both books are from 1908-1911 and carry their period; Burnett's colonial attitudes are named in the grown-up panel only.

## The game or activity

Every screen: *You're reading short pieces of long books. Each card says where in the book it comes from.* No score, timer, lock or mastery label. Drags have tap equivalents. Every task shows its own book card, with read-aloud, so it can be done at any reading length.

- **(a) Close Look (Seedling).** Sort four Mole lines into *Says*, *Does*, *Thinks*, *How others treat him*; tap the words that make the river alive; tap the garden's repeated word.
- **(b) Side by Side (Seedling, Sapling).** Two-sided trays with a *Both* middle: Mole and Rat; river and Wild Wood; Mary and Colin in chapter 18.
- **(c) The Chapter Map (Sapling).** Twelve chapter cards in a row. **Join the string** from four early lines to the chapters they set up; place Mole's three narrator words; one *Secret Garden* join (chapter 5 to 13).
- **(d) Two Threads (Keeper's Desk).** Tag chapters 6-10 as *Toad* or *Mole and Rat*; tap the repeated words in the whiskered face; the three "Then" paragraphs; the last chapter of *The Secret Garden*.
- **(e) The Thread of a Theme (Sapling, Keeper's Desk).** Order four home moments; three "coming alive" moments; pick the opinion-free summary.
- **(f) Weigh the Evidence (Keeper's Desk).** Sort Toad's five lines into *Shows a change*, *Shows he still loves attention*, *Could be read either way*; three lines accept two trays, because the book supports both; no ruling; then Burnett's rhyme beside Grahame's "Home!".
- **(g) A Guide for the Next Keeper (Act V; Sapling, Keeper's Desk).** Choose a book and a thread (Mole's change, Toad, home; Mary's change, coming alive); pick three book cards (beginning, middle, end) and keep them as a first version; mark each strong or weak and swap at most one; choose a limit card; write or tell the guide off-screen.
