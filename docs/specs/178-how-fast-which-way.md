## The knowledge

**Motion words.** *Distance*: how far. *Speed*: how far in each unit of time (km per hour, meters per minute). *Direction*: which way. *Stationary*: not moving relative to the thing you compare with. *Steady speed*: the same distance in each equal time. *Speeding up* (accelerating) and *slowing down* (decelerating) name a **change** in speed. A fast steady cart is not speeding up; a slow cart gathering speed is.

**Testing a design.** Set a goal you can see (stop in the basket ring). Predict, try, mark where it stopped, and judge from the mark, never from hopes or fun. If it missed, change **one** thing (height or the turning board). A miss is something to improve.

**Patterns predict.** Repeat each test, record a table, look for the trend ("each block higher, about 30 cm farther"), then predict an untested case before trying it. A pattern gives "about", not an exact number, and holds only near the cases tested. Pattern-seeking is real science beside the fair test (spec 155). The model's straight line is fair physics for one surface (roll distance grows with drop height when losses are small); real carts lose some speed on the ramp.

**Distance-time graphs.** Read the axes first: time across, distance from a stated start up. The line is a record. **Flat**: distance not changing, so stopped. **Steeper**: more distance per unit of time, so faster. **Sloping down**: distance from the start shrinking, so coming back. A steep uphill path can draw a flatter line. Speed comes from the slope, never from a point's height. A line never goes straight up or back in time.

**Speed in numbers.** Start with distance = speed × time ("6 km/h means 6 km in each hour"), shown on a double number line, then speed = distance ÷ time for one straight part of the graph. **Average speed** = total distance ÷ total time, including stops. It is not the mean of the separate speeds. The pack's courier trip (made-up data): 6 km in the first hour (6 km/h), stopped an hour at the high ford, then 6 km in 3 hours up the steep path (2 km/h): 12 km in 5 hours, 2.4 km/h on average (not 3, which forgets the stop, and not 4, the mean of 6 and 2).

**Relative motion.** Every motion answers "compared with what?" Pip sitting on a barge drifting at 2 km/h is still compared with the barge and moving at 2 km/h compared with the bank. Neither is the "real" one; the frame is the observer's viewpoint, not the scenery. **Same direction: subtract** (two boats side by side at 4 km/h are still to each other). **Opposite directions: add** (boats approaching at 4 and 5 km/h close at 9 km/h). **One thing moving on another: add going with, subtract going against** (Pip walks forward at 3 km/h on a barge moving at 5: 8 km/h compared with the bank). A boat rowing 6 km/h through water in a 2 km/h current makes 8 km/h downstream and 4 km/h upstream compared with the bank; a 12 km trip takes 1.5 hours down and 3 hours back, so the round trip averages 24 ÷ 4.5 ≈ 5.3 km/h, not 6.

**Resultant forces (one line only).** Same way: add. Opposite ways: take the smaller from the bigger; the resultant points toward the bigger. The resultant is the **one force that does the same job as all of them**: it replaces them, never joins them as an extra arrow. Zero resultant: no change in motion, so a still thing stays still **and** a moving thing keeps a steady speed in a straight line (a towed barge with 250 N forward and 250 N back glides steadily). A resultant points the way the motion **changes**, which need not be the way it is going: a barge moving upstream with a slack rope and 200 N of water push back is slowing down. A bigger resultant changes motion faster; it does not set a speed. Newtons (N) from Sapling up; "squares" at Seedling, as in spec 154. Angled forces only previewed (Basel card).

**Real anchors (see audit).**
- **Galileo's ship.** In his *Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems* (Florence, 1632), Galileo has the character Salviati describe a closed cabin below deck on a large ship, with flies, butterflies, a bowl of fish and a dripping bottle. While the ship moves smoothly at a steady speed, nothing in the cabin behaves differently from when it is at rest, so nobody inside can tell whether the ship is moving. Salviati asks the reader to picture (or try) it; the book reports no trial on a real ship.
- **The chip log and the knot.** Sailors threw a wooden board on a knotted line off the stern and counted the knots that ran out while a small sandglass emptied. The first known printed description of the log and line is William Bourne's *A Regiment for the Sea* (1574); "knot" as a speed is recorded in English from the 1630s. Later accounts give 47 ft 3 in (about 14.4 m) between knots and a 28-second glass, chosen so the ratio matches one nautical mile to one hour; older accounts used 30 seconds. The count was the speed in **knots**, nautical miles per hour. Since 1929 the international nautical mile is exactly 1852 m, so 1 knot = 1.852 km/h. The log measured speed **through the water**, not over the ground: a relative-motion lesson in itself.
- **The Basel ferries.** Four ferries (Wilde Maa, Leu, Vogel Gryff, Ueli) still cross the Rhine in Basel with no motor or oars: each hangs from a cable over the river, the ferry keeper angles it with the rudder, and the current pushes it across. The first ferry link dates from the mid-19th century (1854 per Basel's tourism office; one account says 1847), so child text gives no year. Keeper's Desk only: two angled pushes combining.

**Contrasts:** fast vs speeding up; hoped vs marked; one roll vs three; the path's hill vs the line's slope; high vs steep; mean of speeds vs total ÷ total; bank vs deck; with vs against; extra vs replacing arrow; motion vs change.

**Top misconceptions designed against:** speed and speeding up are the same; "it worked because I wanted it to"; change everything at once; one reading is enough; predictions must be exact; the graph is a picture of the path; higher means faster; an axis value is the speed; average = mean of speeds; forgetting the stop; motion belongs to the object; the ground is the true frame; the resultant is one more force; zero resultant means stopped; the resultant points the way it is going.

## The game or activity

**(a) Letter-Cart Ramp, model `cart-ramp-v1`.** Dot's letter cart rolls off a 1-5 block ramp onto a mat. *A made-up model: each block adds 30 cm of roll; the three tries per height are authored.* **Design mode** (Sprout): a basket ring at 60 or 90 cm, or off to the side; set height and the turning board, tap **Predict**, **Roll**; a marker stays where it stopped, and the child judges by pointing at it. **Pattern mode** (Seedling): heights 1-4, three tries each, fill a table and dot chart; the 5-block result stays hidden until a prediction band is placed. Tries: 1: 31, 29, 30; 2: 58, 62, 60; 3: 91, 88, 91; 4: 118, 122, 120; 5: 148, 153, 149 cm.

**(b) Word Wall.** Eight term cards (distance, speed, direction, stationary, steady speed, speeding up, slowing down, resultant), each turning over to a meaning, an example and a non-example. The child sorts scene tiles into *shows it* / *doesn't*. Terms become tappable glossary words on every bench.

**(c) Trip Line.** A strip map above a distance-time graph. **Play** moves the courier and the line draws itself. Tapping a line part highlights its stretch of map and sentence. **Speed lens** (Sapling) shows rise, run and the division. **Draw the story**: snap line-part tiles (out fast, out slow, stopped, back) to match a story card. Data: Trip A (courier, hours, km): (0, 0), (1, 6), (2, 6), (5, 12). Trip B (Léon's ferry, minutes, meters from the Les Saules landing): (0, 0), (4, 120), (6, 120), (10, 0), (12, 0). *Made-up journeys, not real travel times.*

**(d) Ferry Deck.** One river scene, three viewpoints: **bank**, **barge**, **other boat**. The same moment redraws with the viewer fixed in the center; each moving thing shows a speed "compared with you". A number-line strip adds or subtracts speeds (Sapling). *A simplified picture: one straight river, steady speeds, no wind.*

**(e) Tow Line.** A barge on the towpath with arrows for rope, water push and helpers. **Combine** fades them and draws the single resultant in their place; the child predicts *speeds up*, *keeps steady* or *slows down*, then a gauge shows the change. Cases include zero resultant while moving and a slack rope upstream. Keeper's Desk adds the Basel card with a drawn, not calculated, resultant. *A simplified picture: forces along one line, steady water push, arrow lengths to scale.*

No timers, scores, streaks, medals or locks. Every bench is open from the first visit.
