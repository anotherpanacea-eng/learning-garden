## The knowledge

Sourced in the audit. Willowbank, the quarry and the drawer are fiction.

**How paleontologists work.** The work runs in an order, and each step protects information: (1) *prospect*, walking exposed rock, often where erosion has just uncovered bone; (2) *record*, mapping and photographing where each piece lies and in which layer before anything moves; (3) *excavate*, with heavy tools for rock far from the bone and fine tools (brushes, awls) near it; (4) *jacket*: trench round the bone, a paper layer, then burlap strips soaked in plaster; undercut, flip, cap; (5) *transport*, sometimes by helicopter; (6) *prepare* in the lab, cleaning rock away slowly; (7) *study and publish*, so others can check. Once a fossil is lifted, its exact position cannot be recovered, so the record comes first. It is teamwork: prospectors, preparators, curators, researchers.

**A find that waited.** In 1954 Walter Georg Kühne found a large titanosaur skeleton near Orcau in the Tremp Basin (Catalonia, southern Pyrenees). Partly dug out, it was forgotten for more than sixty years, then excavated, studied and named *Abditosaurus kuehnei* in 2022 ("forgotten reptile"). It is the most complete titanosaur skeleton yet found in Europe. Its closest relatives were South American and African titanosaurs; its ancestors probably reached Europe's Ibero-Armorican island from Africa about 70.6 million years ago, when seas were lower. No size figures are taught.

**Fossilized dung (coprolites).** A coprolite is fossilized dung, a trace fossil (it records eating). Hard, less-digested bits survive: bone, scales, plant fibers, wood. Teeth show what an animal *could* eat; dung contents show what it *did* eat, at least in that meal. Which animal left a particular dropping is often uncertain; scientists reason from size, age, place and contents.
- **Mary Anning** noticed by 1824 that "bezoar stones" at Lyme Regis often lay in the belly region of ichthyosaur skeletons and, broken open, held fish bones and scales. This led William Buckland in 1829 to name them coprolites, citing her (142 tells her wider life).
- **Meat:** a coprolite about 44 cm long from Saskatchewan (Chin and colleagues, *Nature* 1998) is roughly 30 to 50 percent broken bone of a young dinosaur; size, age and contents point to *Tyrannosaurus*.
- **Plants:** in Montana's Two Medicine Formation (about 74 to 80 million years old), coprolites up to about 7 liters are mostly rotted conifer wood, identified partly by dung beetle burrows; nearby bones and eggshell point to the hadrosaur *Maiasaura* (Chin 2007). A 2017 Utah study found similar coprolites with crustacean shell bits.

**Dinosaur sizes.** Dinosaurs ranged hugely. *Compsognathus* was about 70 cm to 1 m long including its tail and weighed about 2.5 to 3 kg, roughly hen to turkey size. The largest sauropods, such as *Argentinosaurus*, are estimated at about 30 to 35 m long, among the largest land animals ever; estimates vary because skeletons are incomplete. The smallest living dinosaur is a bird: the bee hummingbird, about 6 cm long and about 2 grams. Cards compare to a hen, Pip and a made-up 3 m hay cart, never to the child.

**Dinosaurs around the world.** Dinosaur fossils have been found on all seven continents. Antarctica has the fewest because ice covers most rock, so searchers work only on exposed outcrops; fewer finds does not mean fewer dinosaurs. *Cryolophosaurus*, a crested meat-eater, came from Mount Kirkpatrick in 1990-91, in early Jurassic rock about 196 to 188 million years old (named 1994). In the Triassic the continents formed one landmass, Pangaea; it began to split about 200 million years ago, near the start of the Jurassic. Related animals and plants on continents now separated by ocean are evidence the land was once joined; the classic cases are the plant *Glossopteris* and *Lystrosaurus* (Africa, India, Antarctica), which was not a dinosaur. After the split, dinosaurs on separate continents changed separately (USGS). Map pins: *Tyrannosaurus* (North America), *Argentinosaurus* (South America), *Abditosaurus* (Europe), *Microraptor* (Asia), *Cryolophosaurus* (Antarctica), *Spinosaurus* (North Africa), *Australovenator* (Australia).

**Layers record change** (the Space Systems topic). In undisturbed layers the lowest formed first (139 owns the rule and its fold exception). Fossils in different layers lived at different times, so layer order gives the order of life and landscapes: shells under leaves means sea, then land. Relative order only; the Life Rope adds approximate years at Sapling.

**Life changed over time.** Older rocks hold different, often extinct life; younger rocks, forms more like today's. Anchors: oldest widely accepted fossils (stromatolites, mats built by microbes) about 3.5 billion years ago; first land plants (tiny spores) roughly 470 million years ago; first dinosaurs by about 233 million years ago, perhaps earlier; non-bird dinosaurs gone 66 million years ago; our species about 300,000 years ago. **Horses:** *Hyracotherium* (older name "Eohippus"), about 55 to 45 million years ago, was small, with four toes on each front foot and three on each hind foot; modern horses stand on one toe. The record is a branching bush, not a line. Change happens across generations in populations, never to one animal (mechanism stays with 161); no "more advanced" language.

**Dinosaur to bird** (Keeper's Desk, Sapling preview). *Anchiornis*, a feathered dinosaur from China about 160 to 155 million years ago, is older than *Archaeopteryx* (about 150 million years ago, Solnhofen, Bavaria; first skeleton found 1861), which has teeth, clawed fingers and a long bony tail with feathered wings. *Sinosauropteryx* (described 1996) had simple filaments and could not fly. *Microraptor* (China, Early Cretaceous, about 120 to 125 million years ago) had long flight feathers on arms and legs. Feathers came before flight, probably first for warmth and display. Whether flight began "ground up" (running, leaping) or "trees down" (gliding) is still argued; the pack picks no winner.

**Mass extinctions, calmly** (Keeper's Desk). A mass extinction is a loss of a large share of species in a geologically short time, unlike ordinary background extinction. Five are usually named: end-Ordovician about 445 million years ago (linked to glaciation and falling seas; details debated); Late Devonian about 372 (cause still debated); end-Permian about 252 (the most severe: probably more than 80 percent of sea species; linked to huge Siberian Traps eruptions); end-Triassic about 201 (linked to the Central Atlantic eruptions); K-Pg 66 (an asteroid impact, taught in 142). Recovery took millions of years (at least five after the end-Permian), and new kinds spread into the emptied space: after the K-Pg, mammals and birds diversified. Child text: "many kinds died out over a short span of deep time"; no peril, nothing about the future.

### Dig Square (model `dig-v1`)

A 4 × 4 grid over a made-up bank at the old quarry; a bone edge shows in B2. Buttons: **Look** (tap squares), **Record** (writes square, layer and direction into a notebook line), **Dig** with a tray tool (pick, trowel, brush, awl), **Jacket** (order three cards), **Carry**, **Clean**, **Label**. A pick beside bone draws a hairline crack with a tool note; lifting before Record leaves *Where was it lying? Not written down.* plus a card that real fossils cannot be put back. **Start again** resets the model only. No right/wrong mark; feedback names the step.

### Slice Table (model `slice-v1`)

Three made-up coprolites (C1 bone bits, C2 wood fibers, C3 fish scales). Tap **Slice**, then **Magnify**; contents are drawn and named in text. The explorer picks *meat-eater*, *plant-eater* or *fish-eater*, answers "Do we know exactly who made it?", and sees a tooth card beside the slice (*could eat* / *did eat*). Real-case cards are labeled "A true thing from science."

### Pack cards

**Size Line:** silhouettes on one scale beside a hen, Pip and the made-up 3 m cart; tap to order. **Finds Map:** seven pins (list view at 320 px), Antarctica under ice; a **Joined land** toggle (*a simplified picture*). **Quarry Wall:** a made-up five-layer column (bottom to top: shells, fish, ferns, footprints, leaves and a small mammal tooth), with a line where several kinds stop. **Life Rope:** a made-up 35 m rope, 1 m for every 100 million years, from the mill to the bridge; Sapling cards pinned at real approximate distances (oldest fossils 35 m, K-Pg 0.66 m, our species 3 mm); it links to 133's lens. **Feather Board** (Keeper's Desk): four specimen cards on a time strip.
