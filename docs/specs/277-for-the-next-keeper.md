## The knowledge

Sourced in the [audit](../source-audits/277-for-the-next-keeper.md); unconfirmed claims were cut or marked `[check]` there and kept out of child text.

**Paragraphs and headings (NC Years 3-4; Appendix 2 Years 3-4).** A paragraph groups related material around one theme. In narrative a new paragraph conventionally marks a change of time, place, topic or speaker; in dialogue each new speaker conventionally starts a new paragraph (a widely taught layout custom, not a grammar rule; the child text says so). In non-narrative writing, headings and sub-headings are simple organisational devices that help a reader find a section. Time words at each paragraph's start (at dawn, by noon, that evening) fix a recount's order.

**Linking paragraphs (Appendix 2 Year 5).** Ideas are linked across paragraphs with adverbials of time (later, meanwhile), place (nearby, beyond the bridge) and number (secondly, finally). *Meanwhile* can mean at the same time as what came before (its other sense is "during the time between"); *later* means after. The choice is decided by what the next paragraph changes, which every item states.

**Cohesion (Appendix 2 Years 4 and 6; DfE glossary).** A text has cohesion when it is clear how the meanings of its parts fit together. Devices include repeated words and synonyms, determiners and pronouns that refer back (the glossary's own example: "the football" refers back to "a football"), adverbials that make relations clear (on the other hand, in contrast, as a consequence), and ellipsis, the omission of an expected, predictable word or phrase ("Dot did too"). A pronoun with two possible referents is ambiguous; using the noun fixes it.

**Revising and editing (NC Years 3-6; KS3; CCSS W.4.5, W.6-8.5).** Revising changes content, order or wording so writing works for its audience and purpose; editing and proofreading correct spelling, punctuation and grammar. The national curriculum asks pupils to assess the effectiveness of their own and others' writing, propose changes to grammar and vocabulary, and proof-read; KS3 asks them to consider how writing reflects its audience and purpose and amend vocabulary, grammar and structure for coherence. Research syntheses (Graham and Perin 2007, *Writing Next*; the EEF's Key Stage 2 literacy guidance) find that explicitly taught planning, revising and editing strategies, modelled and then practised with support, improve writing. Many word choices have no single right answer; this pack keys only what the stated reader decides.

**Short research (CCSS W.4.7, W.4.8, W.5.7).** Short projects build knowledge about a topic from print and digital sources with brief, organised notes. A useful question can be answered by the sources at hand. A note keeps a fact in a few words; sorted under headings, notes become paragraphs. The frog facts are the ones already audited for pack 161: spawn in late winter or spring, later where it is colder and higher; back legs before front legs; the tail absorbed before the froglet leaves the water.

**Evidence from a story (CCSS W.4.9, W.5.9).** A point about a literary text is supported by quoting its exact words, in quotation marks, and explaining how they show the point (often taught as point, evidence, explain). A line can be in the text and still not show the point. Comparing two texts on one idea needs a line from each.

**Form (KS3 writing; CCSS W.6-8.4).** KS3 lists stories, scripts, poetry, notes and polished scripts for talks, arguments, and personal and formal letters, and asks pupils to select the appropriate form. A script puts the speaker's name first and actions as stage directions, conventionally in brackets or italics; this pack teaches brackets.

**Techniques for effect (KS3 writing).** KS3 asks pupils to draw on literary and rhetorical devices from their reading to enhance the impact of their writing. A rhetorical question affirms or denies a point by asking it; anaphora repeats a word or words at the start of successive clauses or lines; a tricolon is a series of three parallel elements; contrast sets one thing against its opposite. Which device suits a piece is often taste once the purpose is met.

## The game or activity

Every screen: *These drafts were written for this table. Your own writing stays on paper, or you say it aloud.* No score, timer, lock or mastery label. Drags have tap equivalents. Every task shows its own draft or card, with read-aloud, so it can be done at any reading length.

- **(a) Paragraph Sort (Seedling).** Nine strips of Pip's "Visit the Bakery" into three headed trays; order Dot's three market-day paragraphs by their opening time words; **Mark the break** in three blocks (a time and place change, a new speaker, a topic change in a report).
- **(b) The Linking Word (Sapling).** Four joins, each with a stated "What changes" line: same time elsewhere, a new place, the second reason, the last reason.
- **(c) Holding It Together (Sapling, Keeper's Desk).** Tap every word that means the parcel (parcel, package, it, bundle); fix an ambiguous "it"; join paragraphs by meaning (contrast, consequence, addition); **Leave it out**, two ellipsis pairs.
- **(d) The Red Pencil (Seedling, Sapling, Keeper's Desk).** Pip's visitors' notice with a reader card: cut one sentence, replace "the thing", fix "bred"; sort four changes into revising or editing; sort four changes into **needed** or **either works**; at Keeper's Desk, choose a plan for a stated reader.
- **(e) The Research Corner (Seedling, Sapling).** Three source cards (a library frog book, the miller's pond notes, Pip's unchecked guess); pick the question they answer; tap the sources that help; sort four notes under two headings; choose the short note; tell or write two paragraphs off-screen.
- **(f) Point and Quote (Sapling, Keeper's Desk).** Two fable cards quoted exactly from 261 and three book cards from 272; **Find the line** for a stated point; **Build the paragraph** in point, quote, explain order; **Two stories, one point** with a line from each.
- **(g) Form and Effect (Keeper's Desk).** Match four jobs to four forms; turn a story line into a script line; tap the repeated opening, a rhetorical question and the group of three in Bramble's advert; try one technique on paper; an **Either works** card.
- **Your Page (Act V; Sapling, Keeper's Desk).** Pick a form, a reader and two or three heading cards; write on paper; keep the first plan; read to a grown-up and tick a four-line reader checklist; make one change and keep the new plan beside the first; pick a limit card, or *A different limit, told to a grown-up* (stored only as its code, `told`), so no one has to choose a limit that isn't true.
