## Purpose

Medieval Times has 25 topics in the pinned Marble catalog. Specs 137 (*Mills, Bridges and Pilgrims*: manor, mill, Domesday Book, pilgrims, towns, guilds, what a castle was for) and 138 (*Keepers of Books*: Timbuktu, Baghdad, beyond Europe) place eight. Neither covers the Vikings, castle design, clothing or 1066, which is where children meet the strongest myths: horned helmets, one unchanging castle shape, everyone in bright silk, and the Bayeux Tapestry as a neutral photograph.

Four readings, each built around one reasoning habit:

1. **Longships** (evidence over myth): Vikings as traders, settlers and raiders; shallow hulls; Dublin and York; silver from the Islamic world; L'Anse aux Meadows and its tree-ring date of 1021; horned helmets made famous by 1876 opera costumes.
2. **Castles through time** (design as problem-solving): motte and bailey, stone keeps, Rochester's fallen corner and round rebuild, gatehouses, concentric Beaumaris, Loarre on its Pyrenean rock.
3. **What people wore** (objects as signals): wool and linen, plant and insect dyes, rich and poor, buttons, the 1363 English clothing law.
4. **1066 and the long embroidery** (who made the source): Alfred and the Danelaw in brief, the three claimants, the year's dates, and the embroidery ordered by one side, with Halley's comet linking to the Archive's Paris 1910.

At the Keeper's Desk only, an opt-in folder gives the Black Death in five factual cards.

A child comes to understand that Vikings did more than raid and the horns are a later costume; that castles changed because builders solved problems one at a time; that clothes signaled who you were and color cost money; and that a beautiful source can tell one side's story. It practices division with remainders, year and day differences, ordering and one average (strands M and H1).

**What it does not repeat.** The manor, mill, Domesday Book, pilgrims, markets, guilds and the "pyramid" stay in spec 137; this pack revisits 137's castle-purpose card (as 2.1) and relies on its pyramid card. Baghdad's scholars stay in spec 138. Knights, armor and crusades stay out.

## The knowledge

Checked on 2026-10-25 in two passes (28 web searches; page fetches were blocked). No `[check]` markers remain; sources, secondary supports and limits are in the audit.

**Vikings.** Scandinavians (today's Norway, Denmark, Sweden) who raided, traded, farmed and settled overseas; the Viking Age is usually dated about 790 (Lindisfarne, 793) to 1066 (Stamford Bridge). Dublin grew from a longphort of 841; York (Jorvik) fell to a Danish army in November 866 and became a trading town.

**Longships.** Long, narrow, light, with oars and sail, shallow enough for rivers and beaches. The Viking Ship Museum gives Skuldelev 2 as about 30 m long, about 1 m draft, 60 oars, crew 65 to 70, built near Dublin about 1042 (tree rings).

**Silver.** The Vale of York hoard (found 2007, buried about 927 to 928): 617 silver coins and 65 other objects in a gilt-silver cup, with coins from as far as Afghanistan and Iraq, Russia, Scandinavia and North Africa. The pack says silver "passed on through trade," never that one Viking carried a coin from Baghdad.

**L'Anse aux Meadows.** A 2021 *Nature* study found the 993 radiocarbon spike in wood with metal-tool cuts; three pieces were felled 28 rings later, in 1021. That shows presence in 1021, not first arrival; occupation was brief (estimates three to 13 years). Two Icelandic sagas, written down about 1220 to 1280, tell of voyages to Vinland and Leif Erikson; the site is not proven to be his. UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1978.

**Horned helmets.** Romantic painters drew horned Vikings from the 1820s; Carl Emil Doepler's 1876 costumes for Wagner's *Ring* made the image famous, so the pack says the costumes "made the horns famous," never that they invented them. The two near-complete Viking-age helmets, Gjermundbu (Norway, found 1943) and Yarm (England, found in the 1950s, dated to the 900s by a 2020 Durham study), have no horns; Danish horned helmets (Viksø) are Bronze Age. Sources are mostly popular history, so the card says "the helmets that have been found."

**Castle design.** Motte (mound with wooden tower), bailey (fenced yard) and ditch, raised quickly by the Normans after 1066 and often rebuilt in stone. The White Tower was begun in the 1070s. At Rochester in 1215 King John's miners dug under the keep's southeast corner, burned the props with the fat of forty pigs (writ of 25 November) and brought it down; it was rebuilt rounded (English Heritage). Beaumaris (begun 1295, Edward I, never finished) is the textbook concentric castle: a higher inner ring inside a lower outer one, so defenders could use both at once. Loarre (Huesca, Aragon), in the Pyrenean foothills on a limestone outcrop, is among Spain's oldest castles and its most important Romanesque fortress (Turespaña); much dates from the 1000s (begun about 1020), with an outer wall and towers added in the later 1200s, more than two hundred years on. Parts and rooms (keep, curtain wall, gatehouse, moat, great hall, kitchen, chapel, garderobe) follow heritage glossaries and English Heritage site plans. The portcullis, drawbridge and later artillery forts were cut as unconfirmed in this pass.

**Sieges, as design only.** A siege cut a castle off; it could last weeks or months (Rochester about two months). Castles relied on wells inside the walls and food stocked in advance. Undermining is the one attack method named, because it explains a design change. No siege engines, boiling liquids or injuries.

**Clothing and dyes.** Wool and linen for most; woad (blue), madder (red), weld (yellow); kermes, from insects, the costliest red; scarlet a costly dyed wool cloth; working clothes often undyed (secondary sources). Buttons with buttonholes spread in the 1200s. The 1363 Statute concerning Diet and Apparel set what cloth each rank could wear, prompted by lower ranks dressing like the elite.

**1066.** Edward the Confessor died on 5 January (one source: the night of the 4th) with no children; Harold Godwinson was crowned; Harald Hardrada was defeated at Stamford Bridge on 25 September; William landed at Pevensey on 28 September, won at Hastings on 14 October and was crowned on Christmas Day. Context: Alfred beat Guthrum at Edington in 878 and England was divided, with the Danelaw in the north and east (Britannica); in 927 Athelstan took York and is often called the first king of England (a modern label).

**The Bayeux Tapestry.** Wool embroidery on linen, 68.38 m long, about 50 cm tall (it varies, 46 to 54 cm). Maker unknown; probably ordered by Bishop Odo, William's half-brother, and sewn in England. It tells the Norman story: Harold's oath on relics is set at Bayeux, where William of Poitiers puts it at Bonneville; its content as a promise to William rests on Norman writers, and English accounts differ. It shows Halley's comet; recorded returns after 1066 run 1145 to 1910 (eleven), about every 75 to 76 years. It has been lent to the British Museum for an exhibition; card 4.11 says only that.

**Black Death (Keeper's Desk only).** 1347 to 1351; plague caused by *Yersinia pestis*; fleas from rodents to people, and person to person; lice possibly too. Deaths estimated at a third of Europe's people or more; the pack states that estimates differ. People then blamed bad air (miasma) or God's punishment. With fewer workers, wages rose; England's Statute of Labourers (1351) tried to hold them at old rates and largely failed.

## The lesson

Each reading follows Jane's order: **teach** (one idea per card, 30 words or fewer, worked examples revealed step by step), **guided** (1 to 4 visible steps), then **practice**.

- **Reading 1:** cards 1.1 to 1.11; worked examples on oars per side (8 ÷ 2) and tree rings (993 + 7); guided item: a made-up log 10 rings after 993 (1003).
- **Reading 2:** cards 2.1 to 2.12; guided item: 1215 − 1066 = 149 years.
- **Reading 3:** cards 3.1 to 3.7; worked example: yellow then blue makes green.
- **Reading 4:** cards 4.1 to 4.11; guided item: order three events by month.
- **Keeper's Desk folder:** cards K.1 to K.5, behind a folder card that says what it is about and that opening it is a choice.

**"I'm stuck" ladder** on every graded item: (1) the item's hint; (2) an easier worked case plus how to start this one (8 ÷ 2 oars; 1010 − 1000; 1100 before 1300; "who made Pip's poster?"; "why put a lid on a pot?"); (3) **Skip for now**, with no mark against anything. Support, familiarity and independent attempts are recorded separately.

**Contrasts.** Costume / find (Doepler against Gjermundbu). Presence / first arrival (1021). Found / made (the hoard's coins). Problem fix / fashion (Rochester). One side's account / what happened (the oath). A clothing law / what people wore. A cause known now / the best explanation then (germs and bad air).

**Top misconceptions designed against.**
1. Vikings wore horned helmets (A5, Scene 1).
2. Vikings only raided (A7 and cards 1.1, 1.5 to 1.8).
3. One found object proves one person's journey; where it was found is where it was made (A3).
4. A date for presence is a date for first arrival (A6), and its underclaiming opposite, that tree rings can't give an exact year.
5. Castle changes were fashion, or newer means easier (B3, B6, B7).
6. Motte and bailey mixed up (B1).
7. Everyone in the Middle Ages wore bright silk and fur (C2); medieval people had no buttons (C4).
8. A tapestry is woven (D1); a picture is neutral (D4); one side's account is fact, or a biased source is useless (D5).
9. People in the past were foolish for not knowing what we know (E2).

## The game or activity

1. **Readings:** a card carousel per reading, read-aloud on every card, a **Details** fold, and one fixed-position label (*A true thing from history* / *A story from Willowbank* / *A simplified picture* / *Made up for this job*).
2. **Row up the river (`river`):** five made-up places (depths 6, 4, 2.5, 1.5, 0.8 m); tap a boat, then a place. **Model:** a boat floats where the water depth, in meters, exceeds its draft. Longship 1 m (Skuldelev 2); "deep ship" 3 m, made up. Model line: real rivers have rocks, currents and tides, and crews dragged boats past shallows. Activity outside the item count; no safety advice drawn from it.
3. **Castle timeline (`timeline`):** four dated cards on a 1050 to 1350 line (item B4), then five problem-to-fix pairs, each opening its Reading 2 card. A finished timeline can be "sent" to Léon.
4. **The dye pot (`dye`):** three plant jars and a white ribbon; two dips mix colors; a simplified picture with a mordant note. Then C6 with the made-up "one pot colors 4 ribbons" recipe.
5. **The stitch desk (`stitch`):** a scrolling original drawing in the style of the embroidery (labeled as a drawing), six tap spots asking "Who would want this remembered?" Set D beside it.
6. **Keeper's Desk folder (`desk`):** five text cards and set E. No images, no symptoms.

All screens work at 320 px (vertical lists for the river, timeline and strip).
