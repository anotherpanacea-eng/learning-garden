## The knowledge

Sourced in the [audit](../source-audits/276-summer-show.md); unconfirmed claims were cut or kept out of child text.

**Rhyme, syllable, beat.** Words rhyme when their sounds match from the last stressed vowel on (quick, stick); a rhyming couplet is two rhyming lines in a row. A syllable is a beat of a word; many poems have a steady pattern of strong and weak syllables. The Year 2 programme asks for writing poetry and a repertoire of poems learned by heart, recited "with appropriate intonation to make the meaning clear"; Common Core SL.1.4 to SL.4.5 ask for clear, expressive speaking and visual displays (SL.2.5 also asks for audio recordings; this pack records nothing, by design).

**Forms.** A **haiku** is a short poem of one moment, traditionally of nature or a season; Japanese haiku count 17 *on* (sound units) in phrases of 5, 7 and 5, often with a season word. An *on* is not an English syllable (Wikipedia, citing the Haiku Society of America: about 12 English syllables approximate 17 *on*), so 5-7-5 is a convention for English haiku, not a rule, and many poets write fewer; the child text says so. Matsuo Bashō (1644 to 1694) wrote the frog poem (*furu ike ya / kawazu tobikomu / mizu no oto*, 5, 7, 5 *on*). **Free verse** is not dictated by a set meter or form and is often shaped by speech rhythms (Academy of American Poets); Whitman is its American founder figure. **A ballad** is a plot-driven song from the folk tradition, kept by recitation before print, often in quatrains (Academy of American Poets); the strict ballad stanza alternates four- and three-stress lines rhyming abcb, with abab a looser variant (RPO glossary; UCSB English Broadside Ballad Archive). **A sonnet** is fourteen lines, traditionally in iambic pentameter; the Shakespearean form is three quatrains and a couplet rhyming abab cdcd efef gg; the Petrarchan turn (volta) falls between lines 8 and 9 (Academy of American Poets). Shakespeare's 154 sonnets were printed together in 1609. In Sonnet 18 line 9 begins "But" and answers lines 1 to 8; every line has ten syllables if *temperate* has three and *every* two. Lines 2 and 4 (*temperate*, *date*) rhyme only on an unstressed syllable, so items key lines 5 to 8 (CDCD), which rhyme fully. **Enjambment** carries a sentence past a line end; a **caesura** is a pause within a line. **Sound devices:** alliteration ("mystical moist"), assonance ("shake ... May"), onomatopoeia ("creaks").

**Poem, play, prose** (CCSS RL.4.5): poems have verse, rhythm and meter; drama has a cast of characters, settings, descriptions, dialogue and stage directions; prose has sentences, paragraphs and chapters. A script names the speaker before each line and uses no quotation marks. **Text and stage** (RL.4.7): a visual or oral version reflects specific descriptions and directions in the text, and also departs from them. **Pictures** (RL.5.7): visual and multimedia elements contribute to meaning, tone or beauty. **Drama** (KS3): the work of dramatists is communicated through performance, and alternative staging allows different interpretations; RL.7.7 names lighting, sound and color. *The Winter's Tale* (First Folio, 1623), act 3 scene 3, gives the whole direction *Exit, pursued by a bear*, which every production must interpret. **Performing** (KS3): improvising, rehearsing and performing scripts and poetry using "role, intonation, tone, volume, mood, silence, stillness and action".

**Texts.** Public domain, each matched word for word against a Project Gutenberg transcription by script: Stevenson, "Bed in Summer" (1885; #136); Whitman, "When I Heard the Learn'd Astronomer" (*Drum-Taps*, 1865; #1322); Shakespeare, Sonnet 18 (1609; #1041) and the bear direction (#1539); the first two stanzas of "Get Up and Bar the Door" (first printed by Herd, 1776; Child's 1858 text, #43825). Newly written: the hat story in prose, script and ballad; the pattern poem; three haiku; the free-verse poem.

## The lesson

## The game or activity

Read-aloud on every line; each public-domain poem shows **A poem from long ago** and its source line. No score, timer, lock or mastery label. Drags have tap equivalents.

- **(a) The Poem Shelf.** Drop ten poem cards on five kinds (rhyming couplets, haiku, free verse, ballad, sonnet; "What Is Quick?" and "Bed in Summer" are both couplets, and the pattern is a feature, not a kind) with a "how I knew" clue card. Tools: a **clap counter** (one tap per syllable) and **rhyme letters** (tap line ends; a new sound gets a new letter). Whitman's poem is Sapling; the old ballad and the sonnet are Keeper's Desk.
- **(b) The Rehearsal Room.** **Learn It by Heart:** hear "Bed in Summer" (authored audio), then hide words one tap at a time and say it to a grown-up; a sentence to introduce it; a supplied picture to hold up. At Sapling and Keeper's Desk, **Rehearsal Marks** (pause, louder, softer, slower, stand still) on the script or ballad, then a live performance; an improvise card for the grown-up. Nothing is heard or kept but the marks.
- **(c) My Own Poem.** A pattern frame, a rhyme hunt and an ending chooser; then the paper card. No text box.
- **(d) Three Ways to Tell It.** The hat story as prose, script and ballad in three columns; sort nine feature cards.
- **(e) Lights and Set.** Choose light, set pieces and places for the hat scene; **Book or stage?** (D1); **Two stagings** (Dot's bright and quick, Bramble's fading to blue with a wind sound and a silence; same words); at Keeper's Desk, **The bear**: choose one of three ways to show it and say why to a grown-up.
- **(f) Picture Show.** Choose one of eight supplied pictures and a color wash for each ballad stanza; play the cards one at a time while the child reads aloud to a grown-up.
- **The Traveling Show (Act V).** Choose a size; a first running order and light; answer one of Nuria's notes; change one thing or keep it with a reason; pick a limit card. Kept first and changed versions sit side by side.
