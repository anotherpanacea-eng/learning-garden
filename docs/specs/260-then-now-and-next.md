## The knowledge

Sourced in the [audit](../source-audits/260-then-now-and-next.md); terms follow the DfE grammar glossary and the Common Core Language standards, simplified, with US school names in child text.

**Tense and time.** English verbs change form for two tenses, present (*rains*) and past (*rained*). Future time is shown with a helper, most often *will* (*will rain*); US standards call this the simple future tense, and the DfE glossary notes that English, unlike Spanish or French, has no future inflection. The present also tells habits and states (*the brook runs past the mill*) and, after *if* or *when*, future events (*if it rains tomorrow*).

**Regular and irregular past.** Most verbs form the past by adding -ed (spelling changes are pack 254's). A set of common verbs is irregular, and many fall into families: *blow, blew; grow, grew; throw, threw; fly, flew; know, knew*; *sing, sang; ring, rang; drink, drank; swim, swam; begin, began*; *think, thought; bring, brought; catch, caught; teach, taught; buy, bought*; and verbs that do not change (*put, cut, let, hit, set*). Children commonly over-apply -ed (*goed, runned*); in a large study of children's speech this was fairly uncommon and is read as evidence that the rule has been learned. The families are memory aids, not rules (*glow, glowed*), so the pack never tells a child to copy a look-alike. Over centuries many irregular verbs became regular (*help*, whose old past was *holp*, among them), and rarely used ones changed fastest; the most frequent (*be, go*) stayed irregular.

**Progressive.** A form of *be* plus the -ing form (the present participle) shows an action in progress: *is raining* (present), *was raining* (past), *will be raining* (future). A longer action in progress is often interrupted by a short one in the simple past: *Pip was sleeping when the thunder cracked.* The plain present tells a habit that still holds; the present progressive tells now. A past habit takes the past (*every spring it rained*), so habit items pin the present with *these days*.

**Present perfect.** *Has* or *have* plus the past participle (in child text, the "has form") calls attention to the present result of a past event: *he has gone to lunch* implies he is still away, against *he went to lunch* (DfE glossary). The simple past goes with a named, finished time (*yesterday, last spring*); the present perfect goes with a period reaching up to now (*so far, since Monday, up to now*) and often with no time named. With no time named, or with *this week*, US usage also accepts the simple past, so no item keys the perfect on those alone; each keys it on *so far*, *since*, *up to now* or a stated present result, or leaves *rained* out of the choices. For many irregular verbs the participle differs from the past (*went, gone; rose, risen; blew, blown; saw, seen; fell, fallen; grew, grown*). In American speech the simple past is common and accepted with *just, already* and *yet*; the items avoid those words.

**Consistency and choosing.** In one passage about one time, tense usually stays the same; a shift is right when the time really changes, including the present for something still true now (*the ledger lists the floods*) (CCSS L.5.1d asks for correcting *inappropriate* shifts). The past perfect (*had gone*) marks the earlier of two past events; the present after *if* or *when* expresses a real future condition (*if it will rain* is not standard).
