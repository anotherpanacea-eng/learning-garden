## The knowledge

Two writer passes (31 searches) and an independent review pass (5 searches) checked every claim; sources are in the audit.

**Two poles.** The North Pole lies in the Arctic Ocean, largely covered by floating sea ice and ringed by land (Canada, Alaska, Russia, Greenland, northern Europe). The South Pole lies on Antarctica, a continent under an ice sheet, ringed by the Southern Ocean. Antarctic ice (with ice shelves) covers about 13.6 million km², averages about 1.9 km thick and reaches 4,757 m (BAS Bedmap3, 2025). Antarctica is the colder pole, mainly because it is high (average elevation about 2.3 km) and gets little heat from the ocean; the Arctic's thin ice sits on sea water. People have lived in the Arctic for thousands of years; nobody lives in Antarctica permanently. The contrast is a simplification: Greenland carries thick ice on rock.

**Who lives where.** Polar bears, Arctic foxes, walruses and narwhals live in the Arctic. Penguins live almost only in the Southern Hemisphere (the Galápagos penguin, near the equator, is the exception); none live in the Arctic.

**Midnight sun and polar night.** In spec 141's words: Earth spins on its **axis**, tilted about 23.4 degrees and pointing the same way all year, so first the northern and then the southern half **leans toward the Sun**. Near a pole, the leaning half turns all day without leaving the light (**midnight sun**); half a year later it leans away and the Sun stays below the horizon (**polar night**). The Sun is still shining on the other half of the world, and the poles have opposite seasons. Midnight sun stays low, so it is still cold; polar night has stars, moonlight and sometimes the aurora. Computed for 2026 (PyEphem, top of the Sun, refraction included):

| Place | Sun up all day and night | Sun never up |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Willowbank, 42.5°N | never (June day 15 h 18 min; spec 141) | never |
| Tromsø, Norway, 69.6°N (in Sápmi) | about 18 May to 24 July (68 days) | about 28 November to 14 January (48 days) |
| North Pole | about 19 March to 24 September (190 days) | the rest of the year |
| South Pole | about 21 September to 21 March | about 23 March to 19 September |

**Keeping warm.** Adaptations arise over many generations, never by an animal's wish.
- *Wrap up:* fur and feathers trap air; blubber is thick fat under the skin. A polar bear has dense fur, see-through guard hairs that look white, black skin and a thick fat layer. Insulation slows heat leaving; the body makes the heat.
- *Less skin to lose heat from:* the Arctic fox has small rounded ears, a short muzzle and short legs, and is a dog-family animal with furred footpads. Pip, a red fox, has bigger ears.
- *Huddle:* emperor penguin fathers hold the egg on their feet under a fold of skin through winters below −40 °C and huddle; air inside a tight huddle can rise above 20 °C, and birds shuffle so all get turns inside.
- *Change coat:* the white Arctic fox (about 99% of them) is white in winter, brown and gray in summer.
- *Go away:* some animals leave for the worst months (the Arctic tern, spec 144).
- *Older depth:* in the fox's legs, outgoing warm blood warms returning cold blood, so little heat is lost at the paws (counter-current exchange).

**Polar food chains.** Every chain starts with tiny living things that use sunlight. **Antarctic:** phytoplankton → Antarctic krill → Adélie penguin → leopard seal (leopard seals eat krill, fish and penguins). Nearly two hundred kinds of predators eat krill. **Arctic:** ice algae under the sea ice → copepods → Arctic cod → ringed seal → polar bear; polar bears mainly hunt ringed seals. The built legend is reused exactly (**food → eater**). If krill grew scarce, their eaters **may** have less food.

**Two kinds of ice.** Sea ice is frozen, salty sea water that forms and melts on the ocean. Glacier ice forms on land from snow pressed down over many years; an **ice sheet** covers a landmass (Antarctica, Greenland). Glaciers flow slowly downhill under their own weight. **Icebergs** break off glaciers and ice shelves and are fresh-water ice; about nine-tenths of an iceberg is under water (917 ÷ 1,025 ≈ 0.89, typical densities). Snow falls in layers, so deeper ice is older and its bubbles hold old air; the EPICA Dome C core, drilled to bedrock, holds a continuous 800,000-year record. Land ice melting raises the sea and floating ice barely does (all of Antarctica's ice holds about 58 m of sea level, Greenland's about 7.4 m; not taught as numbers). Melting and freezing belong to spec 145.

**Ice near Willowbank.** The Pyrenees keep a few small glaciers. The largest, Aneto, shrank from about 135.7 ha in 1981 to 48.1 ha in 2022 (study in *The Cryosphere*, which reports 64.7% lost; these figures give 64.6%). There were over 100 Pyrenean glaciers in 1850 and 18 in 2022. Ice Age glaciers carved the U-shaped Ordesa valley in the Spanish Pyrenees.

**The Inuit** ("the people" in Inuktitut; one person is an Inuk). About 180,000 Inuit live across Alaska, Inuit Nunangat in Canada, Kalaallit Nunaat (Greenland) and Chukotka (Inuit Circumpolar Council figure; counts are uncertain). Nunavut, a Canadian territory with an Inuit majority, was created on 1 April 1999. Inuit knowledge of sea ice, weather, animals and routes is detailed and tested over generations. Traditional travel uses the qamutik (dog sled) and the qajaq, the source of the English word "kayak". An **inuksuk** (plural inuksuit) is a stone landmark built by Inuit, used to show the way or mark hunting places and caches. In Inuit languages *iglu* means any house; snow houses (*igluvijaq*) were built mainly in Canada's central Arctic and northwest Greenland, and some hunters still build them as trip shelters. Inuit today live in towns, go to school and work, and keep their knowledge, languages and food.

**The Sámi.** Sápmi spans northern Norway, Sweden and Finland and Russia's Kola Peninsula. Population estimates run from about 50,000 to over 100,000. About one in ten Sámi herd reindeer (more in some areas); other Sámi do every kind of work. Sámi National Day, 6 February, marks the opening of the first Sámi national assembly, in Trondheim in 1917, where Sámi from Norway and Sweden met across the border. Joik is a traditional Sámi singing style; a joik can call to mind a person, an animal or a place.

**Older depth, one sentence:** in the 1900s, many Sámi and Inuit children were sent to boarding schools where their languages were not allowed; many communities are bringing those languages back today (Norway's 2023 truth commission; Canada's residential schools).

**To the South Pole.** Roald Amundsen's Norwegian team of five left base on 19 October 1911 with dog teams and reached the South Pole on **14 December 1911**, returning 42 days later. From 1903 to 1906, at Gjoa Haven (Uqsuqtuuq), Amundsen had learned from Netsilik Inuit about skin clothing and dogs. Robert Falcon Scott's British team of five, man-hauling on the last stretch, reached the Pole on **17 January 1912** (some sources say 18), 34 days later, and found Amundsen had been first. **Older depth only:** all five died on the journey back; historians point to several reasons together, among them very cold weather, too little fuel and food, and hauling sledges by hand. One gentle Keeper's note, no detail of suffering.

**Shackleton.** *Endurance* was trapped and crushed by sea ice and sank in the Weddell Sea in November 1915. After a boat journey by six men from Elephant Island to South Georgia, Shackleton rescued the rest of his crew in August 1916; all 28 men survived. In March 2022 the wreck was found 3,008 m down; it is protected under the Antarctic Treaty and was surveyed untouched.

**A continent for science.** The Antarctic Treaty was signed in Washington on 1 December 1959 by twelve countries and came into force on 23 June 1961. It keeps Antarctica for peace and science, bans military bases, exercises and weapons tests (military people and equipment may support science), and sets claims aside: the Treaty neither recognizes nor rejects them. More than 50 countries have now joined. The Madrid Protocol (1991) bans mining except for science. Stations study ice, weather, stars, penguins and krill, with staff who come and go. In the Arctic, the land belongs to the surrounding countries.

**Watching the ice.** Satellites have measured Arctic sea ice since 1979. September ice is shrinking about 12 percent per decade against its 1981-2010 average (NSIDC 12.4% to 12.5% in recent releases, NASA 12.2%), because the Arctic is warming. One year is not a trend (spec 140). How much more ice changes depends on choices people make. No disaster images, no blame, and never a question about a family.

## The games

**Which pole?** Two top-down globes over two cross-sections (ice on water, ice on rock). Twelve cards (polar bear, emperor and Adélie penguins, Arctic fox, walrus, narwhal, leopard seal, an Inuit town, Sámi reindeer herders, a research station, ice on rock, ice on water); tap a card, then **Arctic**, **Antarctic** or **Both** (stations). **Why?** chips follow each placement. A misplaced penguin is read back, never refused. Line: *A simplified picture: real coastlines are more complicated.*

**The Sun dial**, model `sun-v1`. A 24-hour ring with a horizon, four places and twelve months. Predict *Will the Sun set today?*, then **Play a day**: the Sun circles above or below the horizon from an authored table for the 15th of each month (PyEphem, 2026). Sapling adds **Show the lean** (spec 141's globe when built, else a static tilted globe). Line: *A simplified picture: one day in each month.* No location is read.

**The keep-warm bench**, model `warm-v1`. Three jars of 40 °C water in a 0 °C ice bath, wrapped in no layer, a thin or a thick fat layer; predict, then **Wait 10 minutes** up to four times. Warmth above the bath halves every 5, 10 or 20 minutes:

| Minutes | 0 | 10 | 20 | 30 | 40 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| No layer | 40 | 10 | 2.5 | 0.6 | 0.2 |
| Thin fat | 40 | 20 | 10 | 5 | 2.5 |
| Thick fat | 40 | 28 | 20 | 14 | 10 |

Below Sapling, displays round to whole degrees (28.3 and 14.1 show as 28 and 14). A fair-test card names what stays the same. Then the **ice contrast**: thickly wrapped ice in a warm room stays frozen longer; fat slows heat moving in as well as out. Line: *A made-up model with made-up numbers.*

**Blubber glove:** off-screen, adult-led, described only in the grown-up panel; nothing is asked or recorded.

**Chain board.** The built legend exactly ("Arrow: food → eater. Food supplies energy to the eater."). Build the Antarctic chain, then the Arctic chain beside it; **What if krill were scarce?** highlights arrows up the chain, with "may" answers. Reversed arrows are read back.
