## Purpose

One through-line runs across four stations: **who makes the rules, who keeps them, how guilt is decided, and who wrote the story down.** A child comes to understand that:

- Roman and Greek children lived about 2,000 years ago; some things about childhood changed (wax tablets, the bulla) and some did not (dolls, bread), and not every child went to school.
- The Roman Republic spread power on purpose, and events from Sulla to Augustus wore its safeguards away; Augustus said he restored it, and historians still argue whether the fall could have been stopped.
- Everything written about Boudicca comes from two Roman writers, years later, who disagree; a burnt layer confirms fire, not motive; a Victorian statue is a picture made much later.
- Medieval English justice moved from ordeals and trial by battle toward juries after 1215, Magna Carta promised lawful judgment to free men, which left many people out, and a medieval juror was expected to know the case where a juror today must not.

## The knowledge

Adult prose for reviewers; sources and the review pass are in the audit.

**Children then and now.** Roman children wrote on hand-sized wax tablets with a stylus. The *litterator* (primary teacher) was paid by parents, so poorer children had little schooling; many children worked and some were enslaved. Toys were wood, clay, bone or cloth; dolls are common finds; knucklebones (ankle bones thrown like dice) were played by children and adults. Free-born boys wore a bulla, a charm on a necklace. Meals: bread, porridge, olives, grapes; a tunic. One line on Sparta's soldier training and Athenian teachers. "Today" stays general (pencils, screens, electric light); the child's own life is never the comparison.

**The Republic.** By tradition the kings were expelled in 509 BCE. Two consuls, elected yearly, led the state and armies; each could block the other. The Senate was not elected (former magistrates sat for life) and its advice carried great weight. Citizen men voted in assemblies; in the centuriate assembly richer groups held most votes (left out of child text). Plebeians won tribunes with a veto (traditionally from 494 BCE). Women and enslaved people could not vote. Athens (spec 136) voted on laws directly; Rome mostly elected officials.

**The fall.** The Gracchi (133 and 121 BCE) pushed land for poorer citizens and were killed by opponents (Keeper's note only). In 88 BCE Sulla marched his legions into Rome, the first army to do so. In January 49 BCE Caesar crossed the Rubicon, his province's boundary, with an army; which stream it was is still argued (the Fiumicino was renamed Rubicone in 1933; the Pisciatello has supporters). Made dictator for life early in 44 BCE, Caesar was killed by senators on 15 March 44 BCE (one plain sentence, Sapling and up). After Actium (spec 165), the Senate named Octavian Augustus on 16 January 27 BCE. His *Res Gestae* says he handed the state back to the Senate and people; historians dispute how real that was, and his powers grew afterward. "Inevitable or choices?" is a spoken Keeper's Desk prompt.

**Boudicca.** Caesar raided Britain in 55 BCE; Claudius invaded in 43 CE and stayed. The Iceni were a client kingdom; Prasutagus named Nero co-heir with his two daughters, and officials seized it all. Tacitus says Boudicca and her daughters were treated cruelly (only that, in a Keeper's note). In 60 or 61 CE she led the Iceni and Trinovantes; Tacitus names Camulodunum, Londinium and Verulamium as destroyed. Suetonius Paulinus defeated her at an unidentified site. **Sources:** Tacitus, *Agricola* (about 98) and *Annals* (about 115 to 117); his father-in-law Agricola served on Suetonius's staff in Britain about 58 to 62. Cassius Dio wrote in Greek, roughly 211 to 234. They disagree on the cause (mistreatment; recalled loans) and on her death; only Dio describes her looks (tall, tawny hair, golden necklace); both give her speeches no one recorded; no Iceni writing survives. **Ground:** a red and black burnt layer up to half a meter thick under Colchester and a thick red layer over pre-60 CE finds in London confirm fire, not motive. **Later picture:** Thornycroft's scythed-chariot group, begun 1856, set up by Westminster Bridge in 1902, later a suffrage rallying point. No death counts anywhere.

**Medieval justice.** Builds on spec 137's pyramid and caveat. King's law, manor courts, church and town courts. No police: tithings (nominally ten men) answered for each other, the hue and cry, and the sheriff (shire reeve) as the king's officer in the shire. Guilt by ordeal (cold water is the only kind named, as "a hard test") or, after 1066, trial by battle, by most accounts a Norman introduction. In 1215 the Fourth Lateran Council (canon 18) barred clergy from ordeals; a royal order of January 1219 acted on it, and juries followed from about 1220. Medieval jurors were expected to know the parties or find out (Klerman questions how uniform this was); today jurors must not know the case. **Magna Carta:** June 1215; clause 39 promised free men lawful judgment of their equals or the law of the land; unfree peasants were left out; annulled 24 August 1215, reissued 1216, 1217, 1225; four 1215 copies survive. It was later read by many as a jury right; in 1215 it was a baronial safeguard. **Punishment:** for lesser offenses mostly fines; stocks and pillory for public shame; under the Assize of Bread (about 1266) the loaf's weight moved with the wheat price and the pillory followed repeated short weight. No torture or execution appears.

## The lesson

Jane's order: **teach, then guided, then practice.**

1. **Teach 1, then and now:** ask what was the same and what was different; worked example, a Roman child's wax tablet, revealed in three steps.
2. **Teach 2, who makes the rules:** the Republic's two consuls for one year; worked example: who makes, who carries out, what stops one man.
3. **Teach 3, who tells the story:** who wrote it, when, which side; worked example on Tacitus and Dio.
4. **Teach 4, a fair hearing:** make, keep, decide; worked example on the bread law.
5. **Guided item:** three cards (bread law, officials who weigh, a jury) into three trays, with three visible steps.
6. **Practice:** stations and problem sets, any order.

**"I'm stuck" ladder** on every practice item: a hint; then a worked example of an easier similar case plus how to start this one (each set has one in the companion); then "Skip for now", with nothing lost and nothing recorded as failure.

**Contrasts:** then and now; Athens and Rome; a ruler's own account and later historians; two writers on one side; writing and the ground; a portrait and a later picture; ordeal and jury; a juror who knows the case and one who must not.

**Top misconceptions designed against:** everything past was different; "long ago" means grandparents' time; all ancient things happened at once; a republic is a renamed kingdom; the Rubicon's danger was the water; BCE years count up; a ruler's words prove what happened; Boudicca left her own account, or nothing survives; one side's writers agree; the ground records words; a statue shows a real face; sheriffs come from cowboy films; stocks were a prison; Magna Carta began juries or protected everyone; medieval jurors were judges or chosen by lot.

## The game or activity

Four stations, any order. Every card shows its label: **A true thing from history**, **A story from Willowbank**, **A picture made much later** (the statue) or **A made-up model**. No timer, move count or score.

**Station 1, Then and Now.** Six short cards, a map card and a time strip (Great Pyramid about 4,500 years ago, Rome's emperors about 2,000, medieval England about 800, today); a *Then* / *Now* / *Both* sort (A2).

**Station 2, Who Rules Rome? The Rule Board.** A made-up model: four safeguard tiles (one-year term, two consuls, armies kept out of Rome, tribunes who can block). Four event cards slide on one at a time (88, 49, 44, 27 BCE); the child taps the tile they predict each event breaks, then flips the card for the line. Augustus's card dims all four tiles at once while leaving them visible: the forms stayed, the power moved. Model line: *A made-up model. Many people and choices are left out.* Then cards on Caesar's death (one sentence), Augustus and "two ways to tell it".

**Station 3, Two Romans Tell Boudicca.** Seven context cards, source cards for Tacitus and Dio, and the statue. **Who says so?** sort: five claims into *Tacitus*, *Dio*, *Both*, *The ground*, *A picture made much later*. The statue card shows a line drawing, never a battle scene.

**Station 4, A Fair Hearing.** Eleven short cards, plus **the loaf bench** (a made-up model): a sign gives a weight; the child sets a loaf on a picture scale, reads it, says "full weight" or "short" and, at Sapling, how short and what fraction. Weights are whole tens of grams from 400 to 500. Model line: *Real bread laws changed the weight with the price of wheat.*

At 320 px trays stack under the cards and tiles go 2 by 2. Narration reads every card.
