## Purpose

Spec 141 teaches the sky and ends with a five-line exploration card and one radio-delay item; spec 154 teaches forces and Moon gravity. Nothing teaches how a rocket moves, what people do in space, why we send robots, how space telescopes help, or how hard a Mars trip would be. The Space Exploration domain (26 topics) has no built lesson, and S8-III/V is thin above the sky basics.

A child who plays this comes to understand that:

- a rocket throws gas out one way and the gas pushes it the other way, with no air needed (Keeper's Desk: one interaction, two equal and opposite forces on two bodies);
- astronauts are trained people with jobs who float because they fall around Earth with the station;
- the space age is a short, recent run of firsts by people from many countries, women and engineers among them;
- robots go where people cannot yet, and a Mars rover drives partly by itself because messages take minutes;
- a telescope is first a light bucket, and one above the air sees more sharply;
- a crewed Mars trip is far harder than a Moon trip, and whether anyone else is out there is open.

## The knowledge

Each claim is in the source audit.

**How a rocket pushes.** A rocket engine throws hot gas out fast. The rocket pushes the gas back and the gas pushes the rocket forward: one interaction, two equal and opposite forces on two different things (Newton's third law). It does not push on the air; NASA Glenn notes rockets work better in space, since exhaust in air must also shove air aside. A let-go balloon is the everyday picture. To lift off, the push must exceed the rocket's weight (spec 154's unbalanced forces, applied). **Keeper's Desk contrast:** a third-law pair never cancels, because its two forces act on different bodies. The forces that balance on one body (a book's weight and the table's push on the book) are not a pair; the partner of the book's weight is the book's pull on Earth.

**Rocket history (history card).** Gunpowder rockets were first made in China; whether the 1232 "fire arrows" at Kaifeng were true rockets is debated, so the text says only "by the 1200s". Tsiolkovsky (Russia), Goddard (USA) and Oberth (Germany-Romania) each worked out rocket theory independently; Tsiolkovsky's key paper is usually dated 1903. Goddard launched the first liquid-fuel rocket on 16 March 1926 at his aunt's farm in Auburn, Massachusetts; it rose about 12 m (41 ft).

**Milestones** (dates checked; Sputnik, Gagarin, Apollo 11, the ISS and Perseverance were checked by spec 141's audit and are re-used):

STS-95 was also John Glenn's return to space. Johnson "helped with paths for Apollo" avoids the claim that she did all the Apollo 11 math. Hubble's mirror had a 2-micron shape error; astronauts fitted correcting optics in December 1993. **Current missions change.** Artemis II flew four astronauts (three NASA, one Canadian Space Agency) around the Moon, 1-10 April 2026 (NASA); the text states it as a past event, with no forecast of later missions. As of 2026 nobody has been to Mars or lived on the Moon; child text avoids "today" so it cannot go stale.

**Life on the station.** The ISS circles Earth about every 90 minutes (about 16 sunrises a day); the crew keeps GMT. Astronauts float because they and the station fall around Earth together; gravity there is still strong. Since 2023 the water system recovers about 98% of water, including from urine, breath and sweat; each person needs about a gallon (3.8 L) a day. Crew sleep in bags fixed to the wall and exercise about two hours a day (NASA), because bones and muscles weaken. Inside there is air; outside, a spacesuit gives air and protection.

**Robots and rovers.** Rovers go where people cannot yet: far, cold, with thin air (under 1% of Earth's pressure, NASA) that is mostly carbon dioxide, and strong radiation. NASA's rovers: Sojourner (1997, 10.6 kg), Spirit and Opportunity (2004), Curiosity (Gale Crater, August 2012), Perseverance (Jezero Crater, 2021; car-sized, 43 sample tubes). China's Zhurong landed in May 2021 to look for water ice. The jobs: drive, photograph, drill and study rocks, look for signs that water flowed long ago and that tiny life might once have been possible. No rover had found life by 2026. A message takes about 3 to 22 minutes one way (spec 141's range, 56.9 to 400.2 million km), so 6 to 45 minutes for a question and answer (computed). Teams send a day's plan; the rover drives itself between waypoints and stops when its cameras see danger.

**Telescopes.** A telescope first gathers light, like a bucket in rain: twice as wide catches four times as much, so fainter, farther things show. Magnifying comes second. Moving air bends starlight (twinkling), so a telescope above the air sees sharply; "closer to the stars" saves almost no distance. Hubble's mirror is 2.4 m across. Webb's is 6.5 m, made of 18 hexagonal segments, folded for launch, and it works about 1.5 million km from Earth, mostly in infrared light our eyes cannot see (NASA). NASA rounds its 6.6 m mirror to 6.5 m. A circle model gives Webb about 7.3 times Hubble's area (computed); NASA quotes about six times, since Webb's mirror is not a full circle; the item says "about 6". One deep-field card shows a sky full of galaxies.

**Journey to Mars (Keeper's Desk).** NASA's Human Research Program names five hazards: space radiation, isolation and confinement, distance from Earth, gravity fields, and hostile closed environments. Marble's research calls these RIDGE; the bench uses NASA's names. Perseverance's cruise took 203 days (launched 30 July 2020, NASA; computed), about 6.7 months. A far crew must solve emergencies alone and carry or recycle everything. Bone loss figures vary (NASA cites about 1-1.5% a month); the text says only that bones weaken month by month. **Are we alone?** At a 1950 lunch at Los Alamos, Enrico Fermi asked what is remembered as "Where is everybody?" (wording varies; the text paraphrases). In 1960 Frank Drake's Project Ozma listened to Tau Ceti and Epsilon Eridani with a 26 m radio telescope at Green Bank, West Virginia, for about 150 hours and heard no recognizable signal. No search since has found a confirmed one. Silence is weak evidence, since only a tiny part of sky, frequencies and time has been searched. The question is open.

**Fiction boundary.** Willowbank, the letters and the Rover Yard map are fiction; real astronauts appear only on checked cards, with no invented words. No disaster content.

## The game or activity

**(a) Balloon Line.** A balloon on a straw on a string. Controls: **Air** (a little, some, lots), **Nozzle** (left, right), **Let go**. It moves away from the nozzle, farther with more air. After release two equal arrows appear together, one on the gas and one on the balloon. **No air** empties the pictured room; the balloon still goes, a little farther. Keeper's Desk panel: two skaters push apart; their force meters always match, and the smaller skater speeds away faster. Model line: *A simplified picture: real rockets burn fuel to make hot gas, and the No air room is pretend. The arrows show which way each push goes.*

**(b) Station Day.** A module cutaway with seven tap-to-reveal hotspots (water, food, sleeping bag, treadmill, recycling, window, spacesuit) and a lift-flap, *Why does everything float?* One sentence each.

**(c) Decade strip.** Cards from the milestone table, plus Goddard 1926, sit in a tray below a strip from 1920 to 2030 marked every 10 years. Pick a card, tap a slot; **Check** gives per-card feedback and never rearranges. **Send to the Long Line** (when spec 133 exists) places the same cards there, where they crowd into the last two boxes: *The whole space age is shorter than one box.* Card IDs for spec 133: `goddard-rocket`, `sputnik`, `gagarin`, `tereshkova`, `hubble`, `iss-crew`, `mars-rovers-2021`, `webb`, plus 133's existing `moon-landing`.

**(d) Rover Yard.** A made-up 6 × 4 grid with rocks, soft sand and a target. Plan cards: **Forward 1**, **Turn left**, **Turn right**, **Photo**, **Drill**. **Send** fills a *Message on its way* bar, then the rover acts, stopping itself before a rock or soft sand. **What if we steered live?** shows the bar on every move. Model line: *A made-up map and a squeezed delay. A real message to Mars takes 3 to 22 minutes one way, and real rovers drive about as fast as a slow walk for a few steps, then stop and look.*

**(e) Light Bucket.** Two buckets fill in rain (one twice as wide); two mirrors show a dim and a brighter galaxy. **Air** wobbles the view; **Above the air** steadies it. Model line: *A simplified picture: the pictures are drawn, and real mirrors bring light to a point.*

**(f) Mission Board (Keeper's Desk).** Five hazard cards and eight fix cards, each fix with a cost (mass, time, money or risk). Match, then answer two say-why prompts read with a grown-up. An **Are we alone?** card closes the board.
