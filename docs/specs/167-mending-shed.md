## Purpose

The strand map's S5-I family ("material properties vs object; choose/test a material") has no activity. Of the 40 pinned Marble Matter & Materials topics, drafts cover changes, dissolving and separating (spec 145), magnetic materials (154), electrical conductors (163), insulation (153) and energy sources (147). Nothing teaches what things are **made of**, how a material is **chosen** for a job, or how a **test** decides.

After the storm the valley is full of broken things, and the mending is the lesson. A child comes to understand that an object differs from its material; that pieces taken apart and rebuilt are still the same pieces; that squashing, bending, twisting and stretching change a shape but not the material, and materials answer differently; that a job names the **property** it needs, often several, so "best" means best for this job; that a fair test decides, and the conclusion quotes it; that precise words name properties; and, at Keeper's Desk, that many raw materials are finite and mending, reusing and recycling each have costs and savings.

## The knowledge

Plain adult statements, each tied to a source in the [audit](../source-audits/167-mending-shed.md). Search-excerpt pass, 2026-10-25.

**Objects and materials.** An object is a thing with a use (bucket, sack, gate, spoon); a material is the stuff it is made of (wood, metal, plastic, glass, cloth, rubber, clay, stone, paper). Everyday "material" often means cloth; in science it is whatever something is made of. The same kind of object can be made of different materials (a wooden, a metal and a plastic bucket). Many objects are made of several materials (a lantern: metal frame, glass panes, cotton wick).

**Taking apart and rebuilding.** An object made of a set of pieces can be taken apart and the same pieces built into a different object. Counting the pieces before and after shows nothing was made or lost; only the arrangement changed. It is about countable pieces; it does not mean every change can be undone (spec 145).

**Changing shape.** Four actions change a solid's shape: **squash** (press), **bend** (curve), **twist** (turn the ends opposite ways), **stretch** (pull). Materials respond differently: rubber and sponge spring back (elastic); soft clay and soft wire keep their new shape; slate, stone and dry wood barely change and can snap if forced (brittle or rigid). A material stays the same material after a shape change. Dried willow rods are stiff and can crack; basket makers soak them to make them bend, and they keep their woven shape as they dry.

**Properties and words.** A property is how a material behaves in a test. One material has many properties at once.

| Word | The test that defines it | Contrast pair |
| --- | --- | --- |
| transparent / translucent / opaque | Light passes and you see clearly through / some light passes, no clear view / no light passes | window glass / waxed paper / wooden shutter |
| flexible / rigid | Bends without breaking / does not bend | rubber strip / slate |
| elastic | Springs back after stretching or squashing | rubber / clay |
| absorbent / waterproof | Soaks up water / water does not pass through | sacking / waxed cloth |
| magnetic | A magnet pulls it | iron nail / copper kettle |
| thermal conductor / insulator | Heat passes through easily / slowly | metal pan / wooden handle |
| electrical conductor / insulator | Completes a circuit / does not (spec 163) | copper wire / rubber coat |

Heat and electricity are separate tests; one never stands in for the other. **Not all metals are magnetic.** Iron, nickel and cobalt are; ordinary steel is (it is mostly iron); copper, brass and aluminum are not. Some stainless steels barely respond, so the shed avoids steel spoons.

**Choosing a material.** A job names what the material must do. Glass suits a window because it is transparent and waterproof; slate suits a roof because it is waterproof, hard and lasts through frost, though it is heavy and snaps if bent. Many older Pyrenean mountain houses have stone walls and steep roofs of slate or flat stone slabs. A good choice often balances several properties, so there is no single "best material."

**Testing for a use.** A fair test changes one thing (the material), measures one outcome and keeps the rest the same. Repeats show spread (spec 155). The conclusion quotes the result: "waxed cloth, because in our test the least water came through."

**A true thing from history.** In 1823 the Scottish chemist **Charles Macintosh** patented a way to make cloth waterproof: rubber dissolved in naphtha (a coal-tar liquid), spread between two layers of fabric. Early coats went stiff in cold weather and sticky in hot weather, and seams leaked. The raincoat called a "mackintosh" is named after him.

**Revisit: the silt jar.** Flood-mud from Hob's field is a mixture of gravel, sand, silt and clay. A sieve holds back the gravel. The rest, shaken in a jar of water and left, the largest grains settle first: sand within about a minute, silt over an hour or two, and the finest clay can keep the water cloudy for a day or more. It applies spec 145's method choice to grain size, adds settling, and makes no topic claim.

**Keeper's Desk: finite materials and mending.** Mineral deposits are **finite**: new ones form over millions of years, far slower than people use them (USGS). Mining leaves waste rock; smelting uses much energy. Recycling aluminum takes about 5% of the energy of making it from ore (US EIA). Metals and glass can be melted and remade many times if kept clean and sorted. Paper fibers shorten each time; the US EPA gives about 5 to 7 cycles (some mills say fewer), so new fibers are always needed. Recycling still costs collecting, sorting and transport. At recycling plants a magnet lifts out steel cans; a separate machine pushes off aluminum cans. Mending and reusing keep the object itself in use, which usually saves more; a rule of thumb to weigh.

## The lesson

Jane's **teach -> guided -> practice** order. Five teach cards, worked examples one step per tap: *What is it? What is it made of?* (three buckets); *Same pieces, new thing* (the gate becomes a bench; count 9 and 9); *Squash, bend, twist, stretch* (clay stays, rubber springs back); *What does the job need?* (the sack must keep grain dry); *Make it fair* (same square, same water, same time). Each set's first item is **guided** with 1-4 visible steps.

**"I'm stuck" ladder** on every item: hint -> a worked example of an easier similar case plus how to start this one -> "skip for now", with no penalty and nothing recorded as a miss.

**Contrasts:** object vs material; same pieces rearranged vs a new substance (spec 145's baked bread); springs back vs keeps the shape vs snaps; looks right vs does the job; a fair test vs "just trying it"; heat conductor vs electricity conductor; transparent vs translucent; recycle vs mend.

**Top misconceptions designed against** (tagged on choices in the companion): `material-means-cloth`; `object-is-material`; `one-object-one-material`; `always-same-material` (all buckets are metal); `pieces-used-up`; `rebuilt-is-new-stuff`; `everything-springs-back`; `one-rule-for-all` (every material answers the same way); `shape-changes-material`; `rigid-just-push-harder`; `choose-by-looks`; `one-best-material`; `single-property`; `unfair-test-ok` (more than one thing changed); `conclude-from-belief`; `one-trial-certain`; `all-metals-magnetic`; `heat-equals-electric`; `property-mixup` (naming the wrong property); `misread-source`; `see-through-binary`; `never-run-out`; `already-gone` (finite means none left now); `recycling-free`; `same-recycling-for-all` (every material wears out, or none does).

## The game or activity

Every bench shows: *These are simplified pictures. Real materials can behave differently.* No score, timer, lock or mastery label; every drag has a tap equivalent.

**(a) The Mending Pile.** Twelve object cards (bucket x3 in wood, metal and plastic; lantern; sack; spoon; gate hinge; window pane; Dot's mailbag; rope; basket; roof slate). Tap a card; two rows appear: **What is it?** (object chips) and **What is it made of?** (material chips, several allowed). An "object | made of" board fills; multi-material cards accept several chips.

**(b) Take it apart.** Hob's broken gate: 7 planks and 2 crossbars. Tap **Take apart**; the pieces lie in a counted line (9). Choose a plan card (a bench or a shelf) and tap the pieces into place; the counter shows 9 before and 9 after. Nails are left out of the model (stated).

**(c) The Shape Bench.** Six samples (clay, soaked willow rod, rubber strip, sponge, slate, rope) and four action buttons. **Predict first** (springs back / keeps new shape / barely changes or snaps), then act, then **Let go**. A results grid (sample x action) fills. Slate's bend shows a snap: "Still slate. Now in two pieces."

**(d) The Job Board.** Five jobs: barn roof, grain sack, well bucket, mill window, Dot's mailbag. The child picks one of two or three material cards and one or two reason chips (`waterproof`, `transparent`, `strong`, `flexible`, `lasts-in-frost`, `light-to-carry`, `looks-nice`). An authored result shows the job working or failing. Pip's bread patch and Bramble's glass hammer fail kindly.

**(e) The Test Table** (Seedling, Sapling). Choose **one** test: magnet, bend, water drop, light through. Apply it to eight samples one by one; each result is written into a table **before** sorting is allowed. Then sort into two groups named by the property; another test regroups the same samples. Copper, brass and aluminum make "all metals" fail visibly.

**(f) The Sack Test** (Sapling, Keeper's Desk). A fair-test planner: **change** (material), **measure** (mL that drip through in one minute), **keep the same** (chips: square size, water amount, pouring height, time). Four cloths. **Model:** 10 cm squares on a frame over a measuring cup; 100 mL poured from the same height; water that runs off the edge goes to a side tray and is not counted; three authored trials per cloth, revealed one at a time. Labeled: *A made-up model with exact numbers.*

| Cloth | Trials (mL through) |
| --- | --- |
| sacking (jute) | 62, 66, 64 |
| cotton | 48, 52, 50 |
| waxed cloth | 2, 4, 3 |
| oilcloth | 0, 1, 0 |

Bramble's version (100 mL on sacking, 50 mL on waxed cloth) appears as a case to critique. The claim uses chip sentence starters; nothing is typed or graded.

**(g) The Silt Jar** (revisit, any depth). Sieve out the gravel; shake the rest in a jar of water and tap **Wait** (a minute, an hour, a day; labeled pictures, never a timer). Bands appear: sand, silt, then clay.

**(h) The Scrap Box** (Keeper's Desk). Ten scrap cards. Each goes to **Mend**, **Reuse**, **Recycle** or **Not sure yet** with a reason chip. A magnet sorter shows steel lifting and aluminum staying. An energy strip compares 100 units (from ore) with about 5 (recycled), labeled *a rounded comparison from a real figure*.
