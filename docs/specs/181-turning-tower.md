Spec 58 supplies perpendicular axes but explicitly teaches no angle; spec 123 (draft PR #128) turns a machine in quarter turns; proposed spec 165 (draft PR #145) builds the **degree** as one of 360 equal turns on its Shadow Bench. This pack takes ten untouched targets there, from Sprout pictures to Keeper's Desk compass constructions.

A child comes to understand that an angle is an **amount of turn** at a point, never the length of its arms; that the right angle (a quarter turn) is the benchmark that names acute, obtuse, straight and reflex angles; that lines, segments and rays differ by where they stop; that parallel means "same gap, never meeting" and perpendicular means "meets at a right angle", at any length and tilt; that diagram marks state facts the picture may not show; that you estimate before you measure; that a protractor reads correctly only when centered and zeroed, on the scale that starts at zero; that angles side by side add; and, at the Keeper's Desk, that scale drawings multiply and that equal compass arcs find a perpendicular without measuring.

## The knowledge

**What an angle is.** Two arms (rays or segments) meet at a point, the **vertex**. The angle is the **amount of turn** that takes one arm onto the other. It is a property of a shape's corner and a description of a turn: the same thing seen still or moving. Lengthening the arms, or the gap between their tips, or the space between them, changes nothing. Compare two angles by laying one on the other, vertex on vertex with one arm aligned.

**Naming by the right angle.** A **right angle** is a quarter turn (a square corner, 90°); two make a straight line (a half turn, 180°), four a full turn (360°). A corner card (any square corner) checks it at any tilt. Compare first, then name: **acute** is less open than a right angle; **obtuse** is more open but less than a straight line; **straight** is exactly a half turn; **reflex** is more than a half turn and less than a full turn, found as 360° minus the smaller angle on the other side. Mnemonics stay memory hooks only.

**Points, lines, segments, rays.** A **point** is an exact spot. A **line** goes on forever both ways; a **line segment** stops at two ends; a **ray** starts at one point and goes on. Drawn lines stand for ideal lines with no thickness.

**Parallel and perpendicular.** Lines are **parallel** when the gap between them never changes, so they never meet however long they run; two segments of any lengths are parallel if the lines through them are. No drawing runs forever, so two lines that stay apart on the page only suggest they never meet; the same-gap test is the stronger check. Lines are **perpendicular** when they meet at a right angle, at any tilt. **Horizontal** and **vertical** are directions; parallel and perpendicular are relationships between two lines.

**Diagram marks and names.** A small square states exactly 90°. Single ticks on two sides state those two are equal; double ticks mark a second, independent equal pair (not equal to the first). Matching arcs state equal angles; arrows state parallel lines. ∠ABC is the angle at the **middle** letter, B, with arms BA and BC (∠CBA is the same angle). Marks override appearance: the Mark-up Board's diagrams are labeled *not to scale*, and an unmarked feature is unknown.

**Estimating.** Benchmarks: 45° (half a right angle), 90°, 180°, 270° and 360°. Place any angle between benchmarks ("a bit more than a right angle: about 100°"), then measure. An estimate is a reasoned range; a guess of 120° against a measure of 128° is good. A big gap means recheck both the estimate and the placement.

**Measuring and drawing.** Degrees are recapped from spec 165 on a round (360°) protractor first. On the two-scale half protractor: center cross on the vertex, zero line along one arm (turn the protractor, not the page), then read the scale that starts at zero on that arm, checked against the acute/obtuse estimate (47° and 133° are on the same tick). Near 90° the two scales read close together, so placement decides. Short arms are extended; the angle doesn't change. Drawing is the inverse: arm, center, zero, count, dot, join, then re-measure. Accuracy target: within 2°. A semicircle reads only to 180°, so a reflex angle is 360° minus the measured smaller angle.

**Angles add.** Non-overlapping angles side by side add to the whole (35° + 40° = 75°); a missing part is the whole minus the known part, written as an equation with a symbol (35 + x = 90). The whole is named first from a stated fact (a small square: 90°). Diagrams give facts, not measurements.

**Keeper's Desk: scale and construction.** A scale drawing multiplies: with 1 cm for 50 cm, 12 cm on the plan is 600 cm (6 m); adding is the classic error. A triangle from two sides and the included angle: rule 8 cm, set 60° at one end, measure 5 cm along it, join; the third side is exactly 7 cm (the other angles about 38° and 82°, Keeper's note only). **Constructions** use compasses and an unmarked straight edge, with arcs left visible as evidence; measuring afterward only checks. Perpendicular bisector of PQ (8 cm): equal arcs wider than half (5 cm) from P and Q cross at two points (4 cm along and 3 cm either side); joining them cuts PQ in half at a right angle, because the four equal sides form a rhombus. Unequal arcs (5 and 6 cm) make a kite: the line is still perpendicular but crosses 3.3 cm from P, missing the middle; arcs of exactly 4 cm only touch. Angle bisector: one arc from the vertex, equal arcs from the two crossings, join; 70° becomes 35° and 35°. The perpendicular at a point on a line, and from a point off it, are shown as the same equal-arc idea (Keeper's note).

**Real anchors (see audit).**
- **Why 360.** Babylonian astronomers, who counted in sixties, split the zodiac (the Sun's yearly path among the stars) into 12 signs of 30 parts each, by about the late first millennium BCE (dating varies). Greek astronomers later used 360 parts for every circle. Nobody knows for sure why 360 was chosen; a near-360-day year and easy division are common suggestions, offered as ideas only. Child text names no Greek astronomer, since the Hipparchus attribution is hedged in the sources.
- **Al-Ijliyyah of Aleppo and the astrolabe.** A 10th-century astrolabe maker employed by Sayf al-Dawla, emir of Aleppo (r. 944-967), known from one source, Ibn al-Nadim, which says she and her father trained under a Baghdad maker. The modern name "Mariam" is not in that source and is not used. An astrolabe's back has a degree ring and a sighting rule (alidade) to measure a star's angle above the horizon; the Sun is never sighted by eye.
- **The compass and the square.** Han-dynasty carvings (the Wu family shrines, Shandong, 2nd century CE) and later Astana silk paintings show Fuxi and Nüwa holding a compass and a carpenter's square; the claim that together the two words (*gui ju*) came to mean rules and order is `[check]` and stays out of child text. Sources disagree on which figure holds which tool, so child text says only "one holds a compass, one a square".

## The game or activity

**(a) The Sail Hinge, model `sail-hinge-v1`.** Two sail arms pinned at a hub. *A made-up model: openings in 5° steps from 0° to 360°; three arm lengths; the corner card is a 90° square.* **Open/Close** swing one arm; **Longer/Shorter arms** stretch both while the turn arc stays frozen; **Lay one on the other** slides a second hinge vertex-on-vertex with one arm aligned. Sprout shows no numbers; Seedling shows quarter turns; Sapling shows degrees. The corner card is offered before the name buttons (acute, right, obtuse, straight, reflex), so comparing comes before naming; naming is never blocked. Fixtures: A1 pair 60° with arms 1 and 3; A3 pair 110° short and 30° long; A5 set 30° (long), 75° (long), 100° (short), 150° (short).

**(b) The Line Loft, model `line-loft-v1`.** Draw points, segments, rays and lines; **Extend** draws dashed continuations; **Slide the ruler** shows the gap at every point; **Turn the picture** rotates the whole figure; the corner card fits at any tilt. Sort tiles into *Parallel / Perpendicular / Neither*, always including tilted pairs and unequal lengths (the stall poles, sail bars, a tilted brace, a ladder, a non-meeting pair that is not parallel). *A made-up model: drawn lines stand for perfect lines.*

**(c) The Mark-up Board, model `markup-v1`.** Diagrams labeled *not to scale*, some deliberately contradicting their marks. **Read** mode: say what each mark states. **Mark** mode: add the marks for a stated description ("two equal sides, one right angle"); the board checks that every member of an equal group is marked and each stated right angle has a square, and a quadrilateral carries the double-tick case. ∠ABC tasks highlight the middle letter after the child chooses.

**(d) The Vane Guess, model `vane-guess-v1`.** The weathervane swings to an authored angle; the child places a guess on the dial, then **Reveal** shows the angle and the gap in degrees. *A made-up model: the vane stops on set angles; real wind does not.* Trials: 45, 80, 100, 130, 170, 210, 270, 320° (reflex companions 150, 90, 40°). The benchmark strip (45, 90, 180, 270, 360) is optional help, recorded as support. A gap within 15° gets the "Close" line; no score, no tally, no "wrong".

**(e) The Protractor Bench, model `protractor-v1`.** The child drags and turns the protractor; a glow appears only when the center sits on the vertex and the zero line on an arm. Round protractor first, then the two-scale half. **Measure** tasks: p1 47° (no arm flat), p2 135°, p3 88° (near 90°: scales read 88 and 92), p4 25° (short arms: **Extend the arms**), p5 160° (rotated), and p6 210° reflex (measure 150°, then 360 − 150). **Draw** tasks: d1 60°, d2 135°, d3 105°; **Measure my drawing** reports the drawn angle; within 2° gets "spot on". *A made-up model: the screen tool rehearses placement; a real protractor on paper builds the hand skill.*

**(f) The Millwright's Table (Keeper's Desk), model `millwright-v1`.** Ruler, protractor, compass with a held width and an unmarked straight edge on a plan sheet. Tasks: the vane plan at 1 cm : 50 cm; the 8 cm, 60°, 5 cm brace (third side 7 cm, accepted 6.8 to 7.2); the perpendicular bisector of an 8 cm PQ, presented diagonal as well as level; the kite counter-case (widths 5 and 6); the angle bisector of 70°. **Keep my arcs** retains construction marks as evidence; **Check with the square** measures afterward and is labeled a check, not a reason. *A made-up model: the compass never slips; real ones do, which is why real practice matters.*

No timers, scores, streaks, medals or locks. Every bench is open from the first visit.
