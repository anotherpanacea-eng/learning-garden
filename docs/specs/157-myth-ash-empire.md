## Purpose

The *Ancient Greece & Rome* domain (H2, 29 pinned Marble topics) has no declared coverage. Spec 135 (roads, numerals, aqueducts) and spec 136 (the Athenian assembly, theater, Olympics, Troy) take the practical and civic sides. This pack takes the rest: stories, gods and temples, Pompeii as a record, legacy in words and laws, and the western empire's end. One question runs through every station: **what kind of thing is this?**

A child comes to understand that Romulus and Remus is a legend while the Palatine hut village is evidence; that Romans linked their gods to Greek ones (with Apollo and Janus as boundary cases); that the Parthenon was Athena's temple and has had many lives since; that Pompeii's bread and painted walls tell some things and leave others out, and even the eruption's month is argued; that Latin grew into Nuria's Spanish and Léon's French while Basque did not; that Greek thinkers are remembered for how they looked, including when they were wrong; and that the western empire changed slowly, 476 CE is a conventional date, the east lasted until 1453, and historians disagree.

## The knowledge

Adult prose for reviewers; sources are in the audit. Details that could not be confirmed in two search passes were cut.

**Romulus and Remus (a story people told).** In the legend (Livy and others; Britannica's student article), twins are set adrift on the Tiber, a she-wolf cares for them, a herdsman raises them, and they found a city; they quarrel over the site and Remus dies. Tradition dated the founding to 753 BCE, a date worked out by Varro in the 1st century BCE. In spec 133's proposed wording, post-holes of huts on the Palatine Hill date from about 1000 to 700 BCE. Remus's death is in the Keeper's note only.

**The bronze she-wolf.** Long thought Etruscan (400s BCE), the Capitoline Wolf gave a radiocarbon window of about 1021 to 1153 CE (organic residue in the casting core); a 2021 thermoluminescence test of the clay core gave only a broad medieval range (8th to 14th century), and at least one scholar calls the two results inconsistent. The twins were added in the late 1400s, probably by Antonio del Pollaiolo. There is no consensus on a revised date, and the text says some experts disagree. The lesson: an old story, a possibly much younger object.

**Gods with two names.** Romans had their own gods and identified many with Greek ones, borrowing stories and images. Pairs: Zeus/Jupiter, Hera/Juno, Poseidon/Neptune, Athena/Minerva, Ares/Mars, Aphrodite/Venus, Hermes/Mercury, Artemis/Diana, Hephaestus/Vulcan. Apollo kept his name. Janus (doors, beginnings; January) has no Greek match. Mars mattered far more in Rome than Ares in Greece. So the pack says "linked", not "renamed". *Volcano* comes, through Italian, from Vulcan's name.

**The Parthenon.** Athena's temple on the Acropolis, 447 to 432 BCE (spec 133's proposed dates). 8 columns across each end and 17 down each side: 46 outer columns, corners counted once. Pheidias made a gold-and-ivory statue of Athena over a wooden core, almost 12 m with its base; it is lost. A 2023 study found traces of paint (Egyptian blue, purple) on the Parthenon sculptures in the British Museum; the child text says the carvings were painted, not the whole building. The temple became a church in the 6th century, a mosque after the Ottoman capture of Athens in the 1450s, and on 26 September 1687, during a Venetian siege, a shell set off gunpowder stored inside and brought down much of the middle. A neutral Keeper's note says its sculptures are split between the Acropolis Museum and the British Museum and people disagree about where they belong.

**Pompeii, an archive the volcano made.** Aligned with spec 139: in 79 CE Vesuvius erupted, ash and pumice covered Pompeii several meters deep, and digging began in 1748. Spec 139 teaches the volcano; this pack teaches the town as a record.
- *Bread:* 81 carbonized loaves were found in the oven of one bakery (the Bakery of Modestus), most sources say in 1862. They are round, about 20 cm across, tops scored into segments. They show baking in quantity, probably to sell. Some Roman loaves carried a baker's stamp, so the text never claims a loaf cannot name its baker.
- *Walls:* painted election notices give a candidate, an office and sometimes backers, including trade groups such as bakers and fruit sellers. They never say who won. (Counts are not given; sources differ.)
- *Amphitheater:* about 70 BCE, one of the oldest surviving Roman amphitheaters, up to about 20,000 spectators (the park's figure). Most gladiators were enslaved people, prisoners of war or condemned criminals; some free men signed on for pay. The Colosseum opened in 80 CE under Titus; capacity estimates differ. Two sentences, no fight imagery.
- *The date:* 24 August comes from copies of Pliny the Younger's letters to Tacitus, written years later by an eyewitness at Misenum. A charcoal note found in 2018 reads "XVI K Nov" (17 October); if written in 79, the eruption came later, perhaps 24 October. The park's own charcoal note, written on the same wall on 12 October 2023, was still readable ten months later (August 2024), so the old one might be from 78. Open question, Keeper's Desk only.
- *Deaths:* Keeper's note only, in spec 139's words ("Many people fled; many others died"). No casts, bodies, counts or imagery.

**Latin in our words.** Spoken Latin changed over centuries into Spanish, French, Italian, Portuguese and Romanian; English borrowed many Latin words. Root families checked against Etymonline, the Académie française and Wiktionary: *aqua* to *agua*, *eau*, aquarium; *luna* to *luna*, *lune*, lunar; *pons* to *puente*, *pont*; *stella* to *estrella*, *étoile*, stellar; *panis* to *pan*, *pain*. Contrast: *moon* means the same as *luna* but comes from Old English *mona*, a Germanic word. **Basque**, around the western Pyrenees, is a language isolate (Britannica); the mainstream view is that early Basque predates the arrival of Indo-European languages, Latin among them, so the text says "most language experts think". Spec 135 already teaches *mile*.

**Roman law.** The Twelve Tables were drawn up about 450 BCE; the tablets are lost and parts survive in later writers' quotations. In the 500s CE Justinian had Roman law compiled, and that law later became the basis of legal practice in much of continental Europe.

**Christianity.** It spread through the empire over centuries. The Edict of Milan (313 CE, Constantine and Licinius) gave it legal toleration (Galerius had already ended persecution by edict in 311, so the child card says "two emperors agreed that Christians could worship freely"); the edict of Theodosius I and his co-emperors (380 CE) made Nicene Christianity the state religion. Marble's "under Constantine" is corrected to both steps. Factual and respectful.

**Greek thinkers (older depth).** Socrates taught by questioning and wrote nothing; we know him mainly through Plato and Xenophon (Britannica). Plato founded the Academy, where Aristotle (384 to 322 BCE) studied. Aristotle studied sea life in a lagoon on Lesbos and sorted animals into groups; his *History of Animals* stayed a main source into the 1500s. Hippocrates of Kos (about 460 to 370 BCE) taught that illness has natural causes such as diet and surroundings; about 60 works carry his name, by several authors. The four-humors theory appears in one book of that collection (*On the Nature of Man*); it was wrong, and scholars doubt Hippocrates held it, so the text says "one book in the Hippocratic collection". No item asks about the child's own health or body.

**The end of the western empire.** In 476 CE Odoacer removed the last western emperor, the boy Romulus Augustulus. Gibbon's *Decline and Fall* (1776 to 1789) made the date famous; few at the time seem to have treated it as an ending. Causes worked over many years: border pressure, civil wars, money troubles, an empire hard to hold. The eastern empire lasted until the Ottoman conquest of Constantinople in 1453. Historians disagree: Peter Brown's *The World of Late Antiquity* (1971) framed a gradual transformation; Bryan Ward-Perkins (2005) argued for real decline from the archaeology. The pack teaches both and decides neither.

## The lesson

Jane's order: **teach, then guided, then practice.**

1. **Teach card 1, four kinds of thing:** one idea (ask what kind of thing it is before you trust it) with a worked example revealed a step at a time: the wolf story (a story people told), the hut holes (found in the ground), Varro's date (written much later), the bronze wolf (made much later than the story).
2. **Teach card 2, what an object can and can't tell:** a Pompeii loaf, three steps: what you see, what it might tell us, what it cannot tell us.
3. **Teach card 3, words have families:** *aqua*, *agua*, *eau*, *aquarium*, one step at a time; then *water*, same meaning, different root.
4. **Guided item:** sort four cards into the four kinds, with up to four visible steps.
5. **Practice:** stations and problem sets, any order.

**"I'm stuck" ladder** on every practice item: a hint; then a worked example of an easier similar case plus how to start this one; then "Skip for now", with nothing lost and nothing recorded as failure.

**Top misconceptions designed against:** a story with a date is history (Varro's 753 BCE); a statue is as old as its story (the bronze wolf); Romans just renamed the Greek gods; ancient temples were plain white; an object tells everything about its users; an election notice shows who won; gladiators were stars who chose to fight; the eruption date is certain because an old book says so; words that mean the same share a root; every language around the Pyrenees comes from Latin; famous thinkers were right about everything; Christianity became official under Constantine; Rome fell on one day, or the whole empire ended in 476; if historians disagree, one side has no evidence.

## The game or activity

Seven stations, any order. Every card shows its label: **A true thing from history**, **A story people told**, **A story from Willowbank**, or, on the one reconstruction, **A picture of what experts think**. No timer, move count or score anywhere.

**Station 1, Story or ground?** Five cards (twins by the river, the she-wolf, the Palatine hut holes, Varro's date, the bronze wolf) dragged or tapped into four trays: *A story people told*, *Found in the ground*, *Written later*, *Made much later*. Feedback per card. The bronze wolf has a "two ages" flip.

**Station 2, Two Names.** A pairs game: 12 face-down cards, six gods, Greek and Roman cards sharing a symbol (thunderbolt, trident, owl, shell, winged sandals, hammer). A match stays up with a one-line role; nothing counts flips. Then two lone cards slide out: Janus and Apollo. Sprout may play 4 pairs.

**Station 3, The Temple on the Rock.** A plan view of the outer columns; each tap lights a column, corners light once, and the counter reaches 46. A toggle shows the labeled reconstruction (painted carvings, the lost statue; "Some colors are guesses"). A "long life" card follows the building from temple to ruin.

**Station 4, What the Ash Kept.** Four objects (a loaf, the oven of 81 loaves, a painted election notice, the amphitheater). Each opens three layers: *What you see*, *What it might tell us* (the child picks one of three), *What it can't tell us* (revealed after). Sprout sees the two shared cards (Vesuvius covered the town; the ash kept it); older depths add the Keeper's note and the date puzzle.

**Station 5, Word Roots.** A tree with Latin roots in the trunk and three branches: *Nuria's Spanish*, *Léon's French*, *English*. The child drags word leaves to the right branch; *moon* goes in a "not this family" pot with a line on why. Basque, Twelve Tables and Justinian cards sit beside the tree. Spanish and French always show English.

**Station 6, The Thinkers' Bench (older depth).** Socrates (with Plato and the Academy), Aristotle and Hippocrates cards, and a "right and wrong" flip on Hippocrates.

**Station 7, The Slow Ending.** A strip from 300 to 500 CE (313, 380, 476) with a far marker for 1453, plus "one day or slow change?" and "two historians" cards.
