A child learns: a colon opens a list when the words before it make a whole sentence; commas split a short list, and the comma before the last "and" is a choice; semicolons split list items that contain commas; bullets keep one style all the way down and start the same kind of way; a semicolon, colon or dash can join two whole sentences, each saying something different to a reader, and a comma alone cannot; in most school writing, book and newspaper titles go in italics (underlined by hand) and poems, chapters, stories and songs in quotation marks; headings, subheadings, columns, bullets and tables guide a reader. At Keeper's Desk: commas that change meaning (*that* and *which*), commas between coordinate adjectives, and the ellipsis.

## The knowledge

Sourced in the [audit](../source-audits/278-exchange-manifest.md). Child text uses American terms; the grown-up panel names England's.

**Colons and semicolons in lists.** England's Year 6 asks for "the colon to introduce a list and use of semi-colons within lists"; the Common Core places "a colon to introduce a list or quotation" at grades 9 to 10 (L.9-10.2b). Chicago and most school guides put a colon after a complete sentence that introduces a list and none between a verb and its object ("We need: apples" is avoided in running text). For a vertical list, Chicago still punctuates the lead-in as running text, while some house styles (Microsoft's) allow a colon after a fragment such as "You can:"; the pack keys the colon only in running sentences and names the difference for grown-ups. When list items contain commas, semicolons separate the items, including before the final *and* (AP and Chicago both show this). The serial comma in a simple list is a house choice (Chicago and MLA use it; AP generally does not), so the pack teaches it as a choice and never keys it.

**Bullet points.** England's Year 6: "punctuation of bullet points to list information." Guides disagree: Chicago leaves short fragment items without end punctuation and gives complete-sentence items periods and capitals; AP capitalizes each item and is reported to end each with a period; the UK government's GOV.UK style uses lower case, no semicolons and no full stop after the last bullet. The pack keys only consistency within one list and parallel form (each bullet starting the same kind of way), never one house style.

**Joining clauses.** England's Year 6: "the semi-colon, colon and dash to mark the boundary between independent clauses" (its example: *It's raining; I'm fed up*). Common Core L.9-10.2a: a semicolon links closely related independent clauses. A colon between clauses introduces an explanation or elaboration of the first; a dash makes a stronger, less formal break (L.8.2a: comma, ellipsis and dash for a pause or break). A comma alone between two independent clauses is a comma splice (269). Where several marks are correct for a stated sentence, every correct mark is accepted, and the bench says what each tells a reader.

**Titles of works.** Common Core L.5.2d: underlining, quotation marks or italics for titles. Chicago and MLA italicize books, newspapers, films and albums and put poems, short stories, chapters, songs and articles in quotation marks; APA differs for reference lists, many newspapers (AP) use quotation marks instead of italics, and long poems are a gray area. Underlining stands in for italics in handwriting. The pack teaches this common school convention with a hedge. Real titles used: Kenneth Grahame, *The Wind in the Willows* (1908), first chapter "The River Bank"; Robert Louis Stevenson, *A Child's Garden of Verses* (1885), poem "The Swing"; Rudyard Kipling, *Just So Stories* (1902), story "How the Whale Got His Throat." All public domain.

**Layout.** England's Year 6: "layout devices [for example, headings, sub-headings, columns, bullets, or tables, to structure text]"; Years 5 to 6 composition asks for organizational and presentational devices to guide the reader. A table compares items across shared questions, with row and column labels; columns set parallel lists side by side; bullets let a reader scan; numbered lists are for steps.

**Punctuation for clarity (ages 11 to 14).** KS3 asks pupils to use grammar and punctuation accurately and for effect. Common Core: L.6.2a (commas, parentheses, dashes for nonrestrictive elements), L.7.2a (a comma between coordinate adjectives: *a fascinating, enjoyable movie* but not *an old green shirt*), L.8.2a-b (ellipsis for a pause or an omission). Nonrestrictive clauses take commas; restrictive ones do not, and American usage prefers *that* for restrictive clauses. Coordinate adjectives pass the "and" and swap tests (Chicago). Semicolons, colons and dashes for clauses and lists are taught above; paired dashes and brackets are 264's.

## The lesson

Jane's **teach -> guided -> practice**: fifteen teach cards (12 core, 3 Keeper's Desk), one idea each with a three-step worked example revealed a tap at a time; each set's first item is guided with visible steps. **"I'm stuck" ladder** on every item: hint -> an easier case plus how to start this one (*We need two things: bread and jam*; *Paris, France; Rome, Italy*; three matching bullets; cover one side; whole book or part?; compare, follow or scan?; read it without the commas) -> skip for now, no penalty.

**Contrasts:** a whole-sentence lead-in vs *We need* (colon vs none); commas vs semicolons in one list (six bits vs three loads); serial comma vs none (both fine); matching vs mixed bullets; verb-first vs mixed bullets; semicolon vs colon vs dash vs period between the same two sentences; a whole sentence vs a *because* part; book vs chapter or poem; typed italics vs handwritten underline; table vs paragraph; two columns vs one mixed list; *which* with commas vs *that* without; *cold, wet* vs *old stone*; a full quote vs a shortened one.

## The game or activity

Every screen: *Marks tell a reader where one thing ends and the next begins. Try any mark. Nothing here is scored.* No score, timer, lock or mastery label. Drags have tap equivalents; tiles are at least 44 px.

- **(a) The Manifest List.** Six manifest lines (four market lines in the market version) with fixed slots; tiles: colon, semicolon, comma, leave empty. **Load it** shows Pip loading one crate per item, reading exactly the marks placed: commas in both semicolon slots of the cart line give six crates, one labeled TWO BOXES, and commas there on any other line get "Pip can't tell where one load ends and the next begins"; an empty slot after a lead-in makes him read the lead-in and first item as one; a colon after *We need* stops him mid-sentence. Every acceptable pattern is authored (slot 3 of a short list takes a comma or nothing); any other pattern gets the neutral line "Pip loads it just as it stands."
- **(b) Joining Dot's Notes.** Five pairs of clauses about the roads and the town, none from a page; tiles: period, semicolon, colon, dash, comma, nothing. **Read it** says what the chosen mark tells a reader. Every grammatical mark is accepted; a comma or nothing gets the splice or run-on note; a colon where the second part does not explain gets "does it explain?"; note 4 (*Because the river path floods*) takes a comma, and leaving it out gets "usually takes a comma", not a splice note.
- **(c) Dot's Manifest Page.** Choose a title, place five subheadings, choose a layout per part (bullets, a table, two columns, one paragraph; any choice is shown with a note), set one bullet style that applies to every bullet, and tap the odd bullet in two lists. The Ways table (manifest) and the cart-stop table (market) have labeled rows and columns. Title choices and the odd bullet change position between versions and lists.
- **(d) The Book Crate.** Eight titles (six real, two Willowbank); tap italics, quotation marks or plain; **Hand mode** swaps italics for underline.
- **(e) Clear It Up (Keeper's Desk).** Which baskets? (a picture of four baskets, two wet, against *that* and *, which,*); two adjectives (tap a comma slot or none); three dots (tap the sentence Dot's ellipsis stands for); the last word (a period or a dash, both correct, for effect). Then F4 off-screen.
