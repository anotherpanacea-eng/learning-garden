## Purpose

All eight targets are "not yet mapped" in `curriculum/topic-coverage.csv` and named nowhere under `docs/` on `main`. Strand **E2-III** asks for "precise vocabulary and register" and **E2-VI** for "choose language for a real audience; retain dialect respect"; **E5-III** asks for presenting with audience awareness and **E5-VI** for public explanation by a person, not a tap proxy ([six-act strands](../six-act-strands.md)). Built work stops short: the letter round ([specs 39](39-message-reader-spiral.md) and [40](40-changing-letters.md), `#post/letters`) changes what a letter *says* for a reader's needs, never how it *sounds*; nothing teaches formality, dialect or a speech.

A child learns: formal and informal language suit different readers and neither is better; formal writing often swaps a two-word verb for one word (find out, discover) and the swap must keep the meaning (request is not require); most letters and notices use standard verb forms (we were, I did), which are one dialect's forms, not the only correct English; formal and standard are separate questions, since a chat can use standard forms; a dialect belongs to a group of users and a register to a use; writers spell dialect to give a voice, and readers read for meaning; chat and formal writing differ in contractions, slang, tags and whole sentences; speakers change openings, words and closings for listeners; a short speech has an opening, points on the topic and a closing; some words serve many subjects and some one; and good language users notice their own choices.

## The knowledge

Sourced in the [audit](../source-audits/271-council-and-friend.md); unconfirmed claims were cut.

**Register and dialect.** England's grammar glossary distinguishes registers, "varieties" tied to a range of uses (a classroom lesson, a football commentary, a novel), from dialects, which are tied to groups of users. Standard English, it says, is recognized by a small range of forms (*those books*, *I did it*, *I wasn't doing anything*), is not limited to any accent, and covers most registers from casual to formal: "I did it cos they wouldn't do any more work" is its example of casual Standard English. Its stated aim is that everyone can use Standard English as needed in writing and in relatively formal speaking. Linguists describe Standard English as one dialect among many (Trudgill 1999). Common Core asks grade 5 to compare and contrast the varieties of English, dialects and registers, used in stories, dramas or poems (L.5.3b).

**Formal vocabulary and standard verbs.** England's Year 6 grammar asks for "the difference between vocabulary typical of informal speech and vocabulary appropriate for formal speech and writing", with the examples find out and discover, ask for and request, go in and enter; and the difference between informal and formal structures, such as question tags ("He's your friend, isn't he?"). All three vocabulary examples replace a two-word verb with one word. Year 4 asks for "Standard English forms for verb inflections instead of local spoken forms", with we were and I did. The pack keeps the curriculum's examples and adds only came and saw, chosen because 260 owns the forms as tense. A formal swap is only right if it keeps the meaning: to request is to ask; to require is to demand as necessary (Merriam-Webster).

**Dialect and respect.** Contrastive teaching treats home forms as rule-governed patterns and teaches standard forms as an addition for chosen settings, rather than correcting speech (Wheeler and Swords 2006; the Linguistic Society of America's 1997 resolution that such varieties are systematic and rule-governed). These forms are widespread: the eWAVE atlas finds was/were levelling ("you was hungry") in 41 of 77 varieties of English and past participles for past forms ("I seen it") in 43. Common Core L.6.1e asks students to recognize variations from standard English in their own writing and speaking; this pack takes only the writing half for a stated reader and never checks speech (owner default).

**Dialect in a story.** *The Secret Garden* (Frances Hodgson Burnett, 1911; public domain) gives Martha, Dickon and Mrs. Medlock Yorkshire speech. In chapter 7 Martha glosses her own "Nowt o' th' soart" as "nothin'-of-the-sort" and says it takes so long to say; in chapter 18 the narrator says Dickon spoke broad Yorkshire when very interested and at other times tried to modify his dialect so Mary could understand; the narrator says Mary loved his broad Yorkshire and was trying to learn it ("Aye, that we mun", glossed "Yes, indeed, we must"); Dickon speaks of spring ("Come springtime seems like as if all th' world's callin'"); and Mrs. Medlock speaks broad Yorkshire "because there was no one to hear her" (and because she is astonished). Martha's, Dickon's and Mrs. Medlock's switches are audience choices the book itself names. The book also frames Yorkshire through class: Martha says she is "too common" and talks "too much Yorkshire" to be let upstairs, the narrator calls her an "untrained Yorkshire rustic", and Mrs. Medlock forbids her Yorkshire. Chapter 18 also plays Mary's Yorkshire for fond laughs ("Tha'll make him laugh"). The pack leaves all of these out of child text, keys no item on Yorkshire being funny or lesser, and names the class framing to grown-ups.

**Spoken and written.** Key stage 3 asks for "the differences between spoken and written language, including differences associated with formal and informal registers, and between Standard English and other varieties of English", and for using Standard English confidently in writing and speech. The pack's chat features (question tags, contractions, text spellings, missing words) and formal ones (whole sentences, fewer contractions, linking adverbs such as therefore) are stated as tendencies, never rules.

**Speaking.** Key stage 3 asks pupils to use Standard English confidently "in a range of formal and informal contexts" and to give "short speeches and presentations, expressing their own ideas and keeping to the point"; Common Core asks grade 5 to adapt speech to contexts and tasks, using formal English when appropriate (SL.5.6). The speech frame (opening, two points, closing) is a teaching simplification. The EEF Toolkit's oral language entry reports about six months' additional progress on average.

**Academic vocabulary.** Beck, McKeown and Kucan's "tier two" words are high-utility words met across subjects; Coxhead's Academic Word List (2000) has 570 word families drawn from academic texts. The Word Bank's general words (estimate, predict, conclude, indicate, sufficient, evidence, from the family of evident, and impact) are on the list; floodplain is a domain word. Key stage 3 asks pupils to draw new vocabulary from reading and use it consciously; Common Core L.6.6 asks for accurate use of general academic and domain-specific words.

**Spanish (fluent reader).** Spanish marks formality in the singular pronoun: *tú* to a friend, *usted* formally. Letters customarily follow the greeting with a colon and may close *Atentamente*. The child text claims formality only for *tú*/*usted* and *Atentamente*; plural *les* (for *ustedes*) is not offered as a formality marker, since *ustedes* is the only plural "you" in Latin American Spanish.

## The game or activity

Every screen: *There is more than one good way to speak English. Here you choose words for a reader.* No score, timer, lock or mastery label. Drags have tap equivalents.

- **(a) Two Letters (Seedling, Sapling).** Two fixed letters with the same news; four tile gaps each (greeting, thanks or request, find out/discover, a closing or enter/go in). The fitting tile is first in four of the eight gaps and second in four, two of each per letter; the formal tile leads in four; tile length does not mark the fit; each gap's order is fixed. Each tile shows a reader-named feedback line; **Read both** highlights the shared news. Replies up the river: Nuria (*estás*) and the council (*Atentamente*), each beside English.
- **(b) The Letter Form (Seedling).** Four pairs, *many people say* beside *most letters write*, each "both are English". Items B1-B5 give a time word before any tense is keyed.
- **(c) Voices in Stories (Sapling).** Five line cards from *The Secret Garden* (four quoted exactly from the Project Gutenberg text, one marked retold), read aloud first; tap a word for its meaning; tap who is listening (each card names its speaker and listener). Two Willowbank notices for register in texts.
- **(d) Talk or Text? (Keeper's Desk).** Six feature cards into chat, formal writing or both; **Two dials** places one line on formality and on forms.
- **(e) The Speaking Corner (Sapling, Keeper's Desk).** **Who's Listening?** matches openings to three listeners. **Your Turn to Speak** lays four cards (opening, two points, closing) for the market welcome or, in Act IV ch. 4, the low fields (three plan cards with costs and a question card), then the child speaks to a grown-up. Nothing is stored.
- **(f) The Word Bank (Keeper's Desk).** Seven words met in the pack, each with a meaning and the line it came from; **Use it yourself** is spoken.
