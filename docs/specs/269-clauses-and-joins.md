## The knowledge

Sourced in the [audit](../source-audits/269-clauses-and-joins.md); terms follow the DfE grammar glossary, simplified, with US words in child text.

**Word classes and their jobs.** A conjunction links words, phrases or clauses: coordinating ones (*and, but, or*) link equals; subordinating ones (*because, when, if, although, until*) introduce a subordinate clause. A preposition links a following noun, pronoun or noun phrase to the rest of the sentence, often for place, direction or time. Some words, such as *before*, *after* and *since*, act as a preposition before a noun and as a conjunction before a clause. An interjection expresses a sudden feeling or reaction (*oh, oops, hooray*) and often stands apart from the sentence's grammar. Removing a preposition or conjunction changes or breaks meaning; removing an interjection removes only the feeling.

**Correlative conjunctions** come in pairs (*either... or, neither... nor, both... and, not only... but also, whether... or*). Usage guides ask that the words after each half match in grammatical form (parallel structure): *either by boat or on foot*, not *either by boat or walking*.

**Relative clauses.** A relative clause is a subordinate clause that modifies a noun, usually introduced by a relative pronoun (*who, which, that, whose*) or a relative adverb (*where, when, why*) that refers back to it; *that* is often omitted (*the prize I won*). *Who* is for people, *which* for things, *that* for either. Formal American style often prefers *that* for clauses that pick out which one and *which* (with commas) for added facts; Merriam-Webster's usage note says both *that* and *which* introduce restrictive clauses, so the preference is style, not a rule; British editors commonly allow *which* there too.

**Clauses and phrases.** In school usage a clause has a subject and a verb; a phrase is a word group without its own subject-verb pair (noun, verb, adjective, adverb, prepositional phrases). The DfE glossary is more technical (a clause is a phrase whose head is a verb); the grown-up panel says so. A main clause can stand alone; a subordinate clause depends on one. A prepositional phrase can work as an adverbial (*at dawn*). An opening modifier attaches to the next noun; when that noun is the wrong doer, the modifier dangles (*Rowing across the river, the letter got wet*).

**Sentence shapes.** Simple: one independent clause. Compound: two or more independent clauses, joined by a coordinating conjunction (usually with a comma before it in US style) or a semicolon. Complex: one independent clause and at least one dependent clause. Compound-complex: at least two independent and one dependent. Choosing among them signals how ideas relate (CCSS L.7.1b).

**Fragments and run-ons.** A fragment lacks a subject, a finite verb, or the main clause a subordinate clause needs. CCSS L.4.1f asks children to correct *inappropriate* fragments: fragments are normal in signs, notes, speech and deliberate style. A run-on (fused sentence) puts two independent clauses together without punctuation or a conjunction; a comma splice joins them with a comma alone. Many US school materials count both as run-ons, while some handbooks keep the terms apart; the pack teaches the school convention and the grown-up panel says so. Fixes: a period, a comma plus a coordinating conjunction, a subordinating conjunction, or a semicolon (taught in pack 278).

**Combining and trimming.** Expanding, combining and reducing sentences is a CCSS Grade 5 standard (L.5.3a). Sentence combining has research support: Saddler and Graham (2005) found peer-assisted combining improved fourth graders' combining and revising, the Writing Next meta-analysis (Graham and Perin 2007) lists it among effective practices, and EEF's Key Stage 2 guidance recommends it within sentence-construction practice. Myhill and colleagues' 2012 trial (744 secondary students, 31 schools) found grammar taught in the context of writing, through discussion of choices, helped writing, more so for able writers, while Writing Next found a small negative effect for traditional grammar taught in isolation. Appositives (*Léon, the ferry keeper,*) rename a noun beside it.

**Modal verbs and possibility.** The DfE glossary lists *will, would, can, could, may, might, shall, should, must* and *ought* as modal verbs, which change other verbs' meaning (certainty, ability, permission, obligation) and take no *-s* (*he must*, never *he musts*). Year 5 asks for degrees of possibility with modal verbs or adverbs (*perhaps, surely*). *Must* is ambiguous: a rule (obligation) or a confident deduction. For the past, *must have* is a confident deduction, *might have* or *may have* a possibility, and *can't have* a confident negative. The sureness line is a teaching simplification, labeled as a model.

## The game or activity

Every screen: *These are made-up sentences. Real writers break the rules on purpose sometimes.* No score, timer, lock or mastery label. Drags have tap equivalents. Supplied sentences only: no free-text entry, no grading of the child's own writing.

- **(a) Word Jobs (Sapling).** Tap colored words in three sentences: joining, linking a noun, or a feeling. **Take it out** hides one word and shows what breaks. **One word, two jobs:** *before* with a noun or a clause.
- **(b) Pairs That Travel Together (Sapling).** Match five pair halves; mend two slips in Pip's draft; **Same shape** swaps a mismatched half for a matching one.
- **(c) Who, Which, Where (Seedling).** Pick the pointer word, tap the noun it points to; **Join with a pointer** turns two sentences into one; **Name it** taps the relative clause and its relative pronoun.
- **(d) Mend the Letter (Seedling).** Pip's six-line reply: mark each line sentence, fragment or run-on; choose a fix from fixes that all work. Greeting and sign-off are fine as they are.
- **(e) Sentence Shapes (Sapling, Keeper's Desk).** Sort four Léon sentences by shape; phrase or clause; **Join and trim** Dot's notice; grow with a naming phrase; **Rhythm** (read two versions aloud, either preference fine); **Who is doing it?** (dangling openers, Keeper's Desk).
- **(f) How Sure? (Seedling to Keeper's Desk).** A sureness line (*can't, might, could, should, will, must*, labeled a made-up model). Clue cards at Seedling; Act IV cards at Sapling; **Swap it** for *perhaps, surely, certainly*; **Two jobs for must**; at Keeper's Desk, **What we know so far**, setting a word on each almanac line.
