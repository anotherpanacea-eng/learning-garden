## Purpose

Measurement has many pinned Marble topics on length and mass, and the youngest are untaught. The built Number Garden reads a ribbon on a ruler from zero and from a shifted start; [spec 45](45-measuring-recipes.md) reads a 50 mL jug scale; [spec 113](113-capacity-unit-bridge.md) shows that a smaller unit needs more of itself (mL and L); [spec 64](64-balance-stall.md) uses an equal-arm balance for equality; [spec 92](92-measuring-desk.md) and [spec 75](75-field-kit.md) send real jug readings off screen. Nobody teaches **how to compare two lengths fairly**, **why units must be equal and leave no gaps**, **how much longer**, **which is heavier and by how many units**, or **how to estimate a length from a benchmark**. This pack takes ten untouched topics there (eight assigned, two added at review).

A child comes to understand that a fair length comparison lines up one end first, and heights start from one floor; bending or moving a thing does not change its length; three things can be put in order, and a string can carry a length between two things that cannot meet; a measurement is a count of equal units laid end to end with no gaps or overlaps, always said with its unit; the same object gives more small units and fewer big ones; a ruler counts spaces from zero, and a broken ruler still works by end minus start; cm, m, mm and km fit different sizes of thing; "how much longer" is a difference in one unit; the heavier side of a balance goes down whatever the sizes; weight can be counted in equal cubes, then in grams and kilograms, and a scale is read by valuing one space first; and an estimate anchored on a benchmark, checked by measuring, gets better.

## The knowledge

**Direct comparison.** Length runs from one end to the other. To compare, line up one end of each at a shared start, then look at the far ends; the one that goes farther is longer. Heights compare from the same floor: a tower on a step only looks taller. Words: longer, shorter, taller, the same length; "bigger" and "wider" are not length words. **Conservation:** curling a rope brings its ends closer but leaves its length unchanged; straighten to check.

**Order and the go-between.** Order three by finding the shortest, then the shortest left (or place a new one into a line). If blue is longer than red and red longer than gold, blue is longer than gold. A string pinched to one length carries it to something that cannot be moved; it must not be stretched, cut or re-aligned between the two comparisons.

**Equal units.** A measurement counts same-size units laid end to end, with no gaps (uncounted length) and no overlaps (length counted twice), and always names the unit ("8 clips long"). Mixed sizes make the count meaningless. **The unit sets the count:** smaller units, more of them; the object does not change. Same count with different units means different lengths (10 small clips are shorter than 10 big ones). Two people with different hand spans get different counts for one bench, which is why people agree on standard units. Inches appear once: a book of 25 cm is about 10 inches, because an inch is the bigger unit.

**Rulers and standard units.** Put the start at 0 and read the end; count spaces, not marks. A broken ruler works by counting spaces from the start or end − start (recap of the built Number Garden). A fingertip is about 1 cm wide; 10 mm make 1 cm, 100 cm make 1 m, 1000 m make 1 km. Choose the unit that fits the size: mm for a ladybug, cm for a pencil, m for a door, km for a walk between towns. Conversion beyond this recap belongs to spec 201.

**How much longer.** Measure both in the same unit, then subtract the shorter from the longer, and give the answer with its unit ("15 cm longer"). Drawn as two bars from one start, the overhang is the difference. A mixed pair (2 m and 150 cm) must go to one unit first: 50 cm, never 148.

**Weight.** Weight is felt, not seen: a small tin can outweigh a big pillow. On a balance the heavier side goes down; level means the same weight. Weighing with units: the number of identical cubes that balance an object, so 7 cubes is heavier than 5 of the same cubes. Bigger units, smaller count (a loaf is 4 big weights or 12 small ones and stays one loaf). Grams for light things, kilograms (1000 g) for heavy ones. **Reading a scale:** find what one space is worth (100 g over 5 spaces is 20 g), then count spaces from the last label (3 past 200 is 260 g). Mass and weight are used interchangeably at this level, as the research records allow.

**Estimating lengths.** An estimate is said before measuring and anchored on a **benchmark**: fingertip about 1 cm, a grown-up's big step about 1 m, a door about 2 m, Bramble's hand span 15 cm (made-up). Iterate the benchmark in your head ("three or four doors lying down"), commit, then measure and say over or under, by how much, and adjust. A wild number with no benchmark (a door "20 m") is the main error designed against.

**Real anchors (see audit).**
- **The Egyptian royal cubit.** Royal cubit rods are about 52.4 cm (sources say 52.4 to 52.5), divided into 7 palms of 4 fingers each, 28 fingers. Two rods came from the Saqqara tomb of Maya, treasurer under Tutankhamun; one is in the Louvre (18th dynasty, 14th century BCE).
- **Harappan weights.** In the Indus cities (Mature Harappan, about 2600-1900 BCE), weights were mostly cubes, most of chert, on one standard across cities. The small series doubles (1, 2, 4, 8, 16...); the most common weight is about 13.7 g, the 16 in that series, and larger weights go up by tens. Copper balance pans are reported from Mohenjo-daro and Harappa. Why they were used (trade, taxes) is interpretation, kept out of child text.
- **The meter of 1799.** Delambre and Méchain surveyed the meridian arc from Dunkirk to Barcelona (1792-1798); the meter was defined as one ten-millionth of the pole-to-equator distance, and a platinum bar, the *mètre des Archives*, was placed in the French National Archives in 1799 (22 June in most sources; one gives July, so child text says only 1799).

## The game or activity

**(a) Line-Up Gate, model `lineup-v1`.** Planks, towers and ropes as pictures by a gate post. **Slide to the post**, **Move to the floor**, **Straighten** and **Curl**; **Which is longer?** asks for a tap. **Pip's turn**: Pip compares staggered planks or a tower on a step and the child fixes it. *A made-up model: pictures slide but never stretch.* Planks in A5 are 5, 3 and 4 blocks long (lengths hidden at Sprout and Seedling).

**(b) String Carrier, model `string-carry-v1`.** **Copy this length** pinches a string to an object; **Carry** moves it to a fixed object (the bakery door 200 cm, the shelf 150 cm in the model). **Order** mode puts three ribbons in a staircase; **Insert** asks where a new one goes. The string cannot be re-pinched during a carry. *A made-up model: the string never stretches or shrinks.*

**(c) Unit Path, model `unit-path-v1`.** Lay cubes (15 cm), blocks (30 cm) or clips (small 3 cm, big 5 cm) along the bakery bench (120 cm: 4 blocks, 8 cubes) or a strip. Gaps show as bare floor and overlaps as a raised double, each with a text label; the count line totals only a clean row ("8 clips long") and otherwise names the gap, overlap or mixed sizes. **Use the other unit** keeps the bench and shows both counts side by side; **Same count?** lays 10 small and 10 big clips on two tables. *A made-up model: unit sizes as stated; real clips vary.*

**(d) Tape Table, model `tape-ruler-v1`.** A drawn ruler (0-30 cm with mm marks), a meter tape (0-5 m) and a broken ruler starting at 4. **Slide to zero**, **Zoom to mm**, **Measure both** and **How much longer?**, which draws both lengths from one start and labels the overhang. A unit chip on every reading keeps cm and m apart; mixed pairs prompt "same unit first". *A made-up model: drawn rulers, not real measuring.*

**(e) Bakery Scales, model `bake-scale-v1`.** The baker's equal-arm balance and a dial scale (0-500 g, labels every 100 g, 5 spaces between). **Hold both** shows a picture-and-text heft cue; **Put on the balance** tips the heavier side down, with a status line that never relies on motion or color alone. **Add a cube** weighs in identical cubes; **Use big weights** (150 g) beside small (50 g) for the 600 g loaf. Pairs include small-heavy and big-light. *A made-up model: weights as stated; a screen cannot give the feel of weight.*

**(f) Benchmark Card, model `benchmark-v1`.** The card: fingertip about 1 cm, Bramble's hand span 15 cm, a grown-up's big step about 1 m, a door about 2 m. **My estimate** must be entered (or skipped) before **Measure**; then an **estimate, measured, over or under by how much** strip. Things to estimate include the door (200 cm), the banner (60 cm), the café front (7 m 40 cm). *A made-up model: Bramble's span is invented; a child's own span is their own.*

No timers, scores, streaks, medals or locks. Every stop is open from the first visit.
