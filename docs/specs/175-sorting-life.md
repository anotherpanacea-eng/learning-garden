## Purpose

In **Ecosystems & Habitats** and **Organisms & Life Processes** the classification spine is untouched in `curriculum/topic-coverage.csv`: every target below is "not yet mapped" and unclaimed by any spec or draft. Nothing teaches the five vertebrate groups, the broad groups of living things, reasons for a classification, scientific names, or the evidence for shared ancestry.

A child comes to understand that animals are grouped by bodies, not homes or movement; that the big groups rest on how each gets food and how big it is; that a classification needs two decisive reasons; that a key is a chain of yes/no splits; that one animal has one scientific name in every language; and, at older depth, that bones, islands and DNA agree, that ranges move when places change, and that a gene is a section of DNA.

## The knowledge

Sourced in the audit. Willowbank, the card box and every model are fiction, labeled so.

**Bodies, not homes.** Vertebrates (animals with a backbone) form five groups by body features: **mammals** (hair or fur, young fed on milk, most born live), **birds** (feathers, beak, eggs with hard shells), **fish** (gills, fins, most with scales, live in water), **reptiles** (dry scaly skin, most lay eggs on land), **amphibians** (moist skin without scales, young usually start in water). Habitat and movement mislead: a dolphin lives in the sea and is a mammal (breathes air, feeds young milk); a bat flies and is a mammal (fur, milk; the only mammals capable of true flight); a penguin cannot fly and is a bird; a fire salamander looks like a lizard and is an amphibian. The platypus is a mammal that lays eggs: it has hair and its young drink milk from pores on the mother's skin (Keeper's note). The five groups are one useful scheme: in family-tree classification birds sit inside the reptile branch (draft 173); a grown-up note only.

**Big groups.** **Plants** make their own food from light. **Animals** eat other living things and can move at some stage of life; corals are animals (many small animals called polyps; partner algae live in them, but corals do not photosynthesize). **Fungi** (mushrooms, molds, yeasts) cannot make their own food; they release digestive juices and absorb what they break down. Whittaker's five-kingdom scheme (1969) gave fungi their own kingdom; later DNA work found them closer to animals than to plants. **Micro-organisms** are too small to see without a microscope (bacteria, many pond-water cells). Schemes change with evidence.

**Good reasons.** A decisive feature separates the groups being compared (feathers, milk, gills, makes its own food). A shared feature cannot decide (sharks and dolphins both have fins). Irrelevant features never decide (color, size, name, home). A good classification gives a claim, two pieces of evidence and a reason: "a mammal, because it has fur and feeds its young milk, and only mammals do both."

**Keys, tables and graphs.** A key asks one yes/no question about something visible at each step. Each question splits one group into two, so one thing per ending needs one question fewer than the things (6 animals, 5 questions). A feature table records the same evidence. Graphs follow the data: categories take a bar graph, one measure over time a line graph, two measures across many individuals (beak depth against seed hardness) a scatter graph.

**Names.** Carl Linnaeus published *Systema Naturae* in 1735, used two-part names for plants throughout *Species Plantarum* (1753) and extended them to animals in the tenth edition of *Systema Naturae* (1758), the agreed starting point for animal names. The first part names the genus, the second the species. The white-throated dipper is *Cinclus cinclus* in every language; in Spanish it is *mirlo acuático*, in French *cincle plongeur*.

**Evidence for the long change** (Keeper's Desk; Sapling preview). Independent lines of evidence agree. **Bones:** the bat's wing, whale's flipper and horse's leg hold the same forelimb bones (one upper, two lower, wrist, fingers) shaped for different jobs: homologous structures, a sign of a shared ancestor. A butterfly's wing does the bat wing's job with no matching bones (analogous), so a shared job alone shows no kinship. **Islands:** the Galápagos are volcanic, about 900 to 1,000 km off South America, never joined to it. Darwin spent about five weeks there from September 1835. At least 13 kinds of finch live there (13 to 18 by source), descended from one ancestor. From 1973 Peter and Rosemary Grant measured finches on Daphne Major. In the 1977 drought small seeds ran out; medium ground finches with deeper beaks could crack the large, hard seeds left. Survivors' beaks averaged about half a millimeter deeper than the population's before (Boag and Grant 1981, *Science*), and their young had deeper beaks on average. No bird's beak grew; the group's average shifted. **Fossils:** draft 173. **DNA:** the hippopotamus is the whale's closest living relative; *Indohyus* (about 48 million years old, Kashmir) is a close fossil relative. Darwin and Wallace's papers were read on 1 July 1858 (draft 161). No "more advanced" language; human ancestry is not opened.

**Genes, gently** (Keeper's Desk only). Nearly every plant or animal cell has a nucleus (draft 160) holding chromosomes. Each chromosome is one very long DNA molecule coiled around proteins; a gene is a section of it carrying instructions that affect a feature. Chromosome numbers differ between kinds; no human numbers or human inheritance examples. **History:** in May 1952 at King's College London, Raymond Gosling, working with Rosalind Franklin, took the X-ray image called Photo 51. Maurice Wilkins showed it to James Watson without Franklin's knowledge. Watson and Francis Crick's double-helix model appeared in *Nature* in 1953 beside papers by Wilkins's group and by Franklin and Gosling. Watson, Crick and Wilkins shared the 1962 Nobel Prize. Franklin's death stays in the grown-up panel.

**Moving ranges** (Sapling, Keeper's Desk). When a mountain warms, the band of suitable conditions can move uphill; populations follow as young settle where it now suits and do poorly at the warm edge. Animals do not decide to move. A summit species can run out of mountain; a population can vanish from one place (local extinction) and survive elsewhere. In Spain's Sierra de Guadarrama, butterfly communities in 2004-05 sat about 293 m higher than comparable ones in 1967-73 (Wilson et al. 2007). Patterns differ: a 2001-2021 study of 177 bird species in four European ranges found about 63 percent moved uphill overall, with no significant shift in the Pyrenees. Habitat change (151) and invasives (166) also move ranges.

## The game or activity

Every screen: *These are simplified pictures. Real animals vary.* No score, timer, lock or mastery label. Drags have tap equivalents; 44 px targets.

- **(a) Five Trays.** Twelve cards from the spilled box. **Free sort:** the child picks a rule chip (lives in water, flies, has legs, my own idea) and sorts into two to four trays; nothing is marked. **Scientists' trays:** five labeled trays; each placement shows its deciding feature. **Tricky cards** (dolphin, bat, penguin, fire salamander) come last with "What would you check?"
- **(b) Big Groups.** Four bins that respond to the child's answers (*Makes its own food? Seen without a microscope? Eats other living things?*), never to the picture. Cards: oak, moss, field mushroom, bread mold, coral, a magnifiable pond drop, soil bacteria, trout. **Looks like, but isn't:** mushroom and moss, coral and seaweed.
- **(c) Prove It.** A claim-evidence-reason frame for look-alike pairs (dolphin and shark, bat and dipper, fire salamander and wall lizard): shared chips gray out; the child pins two decisive ones. Then **Fix Bramble's poster.**
- **(d) The Branch Book.** **Follow** a five-question key for six valley animals (dipper, bat, otter, trout, wall lizard, common frog), Go back at every step. **Fill the table** of features beside the key. **Build** a three-animal key from candidate questions, some that split and some that do not (116's pattern, vertebrates). **Which graph?** three datasets, three graph cards. **Names card:** the two letters and *Cinclus cinclus*.
- **(e) The Long Change (Keeper's Desk).** **Bone Plan:** tap a bone on the bat wing, whale flipper or horse leg and its match lights on all three (four colors); a butterfly wing comes last: *Same job. No matching bones.* **Island Beaks:** a Galápagos map, three beak cards and foods ([check] examples), then the **Daphne drought model** (made-up: beaks of 8, 9, 10 and 11 mm; only 10 mm or deeper crack the hard seeds and raise young; the average goes from 9.5 to 10.5 mm; real survivors averaged about half a millimeter deeper). **Four lines** (bones, islands, 173's fossils, whale-hippo DNA) pin to one claim. **The Zoom:** cell to gene, a tap per level, then the Photo 51 file.
- **(f) The Moving Band.** A made-up mountain (summit 2,800 m) with a "cool enough" band from 2,400 to 2,600 m. Each **Warmer** tap lifts the band 100 m and a butterfly population follows; at step four the band's lower edge reaches the summit. *Made-up: real slopes are uneven and species need food plants too.* Then two real cards.
