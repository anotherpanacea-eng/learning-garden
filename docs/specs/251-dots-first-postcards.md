## Purpose

None is a spec target on `main`; all seven are "not yet mapped" in `curriculum/topic-coverage.csv`. A child learns: a space marks where a word stops, and words can be counted by them; names of people and places, the days of the week and I take capitals, and a job word (the baker) does not; a capital can change meaning (a dot, Dot; a pip, Pip); the Year 1 words letter, capital letter, word, sentence, punctuation, period, question mark, exclamation mark, singular and plural; that letters which start alike form families, with the honest caveat that schools sort them differently; that capitals are tall and reach the top line; the tricky part of common exception words; and how to listen, say back, write, then check a supplied line on paper.

## The knowledge

Sourced in the [audit](../source-audits/251-dots-first-postcards.md). Child text uses American terms (period, tricky word); the grown-up panel names England's (full stop, common exception words).

**Spaces.** England's Year 1 punctuation content: separation of words with spaces; its Year 1 grammar statement: "leaving spaces between words". Common Core RF.K.1c: understand that words are separated by spaces in print. Counting words by the spaces is Marble's own evidence item. Not every writing system spaces words: Thai, Chinese and Japanese are usually written without word spaces, which is why software needs dictionaries to find Thai word breaks (Unicode line-breaking practice). A "finger space" is a classroom habit, not a rule; left-handed writers may use a spacer.

**Capitals for names, days and I.** England's Year 1: a capital letter for names of people, places, the days of the week, and the personal pronoun I. Common Core L.K.2a: capitalize the first word in a sentence and the pronoun I; L.1.2a: capitalize dates and names of people. English style guides and dictionaries capitalize the days; Spanish and French write them in lowercase (*lunes*, *lundi*), so child text says "in English" and the grown-up panel names the contrast (flagged for a fluent reader). Job words used as common nouns (the baker, the miller) stay lowercase; spec 120 writes Willowbank's supporting cast that way. Many cast names are also common nouns (dot, pip, bramble, ember, puff); the capital marks the name, so every item that keys a name gives a cue card saying who is meant. Cue cards are a picture plus read-aloud words, never printed text, so a cue never shows the capital it asks for. *Keeper of the Ways* is written as a title for a person, so Keeper and Ways take capitals.

**Year 1 terms.** England's Appendix 2, Year 1 terminology for pupils: letter, capital letter, word, singular, plural, sentence, punctuation, full stop, question mark, exclamation mark; and "how words can combine to make sentences." *Letter* has two senses (an alphabet letter, and a message sent by mail); item C6 fixes the sense with a stated cue before keying. *a* and *I* are both letters and words, so the Word Shelf keeps them out of its sort. Child text defines a letter as one sign from the alphabet (a period is a mark, not a letter), a word as one or more letters that mean something, and a space as the gap *between* two words, so the A4 fix ("Add a space between words") followed literally never puts a space before a period.

**Letter families.** England's Year 1 handwriting: "understand which letters belong to which handwriting 'families' (ie letters that are formed in similar ways) and to practise these." The curriculum names no families. A widely used UK grouping has four: letters that start like c (c, a, d, o, g, q, and in many lists e, s and f), letters that start with a straight downstroke (l, i, t, u, j, y), letters that go down, back up the same line and over or round (r, n, m, h, b, p, k), and slanted letters (v, w, x, z). Ladder and robot letters both begin with a downstroke; what tells them apart is whether the pencil goes back up that line, so the pack plays each letter's whole stroke sequence when sorting and never sorts by the first stroke alone, often called curly caterpillar, long ladder, one-armed robot and zig-zag monster. Lists disagree on e, s and f; k and y are written in more than one shape, so their family depends on the shape taught. The pack sorts only the letters the common lists agree on and leaves e, s, f, k and y unsorted, saying why. Stroke order and letter shapes also vary by school and program; the pack draws one plain print model, newly made, with no lead-in strokes, and the grown-up panel says to follow the school's if it differs. Every stroke claim in child text must be true of that model: c, a, d, o, g, q start near the top of a curve going left; l, i, t, u, j start at the top with a downstroke and do not retrace it; r, n, m, h, b, p go down and retrace up before going over or round; v, w, x, z each include a slant; every capital reaches the top line, and D starts there.

**Capitals.** England's Year 1: form capital letters; Common Core L.K.1a and L.1.1a: print many, then all, upper- and lowercase letters. Shape facts the screen can show: in plain print E, H and L use only straight lines; O, C and S only curves; B, D and P both. Capitals reach the top line, as tall lowercase letters (b, d, l) do; a, c and o stop at the middle. The pack does not claim one correct start point for every capital.

**Tricky words and the days.** England's Year 1 spelling: common exception words and the days of the week; Appendix 1's Year 1 list (the, a, do, to, today, of, said, says, are, were, was, is, his, has, I, you, your, they, be, he, me, she, we, no, go, so, by, my, here, there, where, love, come, some, one, once, ask, friend, school, put, push, pull, full, house, our) with the note that attention should go to the parts that do and do not fit the phonics taught. Appendix 1's Year 2 work lists *Monday* among words where o spells the vowel of *up*. The EEF's key stage 1 literacy guidance suggests teaching spellers to identify the "tricky" part of a word. The pack keys a tricky part only where it holds across common American and British accents (ai in said, ie in friend, o in come, some, love and Monday, the silent final e in come, some and love); Merriam-Webster gives Wednesday without its first d or its second e in American speech (\ˈwenz-\) and lists a British form with the d, so the pack keys both letters and says "most people don't say this d or this e". Words whose irregular part shifts with accent (was, are, our, there, and so on) get whole-word cards with no keyed part and no claim about which part is tricky. Common Core L.1.2d: conventional spelling of frequently occurring irregular words.

**Dictation.** England's Year 1 and Year 2: write from memory simple sentences dictated by the teacher that include words using the GPCs, common exception words and punctuation taught so far. Every tricky word in the Postcard Desk's nine lines is on England's Year 1 list or is a day, and the lines use only the punctuation in spec 90 (no commas, apostrophes or speech marks). The routine (listen, say back, count the words, write, check) is a common classroom one; the EEF recommends extensive, purposeful transcription practice. Santangelo and Graham's 2016 meta-analysis found handwriting instruction improved legibility and fluency and also the quality and length of writing; this is why the paper practice matters even though the app never sees it.

## The lesson

Jane's **teach -> guided -> practice**: fourteen teach cards, one idea each with a three-step worked example revealed a tap at a time; each set's first item is guided with visible steps. **"I'm stuck" ladder** on every item: hint -> an easier case plus how to start this one (Pipnaps; i see dot; d, Dot, Dot naps; c starting round; said; check one thing at a time) -> skip for now, no penalty.

**Contrasts:** no gaps against gaps inside words against a gap after each word; a dot against Dot, a pip against Pip; day against Monday; the baker against a name; a mill against Willowbank; the first word against a name in the middle; a letter against a word against a sentence; letter as a mark against letter as mail; period, question mark and exclamation mark on one line; hat against hats against glass; starts like c, straight down, down and over, slanting; straight, curvy or both; sounds-right spellings against book spellings; Pip's chart against Dot's chart.

**Misconceptions tagged on choices:** `no-spaces`, `space-inside-word`, `mark-fine`, `random-capital`, `day-word-capital`, `place-word-is-name`, `job-is-name`, `day-lowercase`, `name-lowercase`, `first-word-only`, `long-word-capital`, `word-is-letter`, `sentence-is-letter`, `phrase-is-sentence`, `word-is-sentence`, `mark-mixup`, `one-many`, `s-means-plural`, `one-sense-only`, `ladder-for-curly`, `zigzag-for-curly`, `curve-missed`, `one-true-scheme`, `sound-spelling`, `letter-order`, `wrong-tricky-part`, `every-letter-sounded`, `spaces-fine`. Spelling distractors get sentence-specific feedback ("Books spell it said"); no feedback says a distractor "isn't a word" (F2's *tome* is a real word, and the feedback says so).

## The game or activity

Every screen: *Writing happens on paper, with a grown-up. The screen only shows. Nothing you write is saved.* No score, timer, lock or mastery label. Drags have tap equivalents; targets are at least 44 px; every letter animation has a text description of its strokes and a reduced-motion still sequence.

- **(a) The Space Bench (first slice).** Five squashed postcards, each with exactly one reading (hand-checked: no line hides a second split such as "them ill"). The child taps the gaps between letters; Pip points to each piece he can see, and reads the line aloud (authored audio) only once every gap fits. Then **Count the words**. A paper card asks for a finger space.
- **(b) The Capital Stamp (first slice).** Six lines with cue cards; tap each word that needs a capital and Dot's stamp changes its first letter; taps can be undone. Feedback by word kind (name, day, I, job, other). The first word already has its capital (spec 90's).
- **(c) The Word Shelf.** Letter, word or sentence (six cards); name the marks on one postcard; singular or plural (five cards, including *glass*).
- **(d) The Slate.** Four family tabs plus a "sorted in different ways" tab; each letter plays its strokes from a start dot, one stroke per tap. **Which family?** sorts eight letters with their whole strokes played. **Capital corner** pairs each capital with its small letter; **Straight, curvy or both?** sorts nine capitals; the tall-line card. A paper card closes the bench. No input on the letters themselves.
- **(e) The Tricky Word Mailbox.** **Find the tricky part** for seven words (taps on letters, keyed as above); whole-word cards for the rest; **Look, say, cover, write, check** on paper, with a Show button for the child's own comparison (nothing kept); the days card.
- **(f) The Postcard Desk.** Nine supplied lines at three depths. A grown-up opens the line, reads it twice and covers it; the child says it back, counts words on fingers, writes on paper, then taps **Show the line** to compare against a look-for list (word count, spaces, capitals, end marks, tricky words). **Mail it** keeps only the line's id. Spoken self-checks are never recorded.
