## Purpose

[Spec 152](152-up-through-the-canopy.md) teaches what a rainforest is, where rainforests grow, the four layers, plant adaptations, the forest's rain, thin soil, Indigenous knowledge and everyday foods. [Spec 144](144-ways-of-the-birds.md) gives the rainforest one postcard and claims the Animals-of-the-World *Rainforest Animals* topic. Neither teaches the rainforest's **animals** as a subject: what it feels like on the floor, the insect crowd, sorting rainforest life into groups, how animals survive there, the Amazon as a place, biodiversity as a concept, or how rainforest goods reach a market. Seven Rainforests topics on the pinned graph cover exactly these and are unclaimed.

A child comes to understand that the rainforest floor is dim since the treetops catch the sunlight, sticky since warm air there holds lots of water, loud since hidden animals call, and dripping since rain keeps sliding off the leaves; that insects are its biggest crowd, and leaf-cutter ants grow fungus on cut leaves and eat the fungus; that rainforest life sorts into groups by body features, plants included, with a key for tricky cases; that features help animals survive by hiding, warning, gripping, reaching or waking at night, and are passed down rather than chosen; that the Amazon spans nine countries and territories and its river carries the most water of any; and, at older depth, that biodiversity means variety, that a small slice of land holds a big share of known kinds, and that everyday goods trace back to rainforests along chains that buyers' choices can push.

## The knowledge

Sourced in the audit. Willowbank, the set, the puppets and every model are fiction, labeled so.

**Inside the forest (Sprout).** Under a thick canopy only a small share of sunlight reaches the floor (152 teaches the 1 to 5% range), so the floor is dim at midday. The air is warm all year and full of water: humid, so skin feels damp and sticky. Frogs, birds, monkeys and insects call day and night. Rain and dew keep running off leaves long after a shower. Each sense is taught with its cause; "humid" comes after "sticky". Contrast: Willowbank's willow wood, cooler, brighter and quieter.

**The insect crowd (Sprout to Seedling).** Insects are the most numerous animals in the rainforest. Leaf-cutter ants (Central and South America) cut leaves and carry them to an underground fungus garden; the fungus feeds the colony, especially the young; workers also drink leaf sap. The leaves themselves are not the ants' food. Gallery: titan beetle (about 16.7 cm, among the largest beetles), blue morpho (bright upper wings, brown underneath), stick insects (living insects shaped like twigs), and the goliath birdeater, the heaviest spider, with eight legs: a minibeast, not an insect. Most rainforest insects are harmless to people and do vital work.

**Sorting rainforest life (Seedling to Sapling).** Groups by defining body features, never home, size, fierceness or movement: mammals (fur, babies fed milk: jaguar, howler monkey, bat, sloth), birds (feathers: toucan, macaw, hummingbird), reptiles (dry scales: boa, iguana, caiman), amphibians (moist skin: tree frog, poison dart frog), insects (six legs: morpho, leaf-cutter ant, titan beetle) and plants (make their own food: kapok, orchid, fern). Near misses carry the lesson: the bat flies but is a mammal; caiman and tree frog both live by water; hummingbird and morpho both sip nectar. The key asks six yes/no questions in a fixed order; the spider ends on an "other minibeasts" shelf. The rules are typical patterns, and the model line names an exception (egg-laying mammals). Classification lets scientists keep track of enormous variety.

**Rainforest Animals, supported inline.** The seven animals in that topic's description carry layer tags on the puppets: jaguar and poison dart frog on the floor, morpho in the understory, sloth, toucan and howler monkey in the canopy, macaw in the canopy and emergent layer. Layers themselves are 152's.

**Survival tricks (Seedling to Sapling).** Always feature, then job, then survival. Camouflage hides an animal against the right background and fails on a mismatched one; warning colors do the opposite and advertise poison (wild poison frogs get their toxins from their insect food, so the card speaks of wild frogs). Gripping (prehensile) tails, found in spider and howler monkeys, can hold the whole body's weight and free both hands. A toucan's long beak reaches fruit on twigs too thin for its weight. Many rainforest animals are nocturnal, which can hide them from hunters, give them food with less competition, or for some keep them cool. Features are passed down over generations; no animal chooses or stretches its way into one. Bates's mimicry (harmless butterflies that resemble bad-tasting ones) is a Sapling history card.

**The Amazon (Seedling to Sapling).** Map first: South America; nine countries and territories (Brazil, Peru, Colombia, Venezuela, Ecuador, Bolivia, Guyana, Suriname, and French Guiana, part of France); most of it in Brazil. The Amazon River carries more water than any other, roughly a fifth of the world's river flow to the sea (sources give 15 to 20%; not in child text); which river is longest is a separate, debated question. About 1 in 10 known species; at least 40,000 plant species, about 1,300 bird species and about 2,700 fish species (the topic description says 3,000; described counts run from about 2,260 to 2,720, so the card uses the larger checked count), all estimates that keep rising. Forest and river are kept apart: the forest is the home, and the river crosses it. Two numbers per screen at most.

**Biodiversity (Sapling to Keeper's Desk).** Biodiversity is variety, not abundance. Tropical rainforests cover about 6% of Earth's land (sources range from 2 to 7%) and hold over half of known plant and animal species (some sources say more); both caveats ("tropical", "known") are stated. One average upland hectare in Yasuní, Ecuador holds about 655 tree species, more than are native to the continental United States and Canada together. Many rainforest species are endemic, so clearing a small area can lose kinds for good; that is why protection is a priority. Told briefly and without alarm. The 100-square bench illustrates concentration only and says loss is not exactly proportional to area.

**From forest to shelf (Sapling to Keeper's Desk).** Products sit at the end of chains, traced both ways: rubber (latex from *Hevea*, native to the Amazon, now mostly grown in Southeast Asia), cacao (152's card, revisited), palm oil (hidden in some snacks, soaps and lipsticks, often under other names), timber, and soy (about 77% of the world's soy is fed to livestock; child text says "most"). In the Amazon most cleared land becomes cattle pasture, with soy, palm oil, logging and mining adding more. Demand pulls the chain, so buyers' choices push back: framed as agency, through the café's chocolate, never the child's household. "Never buy any" can harm smallholders and remove the reward for good practice; oil palm yields far more oil per field than other oil crops, so swaps can need more land; a certification label is a useful signal, not a guarantee. No brand or scheme is named.

**History cards (real, checked).**
- **Olmec rubber (about 1600 BCE).** Solid rubber balls from El Manatí, Veracruz, made from *Castilla elastica* latex mixed with morning glory juice for bounce.
- **Maria Sibylla Merian (1699 to 1701, book 1705).** Sailed from Amsterdam to Suriname with her younger daughter Dorothea, watched caterpillars change, painted each stage on its food plant; *Metamorphosis insectorum Surinamensium*, on Suriname's insects and plants. Indigenous and enslaved African helpers supplied knowledge and specimens and are unnamed in her book (Sapling card). The early return (illness) is left out.
- **Henry Walter Bates (1848 to 1859).** Went to the Amazon with Alfred Russel Wallace; sent home over 14,000 species, mostly insects, many new to European science (his own count of 8,000 new is disputed, so not used); described mimicry in 1861 (Sapling card, no date in child text).
- **Henry Wickham (1876, Keeper's Desk).** Carried about 70,000 *Hevea* seeds from Brazil to Kew; seedlings went to Asia, where most rubber now grows. Told plainly; "smuggling" is contested and left to the grown-up panel.

## The game or activity

**(a) The Canopy Stage, model `stage-light-v1`.** *A made-up model.* Treetops `none`, `thin`, `thick` show the floor `Bright`, `Dim`, `Dark and shady`; only `thick` is a rainforest floor. A sound board (drip, buzz, howler whoop, macaw squawk, frog chorus), a **Sticky air** mist button, and four sense-and-cause flip cards. The light control is the only part that changes with input; the other senses are described. Model line: "Real treetops let through a little light, in patches. This stage has three settings."

**(b) Ant Trail and Bug Gallery.** Five gallery cards (one a spot-the-stick-insect picture), a four-step ant trail (cut, carry, feed the fungus, the fungus feeds the nest), a leg counter, and a **one-tree tally**, model `one-tree-v1`: 48 insects, 4 frogs, 3 birds, 2 monkeys on one made-up tree. Model line: "A made-up tree. A real tree can hold many thousands of insects."

**(c) Puppet Trays, model `rainforest-key-v1`.** Nineteen puppets into six trays and a side shelf, by drag or tap-to-place. The key: makes own food? backbone? six legs? feathers? fur and milk? dry scales? Feedback is a question ("What covers it?"), never a verdict; puppets can move freely. Model line: "These trays use simple body rules. A few real animals break them, like mammals that lay eggs."

**(d) Hide, Warn, Grip, Reach.** **Spot bench**, model `spot-v1`: three animals (brown stick insect, green tree frog, poison dart frog) × two backgrounds (bark, leaves) = 6 settings; a match is **hidden**, a mismatch **spotted**, the dart frog always **seen, and left alone**. A **tail bench** (wrapped or loose), a **beak card**, a **day and night clock**, and a **chain builder** (feature → job → survives). Model line: "Real birds see differently, and camouflage isn't perfect."

**(e) The Big River Map.** *A simplified map, not to scale.* Tap countries for names; the French Guiana line; the river line and the longest line; three record cards labeled estimates; a ten-card strip with one card shaded.

**(f) The Many-Kinds Bench, model `slice-v1` (Keeper's Desk).** 100 squares: 6 green (rainforest) with 50 kinds, 94 tan with the other 50. Readouts: about 8 kinds per green square, about half a kind per tan square, about 16 times richer. Jar contrast, the Yasuní card, the endemic card, and a caution line that clearing one square loses no exact number of kinds. Model line: "Real numbers are estimates: about 6 in 100 for land, and half or more for kinds."

**(g) The Supply Line (Keeper's Desk).** Five market goods, each chain revealed forward or backward. Hidden-name card, cleared-land card, land card. A three-option **choice board** for the café's chocolate (buy any, never again, checked farms) gives one honest line each and records nothing about the child. Model line: "Real supply chains have many more steps."

No timers, scores, points, medals, streaks or locks. Every activity is open from the first visit.
