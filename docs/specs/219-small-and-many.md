A child comes to understand that minibeasts live in small places that suit their needs (a woodlouse needs damp and dark, or its body loses water) and are looked at gently and **put back**; that insects are one group among many minibeasts, and that they have more **kinds** than any other animal group, for reasons; that insect records are records **for their size**, and being tiny is the reason; that each insect part is shaped for its job, and a skeleton on the outside must be shed to grow; that a stick insect is **born** twig-shaped and the mix of shapes changes over many generations; and, at the Keeper's Desk, that losing pollinators would cost food **variety and yield**, not all our bread.

## The knowledge

**Small places, matched needs (Sprout).** A minibeast is a small animal without a backbone (spec 116's opening card, reused). Different minibeasts live in different small places (micro-habitats): woodlice, slugs and worms where it is dark and damp (beneath logs, stones and fallen leaves, or underground); in a pond, pond skaters on the surface and dragonfly nymphs below; aphids, caterpillars and ladybugs on sunny leaves. The match has a reason: woodlice are land crustaceans whose skin holds water poorly and whose gill-like parts must stay moist, so damp, dark places keep them from drying out. They do not choose like picking a house; they survive best where it is damp. A habitat (where it lives) is not the same as its food.

**Gentle hands (Sprout).** Look first. If a grown-up says yes, use a soft brush to coax a creature into a tray or clear pot, look briefly, then return it to the exact spot and lay the log back as it lay. Never grab or squash. Some, like butterflies, are only for looking at. Being brave means being gentle. Care has reasons: worms help make soil, woodlice break up dead leaves, bees and hoverflies pollinate, ladybugs eat aphids, and minibeasts are food for birds and hedgehogs, so the unglamorous ones count too.

**Insects and the other minibeasts (Seedling).** Adult insects have six legs and three body parts; spiders have eight legs and two body parts and are arachnids; woodlice are crustaceans with fourteen legs; centipedes and millipedes have many legs; worms have none; a snail moves on one foot. Count features, never "looks creepy". (Body plan from spec 150; spec 62's leg key.)

**More kinds than any other group (Seedling to Sapling).** About a million insect species are named, about half of all named species of living things. About 400,000 are beetles. Mammals number about 6,700 living species, so there are roughly 60 kinds of beetle for every kind of mammal (computed). A careful 2018 estimate puts all insect species at about 5.5 million, so most are not yet named; older guesses ran to 30 million. A **species** is a kind, not a count of bodies: a nest of a million ants is one species. Four reasons insects have so many kinds, each with its "so": small (little food needed, so many small places to live); fast breeding (many young, so populations change and split faster); flight (so new places are reached); a tough, adaptable exoskeleton (so bodies fit many ways of life). Success here means many kinds and many individuals, not size or strength.

**Records for their size (Seedling to Sapling).** In a 2010 study, male horned dung beetles (*Onthophagus taurus*) pulled about 1,141 times their own body weight, the strongest insect yet measured for its size (the child text says "no insect", not "no animal": the study's authors found one mite that held on slightly harder). A flea can jump well over 100 times its own body length (best jumps; averages are lower), launched by springy pads that store energy and release it like a catapult. Dragonflies are among the fastest insects; museum figures give about 50 km/h (30 mph); older, higher claims mixed ground speed with air speed. A bombardier beetle mixes chemicals in a chamber and sprays them out at about 100 °C in rapid pulses (a defense, not a size record). These are ratios, not totals: a person can lift far more than a beetle. Being tiny and light is the reason. Scaled up, a body's weight grows faster than its strength, so a giant flea could not keep the jump (qualitative only).

**Parts shaped for jobs (Sapling).** A compound eye is made of many small units, each with its own lens: a worker honeybee has about 5,000 in each eye, a large dragonfly many more. Insects breathe through **spiracles**, small holes along the body, which lead into branching air tubes (**tracheae**); not through the mouth. Mouthparts fit food: a beetle's chewing jaws (mandibles), a butterfly's coiled sucking tube (proboscis), a housefly's sponging pad. A grown butterfly cannot chew a leaf however it tries (its caterpillar could). The exoskeleton does not grow, so a growing insect **molts**: the old skin splits, the insect climbs out soft, swells, and the new skin, already made underneath, hardens. Engineers copy insects (**biomimicry**): a 2013 camera with about 180 tiny lens units on a dome, modeled on the arthropod eye, saw a very wide view; the Eastgate Centre in Harare (1996) cools itself with air drawn through its mass and out of chimneys, an idea its architect took from termite mounds.

**Born that shape (Sapling to Keeper's Desk).** More than 3,000 kinds of stick and leaf insect are known, most in warm places; many look like twigs and even sway like a twig in the wind. Some jumping spiders look like ants, walk in winding ant-like paths and lift their front legs like antennae when they stop; in a 2017 video test, predatory spiders attacked them about as rarely as real ants. No insect chose or grew its disguise. Variation was already there; insects that were harder to spot left more young; over many generations the mix of shapes in the population changed. An individual keeps its shape all its life. The peppered moth (spec 161) is the real, measured case; the stick insect bench is a made-up model of the same mechanism.

**Pollinators and the food supply (Keeper's Desk).** Pollination is pollen carried to a flower's stigma (what the pollinator does); fertilization is the later joining of a pollen cell with an egg cell inside the ovule, which makes the seed (spec 149 owns the plant side). Wheat, rice and maize, the great staple grains, are pollinated by wind or by themselves. Many fruits, nuts and vegetables need animal pollinators for some or all of their harvest (spec 150 gives 87 of 115 leading crops). A 2016 global assessment (IPBES) found animal pollination directly responsible for about 5 to 8% of world crop production by volume, worth about US$235 to 577 billion a year. So decline threatens **variety and yield**, not all calories. Pollinators include wild bees, honeybees, hoverflies, butterflies, moths and beetles. Causes include habitat loss, some pesticides and disease; what helps includes flower-rich field margins, hedgerows and less spraying, and recovery takes seasons. Hand pollination works for some crops (vanilla is nearly all hand-pollinated; some Sichuan pear and apple orchards are pollinated by hand, for reasons people still argue about), but billions of short-lived flowers over huge areas make it impractical as a general fix.

**History cards (real, checked).**
- **Citrus ants (traditionally about 304 CE, southern China).** A book attributed to the scholar Ji Han describes farmers putting ants in their orange trees to guard the fruit from other insects, with the ants sold at market. Its date is debated (some scholars think the text took its present form much later), so the card says "said to be about 1,700 years old" and "may be the oldest record". (The ant's species is a modern identification.)
- **Hooke's flea (1665, London).** Robert Hooke's *Micrographia* showed a flea and a fly's compound eye drawn through a microscope, huge, on fold-out plates.
- **Charles Henry Turner's bees (1910, St. Louis).** A Black American scientist and high-school teacher, Turner trained honeybees to colored boxes and disks with careful controls, showing bees can see and remember color. Racism kept him from a university post; he kept doing research.
- **Edmond Albius's vanilla (1841, Réunion; Keeper's Desk).** Vanilla brought from Mexico flowered on Réunion but set almost no pods, because its home pollinators were missing. Edmond Albius, enslaved and about twelve, found a quick way to pollinate each flower by hand with a thin stick and his thumb. His method is still used. Slavery ended in French colonies in 1848 and he was freed; people asked for a reward for him, and he never received one. Named in the card body, person first; his later hardships are left out.
- **The Eastgate Centre (1996, Harare, Zimbabwe).** Architect Mick Pearce cooled an office block with air moved through chimneys, inspired by termite mounds; scientists still study exactly how the mounds move air.
- **A story people tell (labeled so):** a biologist, usually said to be J. B. S. Haldane, is said to have joked that the maker of the world had "an inordinate fondness for beetles". Even the earliest printing called it possibly made up.

## The game or activity

Every screen with a model says "A made-up model" and its model line. No score, timer, lock or mastery label. Drags have tap equivalents.

**(a) Under the Log.** *Lift and Look:* a garden cross-section with four spots (under the log, in the soil, on a sunny leaf, in the pond). Predict who lives there from four creature cards, then lift (log and stone lift with a soft-brush cursor) and check; each reveal shows a **what it needs** card (damp, dark, water, leaves). A **mismatch** card asks "What if the woodlouse were on the sunny wall?" and the drawn woodlouse walks back into the shade; nothing on screen dries up or is harmed. *Gentle Hands:* six kind-or-unkind action pairs (brush vs grab; tray vs pocket; look vs squash; put back vs jar; log laid back vs left off; watch the butterfly vs catch it), each showing the creature's calm or startled reaction. A **job card** for each creature (worm, woodlouse, bee, hoverfly, ladybug, ground beetle). Ends with the **Put it back** step: drag the woodlouse home and lay the log as it lay. Revisits spec 28's damp-shelter snail.

**(b) Count the Kinds.** *Insect or not?* Eight lifelike cards (ant, ladybug, bee, spider, woodlouse, earthworm, snail, centipede); tap to count legs and body parts, then sort. *The Kinds Shelf* (Sapling): a to-scale shelf of jars, each jar holding 10,000 named kinds: insects fill 100 jars (beetles 40 of them), mammals about two thirds of one jar (computed). A **kinds, not bodies** panel puts one ant nest beside a hundred different beetles. A **named and not yet named** bar shows about 1 million named against an estimated 5.5 million. *Why so many?* Four factor cards, each with a "so..." to complete from three endings.

**(c) Hall of Records, model `scale-v1`.** *A made-up model.* Four champions (dung beetle, flea, dragonfly, bombardier beetle), one per card, each with "for its size" and "overall". **Flea:** a 2 mm model flea jumps 30 cm, 150 body lengths; slide a **drawn figure** 1.5 m tall through the same 150 lengths: 225 m, about 22 model mills stacked (spec 156's made-up 10 m mill). **Dung beetle:** 1,141 weight blocks, each the beetle's own weight, grouped as 11 rows of 100 and 41. **Dragonfly:** a 5 cm model dragonfly at 50 km/h covers about 14 m a second, almost 280 body lengths. **Bombardier:** a chemistry card, not a size record. **Make it giant:** a switch scales the flea up; the bench shows its weight growing faster than its leg strength and the jump shrinking (qualitative). Model line: "Real fleas, beetles and dragonflies vary. These sizes are picked to make the sums clear." The figure is a drawing; nothing is measured on the child.

**(d) The Close Look.** A magnified meadow beetle with tappable parts: compound eye (zooms into a lens grid and a mosaic "beetle's view"), spiracles (a row of dots, then the branching air tubes inside), mandibles, antennae, exoskeleton. **Mouthpart table:** beetle jaws, butterfly coiled tube, housefly sponge pad, each tried on three ways of feeding (cut and chew a leaf, sip nectar from deep in a flower, mop up a spill on a flat surface); only the one-to-one fitting pairs work, and the others say why. Pairs are by feeding way, not by food, because foods overlap: a butterfly and a housefly can both drink sugar water, and a shared-food line says so. **Molt strip:** a grasshopper nymph's four-step molt (split, climb out soft, swell, harden), with the empty skin left on a stem. **Copy it:** two pairs, eye to dome camera and termite mound to the Eastgate Centre, with a **new-part** card asking what a newly found insect's hollow tube mouth is for (an insect, not a beetle: beetles' long snouts chew).

**(e) Twig and Ant, model `twig-v1`.** *A made-up model; the rule is on screen.* A hedge with 32 stick insects: 16 **twiggy**, 16 **plain**. Each generation, birds find 1 in 4 of the twiggy and 3 in 4 of the plain; each insect left leaves two young shaped like itself. Generations run 16/16, 24/8, 36/4, 54/2; the twiggy share goes 1/2, 3/4, 9/10, 27/28 (computed; 16/16 is the only start of 32 that stays whole for three generations, so it is the only start offered). **Follow one:** a tag on one twiggy insect shows it the same shape all its life, and its young like it. A **mix bar** under each generation. The **ant-mimic card** shows a jumping spider beside an ant, legs counted (eight against six), front legs raised. A link opens spec 161's moth file read-only. Model line: "Real stick insects vary in many ways, and real birds and numbers vary. No insect in this bench changes its own shape."

**(f) The Market Without Bees (Keeper's Desk), model `orchard-v1`.** *A made-up model.* *Two steps:* drag pollen to the stigma (pollination), then watch the hidden step in the ovule (fertilization) labeled apart. *Wind or insects?* Sort seven crops. *The orchard bench:* pollinators **few**, **some** or **many** set apples per 100 blossoms at 10, 25 or 40; a wheat strip beside it never changes. Over three seasons choose **plant a flower margin** (pollinators up one level next season), **spray in bloom** (down one level) or **do nothing**; 81 runs, and no season moves more than one level (computed). The **market shelf** loses apples, cherries, almonds and pumpkins as pollinators fall, and keeps the bread. *By hand?* A helper does about 10 trees a day; a 600-tree orchard in a five-day bloom needs 12 helpers, and 60,000 trees would need 1,200 (computed). Model line: "Real harvests depend on weather, variety and many pollinators. This bench uses three levels and one rule."
