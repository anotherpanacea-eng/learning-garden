## 1. Purpose

The game has no Earth-science home. S7 (*Earth, water, weather and hazards*) has Bank Builder's erosion strips (draft spec 124) and the water models, but nothing on what the ground is made of. S8 (*Deep time, fossils, Earth and space*) has Fossil Close-Look (spec 106), which looks at two fossils and deliberately leaves out rock layers. Marble's **Volcanoes & Earthquakes** domain (28 topics) and the rock topics in **Earth's Systems** have no playable coverage.

This pack fills that gap from the ground Willowbank stands on. A child comes to understand that:

- the ground is rock under a thin skin of soil, and a pebble is a small piece of the Earth;
- rocks come in three families, each named for how it formed, and rocks change from one family to another very slowly;
- water, ice and roots break rock and the brook carries the pieces; soil is broken rock mixed with rotted living things;
- undisturbed layers are read from the bottom up, and a fold can turn that order over;
- the Earth has a thin crust over a hot mantle and core, and its outer shell is broken into slowly moving plates;
- the Pyrenees rose where two plates pushed together, and sea fossils high in the mountains are evidence that the rock formed under a sea;
- some changes are fast (an earthquake, a landslide) and some are slow (a pebble rounding, a mountain rising), and both reshape the land;
- earthquakes are rock slipping underground; volcanoes are openings where melted rock comes out, and a long-quiet volcano is not necessarily extinct.

## 3. The knowledge

Plain adult prose. Every claim is sourced in the audit or marked `[check]`.

**Earth is rock.** Dig deep enough almost anywhere and you reach solid rock (bedrock). Soil is a thin covering. Stones and pebbles are pieces of that rock.

**Three rock families, named for how they formed.**
- *Igneous*: melted rock that cooled and hardened. Underground it cools slowly into large crystals (granite); at the surface lava cools fast into fine-grained rock (basalt).
- *Sedimentary*: loose pieces (sand, mud, shells) laid down in layers, then pressed and cemented over a long time into rock (sandstone, mudstone, limestone). Fossils are usually found in sedimentary rock, because gentle layering can bury remains without destroying them. Not every sedimentary rock has fossils.
- *Metamorphic*: existing rock changed by heat and pressure **without melting** (mudstone to slate, limestone to marble). Marble has crystals but is not igneous.
- Hardness does not tell you the family. People-made materials (brick, concrete) are not natural rock.

**The rock cycle.** Any family can become another: granite weathers to grains, grains become sandstone, buried sandstone can be changed by heat and pressure, rock that melts cools into igneous rock. There is no fixed start or one-way order. Most steps take thousands to millions of years; lava cooling at the surface is one of the fast exceptions.

**Weathering, erosion, deposition.** Weathering breaks rock where it is (frost in cracks, roots, rubbing). Erosion carries the pieces away (water, wind, ice). Deposition drops them somewhere else. Brook pebbles round as they knock together and scrape along the bed: research on river pebbles finds that corners wear off first, and afterwards the pebble slowly gets smaller while staying rounded (Domokos et al. 2014).

**Soil** is a mixture of broken-down rock (sand, silt, clay) and rotted plant and animal matter (humus). It forms very slowly. Different soils suit different conditions; none is best for everything.

**Rock layers and superposition.** Layers of sediment are laid down one on top of another, so in an undisturbed stack the lowest formed first and the highest last. A crack (fault) or an intrusion of once-melted rock is younger than the layers it cuts (the principle of cross-cutting relationships; USGS and NPS). Folding can bend layers and even turn part of a stack upside down, so depth then no longer matches age; geologists follow a layer round the bend and use "way-up" clues such as ripple marks.

**Earth's layers** are a different kind of layer: a thin rocky crust (about 5 km under oceans, about 30 km on average under continents, thicker under big mountains), a mantle about 2,900 km thick of hot rock that is mostly solid but flows very slowly, a liquid metal outer core and a solid inner core. Together they reach about 6,400 km to the center. The deeper you go, the hotter it gets. The superposition rule does not apply to these layers.

**Tectonic plates.** The rigid outer shell (crust plus the top of the mantle) is broken into large plates that ride on hotter, softer, mostly solid rock. They move a few centimeters a year, roughly as fast as fingernails grow (USGS; fingernails about 3.6 cm a year). Plates are not continents, and plate edges are not coastlines. Earthquakes and volcanoes cluster near plate edges.

**The Pyrenees.** The Iberian plate (carrying what is now Spain and Portugal) and the European plate converged and collided. Most of the mountain building happened in the Eocene and Oligocene, roughly 55 to 25 million years ago. Convergence and the first squeezing began in the Late Cretaceous (roughly 80 to 70 million years ago, by different estimates), full collision came in the Eocene, and studies differ on when it peaked (a Catalan geological atlas puts the culmination about 35 to 30 million years ago). The pack teaches the range and the disagreement. The highest summit is Aneto, 3,404 m, in a granite massif; Monte Perdido (3,355 m, the range's third-highest peak) is a massif of Cretaceous and Eocene limestone.

**Sea fossils in the mountains.** Before the collision, a shallow sea lay between Iberia and Europe. Nummulites, single-celled sea creatures with coin-shaped coiled shells (the name means "little coin"), were abundant in Eocene seas, and their shells built nummulitic limestones found across the Pyrenean basins, including the Ainsa basin in the south-central Pyrenees. Eocene marine rock also sits high in the range: Monte Perdido's limestone is Cretaceous and Eocene, and in 2024 a ranger in Ordesa and Monte Perdido National Park found a fossil starfish in Eocene rock, identified by an IGME-CSIC specialist. These rocks show the rock formed under a sea and was later raised by mountain building. Fossil Close-Look (spec 106) shows that a fossil is a body or a trace, and [spec 142](142-footprints-by-the-ford.md) (Footprints by the Ford, drafted alongside) teaches how fossils form and the Pyrenean dinosaur tracksites. This pack does not re-teach fossil formation; it adds what a fossil's setting and layer tell you. Hob finds the coin stone on the high path in Act I; spec 142's slab turns up by the ford in Act VI, so the two finds do not collide.

**Fast and slow changes.** Fast: earthquakes, landslides, eruptions, floods. Slow: pebbles rounding, soil forming, valleys deepening, mountains rising. Both kinds reshape land; fast does not only mean building, slow does not only mean wearing away. Typical mountain uplift is about 0.1 to a few millimeters a year (Apennines 0.2 to 1.6 mm/yr; New Zealand's Southern Alps up to about 3 mm/yr). At 1 mm a year, a million years gives 1 km. Erosion works against uplift, which is why 30 million years of steady 1 mm rise would not leave a 30 km mountain.

**Earthquakes** happen when rock underground suddenly slips or breaks; the energy shakes the ground. Most are small. The Pyrenees has low to moderate seismicity: more than 400 events of magnitude 2 or more a year, of which about ten are felt locally. Damaging earthquakes are uncommon there but have happened; a notable 20th-century example is Arette, France, in 1967 (magnitude 5.3), and the historic 1427-1428 Catalan series near Olot was far stronger. Child text stays at "many small earthquakes, now and then a moderate one" and names no historic disaster with deaths.

**Volcanoes.** A volcano is an opening where melted rock (lava at the surface) comes out. Lava glows because it is hot; it is not fire. *Active*: erupting or likely to erupt. *Dormant*: quiet for a long time but could erupt again. *Extinct*: not expected to erupt again. Status is judged from eruption history and monitoring, not the mountain's shape. **The Garrotxa Volcanic Zone** near Olot, Catalonia, has about forty small volcanic cones. Its most recent eruption, the Croscat, was long given as about 11,000 to 11,500 years ago; newer radiocarbon and argon studies place it about 13,000 to 15,000 years ago. The pack says "more than 10,000 years ago" and teaches why the dates differ. Spanish authorities count the field as volcanically active in the hazard sense, and the Catalan geological survey judges the risk very small but never zero, so it is not called extinct. The Smithsonian Global Volcanism Program lists it as a Holocene field with no historical eruptions, and volcano databases describe it as dormant, so the child text uses "dormant" and the grown-up note explains that hazard planners still treat the field as active.

**Pompeii archive card (older depth only).** In 79 CE Vesuvius erupted and buried the Roman town of Pompeii under several meters of ash and pumice. The covering kept streets, walls, paintings and everyday objects in place for centuries; organized excavation began in 1748. The card says plainly that many people died and many fled; it shows no casts, bodies or victim imagery. Vesuvius last erupted in 1944 and is watched day and night by the Vesuvius Observatory, founded in 1841.

**Earthquake safety** appears only as a card labeled "General safety guidance from official sources," quoting the substance of Drop, Cover, and Hold On as given by the US CDC and Ready.gov-linked guidance: drop to hands and knees, cover head and neck (under a sturdy table if one is close), hold on until the shaking stops, and stay inside if inside. Official Spanish and French guidance gives the same core actions in its own words (shelter under sturdy furniture or by a strong interior wall, keep away from windows, stay inside); the grown-up note says so and asks families to check local guidance. No bench, model result or quiz item produces safety advice.

**Contrasts that show the boundary:**
- Hob's Cut (undisturbed, bottom is oldest) beside the Bent Cliff (overturned fold, top is oldest in that part).
- Rock layers (a time record) beside Earth's layers (what the planet is made of; no age rule).
- Pebble rounding over many floods beside a landslide in one night.
- Granite (crystals from melt) beside marble (crystals from heat and pressure, no melting).
- A 500-year-quiet volcano (could be dormant) beside a volcano with no magma supply (extinct).

**Top misconceptions designed against** (each tagged on at least one wrong choice): the Earth is soil or hollow; all hard rocks are igneous; crystals mean igneous; metamorphic means melted; rocks never change family; weathering and erosion are the same; the top layer is oldest; layers formed all at once; mountains have always been there; a flood carried sea shells up the mountain; plates float on lava; plates move too slowly to measure; lava is fire; an earthquake is an explosion; all earthquakes are huge; dormant means extinct; active means always erupting.

### 5.2 Layer Stack bench (`layer-v1`, a made-up cliff)

**Cliff A, Hob's Cut** (bottom to top): (1) gray limestone with nummulites, (2) sandstone with ripple marks, (3) mudstone with leaf prints, (4) gravel of rounded pebbles, (5) soil. A crack runs through layers 1 to 3 and stops under layer 4.

1. *Order:* tap layers oldest to youngest into a numbered column. Check shows which positions match.
2. *Next layer:* "The brook floods and leaves mud. Where does it go?" The child taps where (Marble's place-a-new-layer step).
3. *The crack:* choose when it happened from cards.

**Cliff B, the Bent Cliff:** the same first three layers, folded so that the right-hand part is upside down. A **Watch it bend** button plays the flat stack folding (model animation). Ripple marks in the overturned part point down. The child answers whether the top layer there is the youngest and traces layer 1 round the bend with taps.

Every screen shows: *A made-up cliff. Real cliffs are messier, and geologists use many clues.*

### 5.3 Pebble Tumble bench (`pebble-v1`, a simplified picture)

A model brook with one pebble. Buttons: **Quiet day** and **Flood**. Rules:
- The pebble starts with 6 sharp corners and size 10 (model steps, no real units).
- A quiet day changes nothing (the pebble does not move).
- Each flood moves the pebble and rounds 1 corner until none are left.
- Once round, every 4th flood after that makes it 1 size step smaller, never below 4.

Counters show floods, corners and size. Results: 4 floods → 2 corners; 6 floods → round, size 10; 14 floods → size 8; 30 floods → size 4. The shape follows the published finding that corners go first and size goes slowly after.

**Contrast panel** beside the brook: three cards that each play once. *Landslide* (one stormy night, the bank slips: a fast change); *Earthquake* (the ground shakes for seconds: fast); *Mountain building* (a ribbon that grows a hair's width per tap: slow; the Sapling view adds "about 1 mm a year"). A sorting row asks Fast or Slow for each card. Sprout and Seedling views stay qualitative, as Marble's Fast & Slow record requires; numbers appear only in Sapling and Keeper's Desk items tied to Tectonic Plates.

Every screen shows: *A simplified picture. Real pebbles take many floods and long distances, and the numbers here are made up.*
