## Purpose

Ratio & Proportion has pinned Marble topics with candidate encounters in draft PR #126 (spec 118's Keeper's Desk cases: ratio notation, ratio problems, a part as a fraction of a whole, a seed-exchange rate) and draft PR #128 (spec 125's paint mixing by kind), but no bench where a child **makes** a ratio, sees it hold or break when scaled, and carries it into proportion, rates and units. This pack takes nine untouched topics there.

A child comes to understand that a ratio is a "for every" group that repeats; that scaling keeps a ratio only by multiplying both amounts, never by adding the same to both; that a difference and a ratio answer different questions; that a ratio is a "times" rule that also reads as a fraction and as a straight line through zero; that a bar of equal blocks shares any total in a ratio once one block is known; that a part of a ratio is a different fraction of the whole; that direct proportion has a unit rate, a scale factor and a constant of proportionality; that a straight line which misses zero is not proportional, and that some amounts go down as others go up (inverse); that compound units join two units with "per" and best buys compare the same amount; and that changing units is proportion too, done in steps, with square units scaling by the square.

**Builds on, never re-teaches:** spec 125's paint mixing by kind (here: by amount); spec 118 Case 1's ratio notation, simplifying and ratio-vs-difference panel (recapped in T3 and T4, not re-taught as new); spec 118 Case 3's 2 : 3 reed-for-apples rate and 5 : 3 nursery share (this pack uses different goods and becomes that case's earlier teaching); specs 45 and 113's measuring jug and liters-to-milliliters bridge (here: as one of several conversions); spec 111's route scale (here: scale factor as a word only); spec 178's speed and average speed (recapped once in T14). **Not targeted:** `mt_vmW2cb5c7A` and `mt_X5cypSGoGU`, first assigned here and now reassigned to siblings 212 and 192 (see Upstream check); percentages (sibling 200); fraction scaling (sibling 198); similar shapes and map scale (sibling 206, spec 119); Metric & Imperial Conversion (`mt_p6MhZJYYPN`, not in the assignment map, so used inline at Keeper's Desk with no claim).

## The knowledge

**For every.** "2 white for every 3 blue" names a group that repeats. More groups make more paint of the same shade. Sprout meets it with scoops shown in groups and no numbers.

**Multiplicative, not additive.** Same shade = both amounts multiplied by the same number (2 : 3 → 4 : 6 → 6 : 9). Adding the same to both (2 : 3 → 4 : 5) changes the share of white (2/5 → 4/9), so the shade is paler. **Ratio vs difference:** a difference subtracts, a ratio asks "how many times?" Mixes 1 red : 2 yellow and 3 red : 4 yellow both have "one more yellow" yet differ (2 yellow per red against 4/3). The *Gazette* scene carries it out of paint: 120 against 80 and 60 against 20 are both "40 more", and 1.5 times against 3 times.

**Notation and the relationship.** a : b keeps its order and simplifies by dividing both numbers by a common factor (6 : 9 = 2 : 3; 10 : 14 = 5 : 7), recapped from spec 118. New here: the ratio as one relationship in three forms. In 2 : 3 (white : blue), blue = 1.5 × white, white = 2/3 of blue, and the table (2, 3), (4, 6), (6, 9), (8, 12) plots as a straight line through zero. A table with a steady **difference** (1, 5), (2, 6), (3, 7) is not a ratio relationship.

**Bar models.** Equal blocks, labeled bars, a brace for the total, a "?" for the unknown. The three moves: draw the bars, find one block, read off. **Dividing in a ratio:** blocks = sum of all parts; one block = total ÷ blocks; each share = its parts × one block; shares add back to the total (20 L in 2 : 3 → 8 and 12; 60 mL in 1 : 2 : 3 → 10, 20, 30; 15 kg in 1 : 4 → 3 and 12). It runs backward too: 12 L of yellow as 3 blocks of a 3 : 2 green gives 4 L per block, 8 L of blue, 20 L in all; actual shares 10 and 14 write back as 5 : 7. **Part to part vs part to whole:** 2 : 3 compares white with blue; white is 2/5 of the pot.

**Direct proportion.** Two amounts are directly proportional when one is always the same number times the other. Built on a double number line and a ratio table first; the formula comes last. **Unit rate**: the amount for one ($6 for 3 kg → $2 per kg → 5 kg for $10). **Scale factor**: what both amounts are multiplied by (heron blue × 4 = 8 : 12). **Constant of proportionality**: the fixed multiplier from one amount to the other (blue = 1.5 × white; cost = 2 × kg). **Graphs:** proportional pairs lie on a straight line through (0, 0), since none of one means none of the other. A $2 pot fee plus $4 per liter is straight and not proportional: 1 L costs $6, 2 L cost $10, not $12. **Inverse proportion** (a first look, Sapling up): 2 painters take 6 hours, 4 take 3; the product stays 12; the graph curves down. The "illusion of proportionality" is designed against: every case asks "does doubling one double the other?"

**Compound units.** A rate joins two different units with "per": dollars per kilogram, liters per minute, jars per 5 kg. Divide the right way round; compare best buys per the **same** amount ($5 for 2 kg = $2.50 per kg; $11 for 5 kg = $2.20 per kg). Exchange rates chain (8 kg chestnut → 10 kg wheat → 6 jars). Speed is spec 178's: one recap line (12 km in 1.5 h = 8 km/h) and no new speed teaching.

**Conversions as proportion.** 1 km = 1000 m, 1 kg = 1000 g, 1 L = 1000 mL: each a fixed ratio, shown as a double number line. Smaller unit, bigger number. Common unit before comparing (2.5 L = 2500 mL > 2450 mL). Multi-step problems convert inside the chain (0.75 kg × 3 = 2.25 kg = 2250 g; 2.5 h = 150 min = 9000 s). **Square units** (Keeper's Desk): 1 m² = 100 × 100 = 10,000 cm², not 100. **Inches** (Keeper's Desk, no claim): exactly 2.54 cm, so 12 in = 30.48 cm.

**Real anchors (see audit).**
- **The Nine Chapters, chapter 2, *Millet and Rice*.** A Chinese mathematical classic compiled under the Han dynasty (scholars place it between about the 2nd century BCE and the 1st century CE; it is named in a source of no later than 93 CE). Its second chapter has 46 problems on exchanging about twenty kinds of grains, beans and seeds, opening with a table of rates (unhusked millet 50, hulled millet 30) and the *jinyou* method: multiply the given amount by the sought rate and divide by the given rate. Child text: "about 2,000 years ago"; D4 computes 10 × 30 ÷ 50 = 6.
- **The meter and the kilogram.** On 19 March 1791 the French Academy of Sciences proposed a length of one ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the equator (the National Assembly accepted it later that month; sources give 26 or 30 March, so child text gives only 1791). In 1795 the kilogram was defined as the mass of one liter of water. Both are defined differently today.
- **The international inch.** Since 1 July 1959, by agreement of Australia, Canada, New Zealand, South Africa, the United Kingdom and the United States, the yard is exactly 0.9144 m, so the inch is exactly 2.54 cm.

**Contrasts:** multiply both vs add to both; ratio vs difference; part to part vs part to whole; blocks counted vs blocks' value; straight through zero vs straight with a start fee; direct vs inverse; total price vs price per kg; smaller unit vs bigger number; length factor vs area factor.

**Top misconceptions designed against:** additive scaling; a bigger batch changes the shade; looking at one color only; ratio read as difference; order swapped; not simplifying; unequal blocks; dividing by one part, or by the number of terms, instead of all the parts; stopping at one block; part-to-part read as part-to-whole; every straight line is proportional; every "more and more" is direct (illusion of proportionality); rate the wrong way round; comparing totals for best buys; wrong conversion direction; ×100 for ×1000; comparing without a common unit; dropping a step in a chain; linear factor for area.

## The game or activity

**(a) Shade Table, model `shade-table-v1`.** Two or three paint jars, a scoop, a sample swatch. Each scoop drops into a pot shown as grouped scoop pictures; **Paint a stripe** lays it beside the sample. *A made-up model: a shade depends only on the ratio of scoops; each authored shade has a fixed swatch and a text name; real paints vary.* Authored mixes: heron blue 2 white : 3 blue (and its multiples ×2, ×3, ×4); Bramble's sky 4 : 5; 1 : 2 blue and its multiples; greens 1 : 1 and 2 : 2; oranges 1 red : 2 yellow and 3 : 4. Undefined mixes get a "no swatch for this mix yet" stripe, never an invented color. **Sprout mode** shows groups of pictured scoops only. **Table mode** (Seedling up) adds a ratio table and the rule strip "blue ÷ white = ?". Builds on spec 125's palette names.

**(b) Bar Bench, model `bar-bench-v1`.** Draggable equal blocks in two or three labeled bars, a total brace, **Fill one block** (child enters total ÷ blocks), **Fill the rest**, **Check the sum**. Blocks cannot be drawn unequal. Totals and parts come from the Set B items and the meadow scene; the bench never fills the one-block value for the child.

**(c) Rate Line, model `rate-line-v1`.** One rate shown three linked ways: a double number line aligned at zero, a ratio table and a graph. Adding a pair updates all three. **Compare a line with a start fee** overlays the pot-fee line ($2 + $4 per liter: (0, 2), (1, 6), (2, 10), (3, 14)) against the proportional flour line ((0, 0), (1, 2), (3, 6), (5, 10)). An inverse panel (Sapling up) plots painters × hours = 12 at (1, 12), (2, 6), (3, 4), (4, 3), (6, 2). The **Word drawer** holds eight word cards, each with a meaning, an example and a non-example (companion table); the words become tappable glossary links on every bench. *A made-up model: prices are invented for practice.*

**(d) Exchange Board, model `exchange-board-v1`.** The miller's chalkboard rates (companion). **Scale both sides** multiplies a trade; **Find one** shows the unit rate; **Compare per kg** puts two sacks on the same basis. Speed is not offered here. *A made-up model: rates are invented; real markets change.*

**(e) Unit Shelf, model `unit-shelf-v1`.** Paired strips (km and m, kg and g, L and mL, h-min-s, inches and cm at Keeper's Desk) slide into line at zero; **Step to the next unit** writes the factor on each arrow of a chain. A tiled 1 m square (Keeper's Desk) zooms from one 100-square row to 100 rows. *Real units; the things measured are made up.*

No timers, scores, streaks, medals or locks. Every bench is open from the first visit.
