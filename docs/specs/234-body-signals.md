## Purpose

Strand S2 has four body packs: 148 (the supper journey, teeth, a red cell's loop), 159 (the Traveler puppet: three skeleton jobs, muscle pairs, joints, the five senses, a sense-brain-muscle relay, the bell jar), 160 (cells) and 174 (diffusion, the four-room heart, aerobic respiration, body temperature). Seven *Human Body* and *Organisms & Life Processes* topics next to them are still "not yet mapped": naming body parts with their senses for the youngest, naming six major bones, the two halves of the nervous system, neurons and the brain map, homeostasis, digestion with enzymes, and anaerobic respiration.

A child comes to understand that each sense has its own body part (eyes, ears, nose, tongue, skin) and that many animals sense too; that six major bones have a scientific name, an everyday name and a job, and that some animals wear their skeleton outside; that the **central nervous system** (brain and spinal cord) and the **nerves** that branch from it carry fast messages in and out, with a spinal **reflex** shortcut; that **neurons** pass a signal electrically along and chemically across a gap, that the brain is mapped by job, that practice strengthens connections and that illusions show the brain builds what we see; that **negative feedback** keeps the inside of a body steady, with insulin and glucagon as a two-way example and hormones as a slower second message system; that digestion is physical and chemical, with **enzymes** as reusable helpers that cut big molecules small enough to absorb; and that short of oxygen, muscles release a little energy **anaerobically**, making lactic acid and an oxygen debt.

**Never the child's body.** Every body in this pack is a drawing (the person in the librarian's fold-out book from spec 148), a wooden model (spec 159's Traveler, "it") or an animal (Pip the fox, Dot the ladybug, a crab, an earthworm, a giraffe). No screen asks the explorer to point to, feel, taste, measure, time or describe their own body, senses, food, feelings or health. Body-part words appear as "the person's", "Pip's" or "the Traveler's", never "your". **Out of scope:** illness, injury, medicine, diet or exercise advice, puberty and reproduction (so the endocrine system is shown only through insulin and glucagon), drinks or alcohol (ethanol is named only as something yeast makes in bread dough), and drug or brain-injury stories.

## The knowledge

Sources in the audit. "About" marks ranges.

**Body parts and senses (Sprout).** Outside parts: head, neck, arms, legs, hands, feet. Five senses, each with a main part: eyes (sight), ears (hearing), nose (smell), tongue (taste), skin (touch, all over, not just fingers). Seeing works because light comes **into** the eyes; eyes send out no beams. The tongue does most tasting; smell helps flavor. Many animals have these senses, though not all: an earthworm has no eyes or ears, but light-sensitive cells in its skin (most at the front end) and touch through its body; child text says only that its skin "can tell light from dark". Aristotle (Greece, 4th century BCE) listed the five senses; scientists now count more, such as balance (159's Keeper's note).

**Naming major bones (Seedling).** Six bones or groups, each with an everyday name and a job: **skull** (head bones; protects the brain), **spine** made of **vertebrae** (backbone; holds the body up, bends because it is many small bones, and guards the spinal cord), **ribcage** (rib bones; protects the heart and lungs and moves when a person breathes), **pelvis** (hip bones; carries the body's weight to the legs and holds some organs), **femur** (thigh bone; the longest, strongest bone; supports and moves), **humerus** (upper-arm bone; muscles pull it to lift and swing the arm). The three jobs (support, protection, movement) are 159's; here they become a lens on each named bone. **Inside or outside:** a person, a fish and a giraffe have skeletons inside; insects like Dot and crabs have a hard skeleton outside (an exoskeleton); a snail has a shell; an earthworm has no hard skeleton. Bigger animals do not simply have more bones: a giraffe has seven neck bones, like a person and most mammals, only much longer. Vesalius (Brussels and Padua, 1543) named bones from real skeletons and showed a person's lower jaw is one bone, not two as Galen had taught from animals.

**The nervous system (Sapling).** It splits into the **central nervous system** (the brain and the spinal cord, inside the skull and spine) and **nerves** that branch to every part. **Sensory nerves** carry messages in; **motor nerves** carry them out to muscles. Nerves carry signals, not blood. In a **reflex** (foot off a thistle), the message turns around in the spinal cord, so the muscle moves before the brain feels the prick; a decided action (catching a tossed apple) goes up to the brain. Fast nerves carry signals at about 70 to 120 m/s; the slowest, about 0.5 to 2 m/s. The pack uses 100 m/s and 1 m/s as labeled round examples. Seeing feeds the system too: 162's eye card shows light to the eye and a message to the brain.

**Neurons and the brain (Sapling to Keeper's Desk).** A **neuron** is a nerve cell: branches (**dendrites**) pick up signals, the **cell body** joins them, a long **axon** carries an electrical signal, and a fatty **myelin** wrap speeds it. At the end is a tiny gap, the **synapse**; a chemical messenger crosses it and can start a new signal in the next neuron. So a signal is **electrochemical**. A person's brain has roughly 86 billion neurons (a 2009 count; later work questions how exact it is). **Brain map, by job** (most tasks use many areas together): frontal lobe (planning, deciding, starting movement), parietal (touch, where things are), temporal (hearing, memory, words), occipital (seeing), cerebellum (balance, smooth movement), brainstem (breathing, heartbeat), and the **limbic system** deep inside (with the amygdala and hippocampus, linked to emotions and memory). The "tenth of the brain" claim is a myth: imaging shows most of the brain active, different parts for different tasks. **Neuroplasticity:** connections that are used often grow stronger and faster, at any age; a 2000 study found the back of the hippocampus larger in London taxi drivers, more so the longer they had driven (a correlation the authors discuss). **Illusions as evidence:** in the Müller-Lyer arrows (1889) two equal lines look unequal even after measuring, so the brain builds what we see. Ibn al-Haytham (Basra and Cairo, 11th century) showed light enters the eye and explained the large horizon Moon by how the mind judges distance. Cajal (Spain) drew neurons as separate cells with gaps; Golgi (Italy), whose stain he used, thought they formed one net; they shared the 1906 Nobel Prize disagreeing.

**Digestion and enzymes (Sapling to Keeper's Desk).** Follow the food (148 recap): mouth, esophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine; helper organs liver, gallbladder, pancreas. Food in the tube is not yet "in" the body until absorbed. **Physical digestion** breaks food into smaller pieces (teeth, the stomach's churning, **bile** splitting fat into droplets). **Chemical digestion** changes big molecules into small ones with **enzymes**: amylase (saliva, pancreas) cuts starch into short chains, mostly sugar pairs (maltose), and enzymes on the small-intestine wall such as **maltase** finish them into single sugars; protease (stomach, pancreas) cuts proteins into amino acids; lipase (pancreas) cuts fats into fatty acids and glycerol. Enzymes are **biological catalysts**: proteins made by cells that speed up a reaction and are not used up, so one enzyme works again and again; they are not alive. Bile is made by the liver, stored in the gallbladder, and is not an enzyme; smaller droplets give lipase more surface to work on. Most chemical digestion finishes and most absorption happens in the small intestine, through finger-like **villi**; the large intestine takes back water. Small, soluble molecules can pass into the blood; big ones cannot. Payen and Persoz (France, 1833) drew diastase (an amylase) out of malt: the first enzyme isolated. No food is judged and no meal is the child's.

**Homeostasis (Keeper's Desk).** Keeping the inside of a body steady. One loop, one vocabulary: a change → **sensor** → **control center** → **effector** → a response that **opposes** the change, back toward a **set point**. This is **negative feedback**: "negative" means opposing, not bad, and it works both ways. It runs all the time, so levels wobble inside a band; it is not an on/off switch that waits for trouble. **Blood sugar:** after a meal is digested, glucose rises; cells in the pancreas sense it and release **insulin**, which helps cells take in glucose and the liver store it, so the level falls. When glucose falls, the pancreas releases **glucagon**, and the liver releases stored glucose. No nerve signal is needed for this loop. **Temperature** is 174's dial (hypothalamus, sweat, shivering), revisited with the same words. **Two message systems:** nerves are fast (a fraction of a second), electrical along neurons and short-lived; hormones from **glands** travel in the blood, are slower and last longer. Claude Bernard (France, 1850s-1870s) wrote of the steady "inner environment"; Walter Cannon (USA) named it homeostasis in 1926 and popularized it in 1932.

**Anaerobic respiration (Keeper's Desk).** Recap 174: aerobic respiration, glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water, energy released. In a sprint, muscles need energy faster than oxygen can arrive. Muscle cells then also break glucose down **incompletely**, without oxygen: glucose → **lactic acid**, releasing much less energy per glucose (energy stays locked in the lactic acid). It is a fallback, not a boost. **Modern view, kept in the grown-up panel:** in the body lactic acid is mostly present as lactate, which muscles and other organs can use again as fuel; rising lactate can accompany hard effort, but this pack does not treat it as the cause of burning or fatigue; it does not cause next-day soreness; levels fall back within about an hour. After stopping, breathing stays fast: the **oxygen debt** (today called EPOC), extra oxygen that partly helps clear the lactic acid and also refills muscle energy stores; child text says "helps clear", not "pays for all of it". **Yeast** without oxygen makes carbon dioxide and ethanol: the gas raises the baker's dough and most of the ethanol bakes off. Ethanol is chemically an alcohol, but child text never uses the word and nothing mentions drinks or brewing. A. V. Hill (UK) studied runners and named the oxygen debt; he and Otto Meyerhof (Germany) shared the 1922 Nobel Prize, and their ideas were later much revised.

## The game or activity

Native buttons, 44 px targets, keyboard operable, tap equivalents for every drag, no timers, no score. **Every screen:** *Every body here is a drawing, a wooden model or an animal.*

**(a) Who Notices?** Pip and the drawn person side by side. Tap a part to hear its name (head, eyes, ears, nose, tongue, neck, arms, hands, legs, feet, skin). Then a **sense sort**: five town pictures (the miller's cat's bell, fresh bread smell, a rainbow over the brook, a sour apple, soft moss) go to five sense parts on the drawn person. An **animal shelf** shows Pip's big ears, Dot's antennae and the earthworm's skin. Nothing asks about the explorer's own senses.

**(b) Name Tags.** The Traveler's frame (159) beside the drawn person's skeleton chart, six tag hooks. Each tag has two sides: scientific name and everyday name. Hang a tag, then tap a job tile (Support, Protection, Movement); each bone accepts every job the companion lists for it (skull: protection; spine: all three; ribcage: protection or movement; pelvis: support or protection; femur: support or movement; humerus: movement), and a Yes names all of that bone's jobs. An **inside or outside shelf**: the person, Dot, a crab, a snail and an earthworm sort into *inside*, *outside*, *shell*, *no hard skeleton*. A giraffe card counts neck bones beside the person's. Model line: *A simplified chart. A real skeleton has about 206 bones; this one names six.*

**(c) Message Lines.** The drawn person with the brain and spinal cord in one color and nerves in another. **Send a message**: choose a start (thistle under the foot, an apple tossed by Dot) and watch the route draw. The thistle route turns at the spinal cord, lifts the foot, then a second line reaches the brain and an "ouch" bubble appears last. The apple route goes up to the brain and back. Keeper's layer: a **speed panel** sets a length (1 m) and a nerve (fast 100 m/s, slow 1 m/s) and shows the time. Model line: *A simplified picture. Real nerves carry many messages at once, both ways.*

**(d) Neuron Relay and Brain Map, model `path-v1`.** Three drawn neurons in a row. **Fire** sends a spark along the first axon; at the gap, small dots cross (**chemical messenger**), and the next neuron fires. **Add myelin** makes the spark move faster. **Practice** taps (Dot's bell pattern) thicken one link: thin, medium, thick; thicker links pass the signal sooner. **Brain map:** tap a lobe or part for its job card. **Illusion card:** two arrow lines; the card says "Most people see the top line as longer"; an on-screen ruler then shows both the same. Nothing about what the explorer sees is asked or kept. Model line: *A made-up model. Real neurons connect to thousands of others.*

**(e) Breakdown Bench, model `snip-v1`.** A drawn tube from mouth to large intestine with the liver, gallbladder and pancreas beside it. Follow a bite of the drawn person's bread. At each stop: **Squash** (physical) or **Snip** (chemical) buttons. Snips use four enzyme pairs of scissors, each fitting one bead chain: amylase (starch into bead pairs), maltase (bead pairs into single beads, small intestine only), protease (protein), lipase (fat). Each pair of scissors makes many cuts and stays whole; a counter shows the cuts. Bile breaks one big fat drop into smaller drops. At the small intestine, only single beads pass the villi into the blood. Model line: *A made-up model. Real molecules are far too small to see, and enzymes are not scissors.*

**(f) Steady Inside, model `steady-v1`.** *A made-up model; the rule is on screen.* A sugar dial from 0 to 100 made-up points with a set point of 50 and a shaded band from 40 to 60 (not a real reading). Event cards, each once per run: **Supper digested** (+32) and **Long walk to the ford** (−16). Each **Next step**, the pancreas card shows insulin (level above 50) or glucagon (below 50), sized by the gap (lots 16 or more, some 4 or more, a little 1 or more), and the gap keeps half its size, rounded toward 50. From 82: 66, 58, 54, 52, 51, 50. From 34: 42, 46, 48, 49, 50. A **mill pond** twin runs the miller's sluice on the same rule. A **two systems** card compares a reflex (nerves) with insulin (hormone). A **temperature** link opens 174's dial. Model line: *Real blood sugar wobbles all day inside a range and is never this tidy. This dial measures nobody.*

**(g) Sprint and Puff, model `sprint-v1`.** *A made-up model of a made-up runner.* Pip on the Market Fair track. Pace buttons: **Rest**, **Walk**, **Jog**, **Sprint**. Each step, oxygen can cover up to 10 energy tokens. Demand: rest 2, walk 4, jog 10, sprint 40. The shortfall (sprint: 30) comes from anaerobic respiration, and lactic acid rises 1 mark a step. The learner always chooses Pip's pace; no lactate mark triggers heavy legs or a forced slowdown. At rest or walking, the schematic lactic-acid marker falls 1 mark a step, never below zero. A separate recovery card explains that extra oxygen after hard effort supports several processes, including refilling energy stores and reusing lactate; this bench does not calculate breathing or fatigue. A run holds at most 20 explicit pace actions, so its marker stays within 0-20; further actions refuse without discarding the run. A **per glucose** panel: aerobic 30 tokens, anaerobic 2 tokens. A **yeast jar** beside the baker's bowl shows bubbles and a *carbon dioxide + ethanol* label, with "most of the ethanol bakes off in the oven". Model line: *A made-up model. Real muscles mix both kinds of respiration and the amounts vary. Nothing here is advice about running.*

No timers, scores, streaks, medals or locks. Every bench is open from the first visit.
