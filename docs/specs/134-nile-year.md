## Purpose

H2 (historical worlds and encounters) owns 29 Ancient Egypt topics in the pinned Marble catalog, and none is covered by a built encounter. The only Egyptian content in the game is spec 95's two writing objects, which its own spec calls "narrow footholds" and which leaves geography, papyrus, writing systems and chronology to "separately reviewed teaching". Spec 120 §9 lists *The Nile and the Nilometer* as a candidate archive pack: a flood people depended on, set beside Hollis's flood post. This is that pack, broadened into a substantial Egypt encounter with real geography, farming, measurement, writing, counting and evidence.

A child comes to understand that:

- Egypt's farms and towns sat on a long, narrow strip of dark soil beside the Nile, with desert on both sides.
- The Nile flows north, so "Upper Egypt" is the south, upstream.
- The yearly flood brought water and new soil. People planned their whole year around it, in three seasons.
- People measured the flood on stone gauges called nilometers. Too low a flood meant hunger; too high a flood did damage.
- Scribes counted harvests and wrote them down, with their own number signs and a 365-day calendar that slowly slid against the seasons.
- We know these things from surviving objects, inscriptions and decipherment, and how objects and the dead are treated today is a real question.
- The Aswan High Dam, completed in 1970, ended the yearly flood below it: a "then and now" that makes the old year visible.

## The knowledge

All facts below are checked against the sources in the audit (search excerpts of reputable pages; direct page fetches were blocked, so a reviewer should open the URLs). No `[check]` items remain in this spec's knowledge; the granary photo's license is a build-time check.

**Land and river.** Egypt is in the northeast corner of Africa. The Nile flows north through it to the Mediterranean, ending in a wide, fan-shaped delta. Almost all farming happened on the strip of land the flood reached, with desert on both sides. Egyptians called their land *Kemet*, "the black land", for the dark soil the flood left, and the desert "the red land" (British Museum; National Geographic; Penn Museum). The game does not name the Egyptian word for the desert: it is usually written *dšrt* ("Deshret"), which is also the name of the red crown of Lower Egypt, and that overlap would confuse more than help. Later symbolic readings found online are not used.

**Upper and Lower Egypt.** Because the river flows south to north, upstream is south. Upper Egypt is the long, narrow valley in the south; Lower Egypt is the delta in the north. On most modern maps, Upper Egypt is at the bottom. The two lands were joined under one king; kings were called "King of Upper and Lower Egypt". Tradition names the unifier Menes. The child text does not name him or date the unification.

**The flood.** Heavy summer rains in the Ethiopian highlands swelled the river. The rise reached Aswan around July, kept rising through August and peaked around mid-September there; downstream at Cairo the peak came later, around October (Britannica, Nile hydrology). As the water went down, it left a layer of new, fertile silt.

**Three seasons.** The Egyptian year had three seasons of four months: **Akhet** (inundation, the flood), **Peret** (the season when fields emerged and crops grew; glossed "emergence" or "growing"), and **Shemu** (harvest, low water). Sources gloss Peret and Shemu slightly differently; the game uses "flood", "growing" and "harvest" and says these are English names for Egyptian words.

**Basin farming.** Farmers divided the floodplain with earth banks into large basins and used canals to lead floodwater in. Water stood for some weeks, then drained, and seed was sown into the wet silt. Staple crops were emmer wheat and barley, giving one main crop a year. The **shaduf** is a pole on a post with a bucket on the long end and a heavy weight on the short end, used to lift water by hand from a canal or river. The earliest Egyptian pictures of shadufs are from the 18th Dynasty, in tombs at Thebes and Amarna (for example Neferhotep, TT 50), about 1350 BCE (UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology, "Cereals"; Butzer). One Egyptological suggestion is that it arrived in the Second Intermediate Period; an older Mesopotamian seal also shows one. The game says "more than 3,000 years ago" and never shows a shaduf at Giza's building time. Shadufs were still used in Egypt in modern times.

**Nilometers.** A nilometer is a gauge for reading the river's height: a stair, a well or a column with marked levels. On Elephantine Island at Aswan, near Egypt's old southern border, a stair runs down to the river with level marks on its walls; it was one of the first places the rising flood could be read. The stairway is a Late Period construction (before 332 BCE) with high-water marks in demotic and Greek, repaired in Roman times, and brought back into use with a new scale in 1870 (Smithsonian Institution archives). Step counts in sources disagree and are not used. The nilometer on Roda Island in Cairo is from the Islamic period: the standing structure dates to about 861 CE, under the Abbasid caliph al-Mutawakkil. The game never calls Roda "ancient Egyptian" or "pharaonic". Readings helped officials expect the harvest and set taxes.

**Too low, too high.** A low flood left fields dry and could bring hunger; a very high flood could break banks and wash away houses and crops. A Roman writer, **Pliny the Elder** (1st century CE), gave a scale for one gauge: 12 cubits, famine; 13, hunger; 14, gladness; 15, security; 16, plenty (Natural History, Book 5, chapter 10 in the Bostock and Riley translation, section 58 in modern numbering; the game paraphrases and does not quote). His scale applies to his own time and one gauge. Different nilometers had different zero points, so a "good" reading on one gauge is a different number on another. This is the pack's central measurement idea, and it links straight to Hollis's and Nuria's posts.

**The cubit.** The royal cubit was a length of about 52 cm (surviving measuring rods run about 52.3 to 52.9 cm; scholarly estimates cluster near 52.4 cm). It was divided into 7 palms, each of 4 digits, so 28 digits. **The game rounds the cubit to 52 cm in every calculation and says so.**

**Calendar.** The civil calendar had 12 months of 30 days (360) plus 5 extra days at the end of the year: 365 days. Each season had four months. The real year from one season to the same season again is about 365¼ days. With no leap day, the civil calendar slipped against the seasons by about one day every four years, so over centuries the month named for the flood stopped matching the flood. A full slip back to the start took about 1,460 years (365 × 4). A later decree proposed adding a day every four years, but it was not kept. The Met dates the civil calendar to at least the middle of the Old Kingdom (about 2450 BCE) and possibly centuries earlier; its exact origin is unresolved. Child text does not date its origin.

**Scribes and counting.** Scribes were people trained to write. They kept records of harvests, stores and taxes. The Met's *Model of a Granary with Scribes* (from the tomb of Meketre, about 1981 to 1975 BCE; object 20.3.11) shows men carrying sacks and scribes recording, two with papyrus rolls and two with writing boards. Egyptian hieroglyphic numerals were additive, base ten, with no place value and no zero sign: a stroke for 1, a hobble (often called a heel bone) for 10, a coil of rope for 100, a lotus for 1,000, a bent finger for 10,000. Each sign is repeated as needed.

**Hieroglyphs and papyrus.** Hieroglyphs are a writing system of pictures used as signs; from the earliest readable texts, many signs stood for sounds, others for whole words, and others (determinatives) clarified meaning (Britannica; The Met). Papyrus was made from the papyrus plant that grew in marshes along the Nile and in the delta: the pith was cut into strips, laid in two crossing layers, pressed and dried, and sheets were joined into rolls. Scribes writing in ink on papyrus mostly used hieratic, a faster, joined-up script (Britannica; The Met). Spec 95 already covers the writing board and palette.

**Pyramids at Giza (later short reading).** The Great Pyramid at Giza was built for King Khufu of the 4th Dynasty, around 2550 BCE (Harvard's Digital Giza; other chronologies place it a few decades later). The game says "about 4,500 years ago". Archaeologists working at the town beside the pyramids (Heit el-Ghurab, the "Lost City of the Pyramid Builders") found bakeries, storerooms, houses and workers' tombs near the pyramids. Current scholarship reads this evidence as an organized, fed workforce with skilled crews and rotating laborers, not enslaved people. The evidence for rewards is strongest for the skilled crews; the game says "evidence suggests" and keeps the uncertainty.

**How we know.** The **Rosetta Stone** carries one decree from 196 BCE in three scripts: hieroglyphic, demotic and Greek. French soldiers found it in 1799; it passed to the British after the French surrender in 1801 and has been in the British Museum since 1802. Because scholars could read the Greek, it helped unlock the other scripts. Thomas Young made early progress; **Jean-François Champollion** announced his breakthrough in September 1822, helped by his knowledge of Coptic. Howard Carter found the nearly intact tomb of **Tutankhamun** in the Valley of the Kings in November 1922. The tomb's objects are now shown at the Grand Egyptian Museum near Giza, which opened fully in November 2025. **Ethics, gently:** who should keep objects taken far from where they were made (in 2022, petitions led by Egyptian archaeologists, including Zahi Hawass and Monica Hanna, called for the Rosetta Stone's return; the British Museum said no formal government request had been made and that British law limits returns: The Art Newspaper, NPR, Smithsonian); and how to treat the mummified dead with dignity. Museums today differ: some keep remains wrapped or covered, some use words like "mummified person" to remind visitors these were people. No image of a body appears in this pack.

**Then and now.** The Aswan High Dam was completed in 1970 (formally dedicated in January 1971). Its reservoir, Lake Nasser, holds back the flood, so the yearly inundation stopped below the dam. Farmers gained steady water for more than one crop a year; the silt that once renewed the fields is now trapped behind the dam, and farmers use fertilizer instead. The game states both sides and draws no verdict.

## The game or activity

- **Map card** (River and land): a simple schematic map, north arrow at top, Nile in blue, green strip, delta fan, the Mediterranean at the top. Tap *south* or *north* end to label Upper and Lower Egypt; a river-arrow toggle shows flow direction. Labeled *A simplified map. Not to scale.*
- **Season wheel** (The flood year): three segments. Tap a segment to reveal what the river and the fields are doing. A *drag the seed* card asks when to sow.
- **Shaduf bench** (The flood year): a side-on drawing. Tap **Lift** to swing one bucket from canal to field channel; a counter shows lifts and liters. **Model:** each lift brings up 10 liters; no spills, no tiredness. Line on the bench: *Made-up numbers for practice. Real buckets and lifts varied.*
- **Nilometer stair** (Measuring the flood): a stair with cubit marks 10 to 18. A slider sets the water; the child reads the mark, then sorts the reading with a **judging card** based on Pliny's scale (12 famine, 13 hunger, 14 gladness, 15 security, 16 plenty) plus a game-made line *above 17: too high, banks may break*. The card says: *Based on a scale a Roman writer gave for one gauge. Our "too high" line is made for this game.* A second gauge with a different zero point appears at Sapling and Keeper's depth.
- **Number tray** (Counting like a scribe): tap stroke, hobble, coil or lotus tiles to build a number; the running total shows in our digits; a **Read it** button reads back both. Free building is never checked.
- **Calendar slider** (Counting like a scribe): years step by 4; a marker for "flood month" slides one day per 4 years against a fixed "real flood" band. **Simplified picture:** the real year is about 365¼ days; the slider ignores the small remaining difference and the flood's own year-to-year variation.
- **Timeline cards** (How we know): drag or tap-to-place cards in order, with Earlier and Later buttons for keyboard and switch users. BCE dates are shown with "BCE" and the line runs left to right in time.
- **Source cards** (How we know): one public-domain museum photograph (granary model) with its record, and one described object (Rosetta Stone, text only). The granary photo's Open Access license is confirmed at build; if it cannot be, the card falls back to a text description. Sort statements into **You can see it**, **The museum says**, **Not in this source**.
- **Then and now card**: two simple drawings of the same field, flood year and dam year, with the trade-offs listed. No checked question.

No timers, scores, medals or locks. Help use, familiarity (a page opened before) and independent attempts are recorded separately.
