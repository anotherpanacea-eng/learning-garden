## The knowledge

Sourced in the [audit](../source-audits/254-one-seed-many-words.md).

**Plurals.** A plural noun normally adds -s or -es and means more than one (DfE glossary). The English national curriculum states the choice by sound: an ending heard as /s/ or /z/ is spelled -s, one that adds an extra beat (/ɪz/) is spelled -es (Appendix 1, Year 1; Common Core L.K.1c says /s/ or /es/). Child text uses the letter form, which gives the same spelling for every worked word except shelved *stomachs*: after s, x, z, ch or sh, usually -es (boxes, bushes, benches, foxes, radishes). Words already ending in e (house, prize) add a beat by sound but are spelled with just -s, which the letter form gets right. Exceptions named at Keeper's Desk: *stomachs* (ch sounding k); o-words split (potatoes, tomatoes; pianos, photos). Consonant plus y: change y to i and add -es (berries, daisies; Appendix 1, Year 2). Vowel plus y: add -s (trays, keys; Year 2's -ey guidance).

**Verb and adjective endings with no root change** (Year 1). -s or -es for the third person (fills, fixes, wishes); -ing, -ed; -er and -est on adjectives (taller, longest, freshest). The regular past ending sounds like /ɪd/, /d/ or /t/ (hunted, buzzed, jumped) but is always spelled -ed (Appendix 1); child text says most doing words show already done with -ed, since irregular pasts (pack 260) and variants like *burnt* exist. Children commonly spell it as heard before they spell it as a word part (Nunes, Bryant and Bindman 1997). -er also makes nouns for a person who does something (spec 34's *painter*): two endings, one spelling.

**Three root changes** (Year 2). **Drop e:** a root ending in a consonant and e drops the e before an ending starting with a vowel letter (hope, hoping, hoped; rake, raking; ripe, ripest); exception *being*. A consonant ending goes straight on (hopeful, movement); exception *argument* (US *judgment* is another). Appendix 1 states the rule for words ending in e with a consonant before it; words ending in oe, ee or ye keep the e before -ing (hoeing, toeing, seeing, dyeing), but -ue words usually drop it (arguing, rescuing) and spec 34's *tier* drops it after i, so child text never says a vowel before e blocks the rule. **y to i:** a root ending in a consonant and y changes y to i before -ed, -er, -est and -es, but not before -ing (carried, muddier, tiniest, tidied; carrying, crying); the only everyday words with ii are *skiing* and *taxiing*. Vowel plus y keeps y (sprayed, stayed), except *said*, *paid*, *laid*. **Double:** a one-syllable word ending in one consonant letter after one vowel letter doubles it before a vowel ending (hopping, biggest, wetter, planned), which keeps the vowel short (Appendix 1). Child text says "only one vowel letter" for "one syllable"; they agree on every worked word, and *quit, quitting* (qu) is why card 10 says "usually". x is never doubled (mixing, fixing; Appendix 1), nor are w and y (snowing, spraying; Britannica Dictionary). Two vowels or two consonants: no doubling (weeding, jumped). **Longer words (Keeper's Desk):** when the last syllable ends in one consonant letter after one vowel letter, double only when it is stressed (beginning, forgetting) and not when it is unstressed (gardening, visited) (Appendix 1, Years 3 and 4; Britannica). American spelling keeps one l on unstressed endings (labeled, traveled); British doubles it (labelled, travelled). The pack uses US forms.

**Odd plurals and group words.** A few nouns change differently: mouse, mice; goose, geese; tooth, teeth; foot, feet; man, men; child, children; sheep and fish usually unchanged (Common Core L.2.1b names feet, children, teeth, mice, fish). Mice, geese, feet and teeth keep an Old English vowel change once more common (*book* once changed too). leaf, leaves; loaf, loaves; half, halves; but roofs, chiefs: f to ves is a list, not a rule. Over-regularized forms (*mouses*, *foots*) are a normal stage; one study of ten children's speech found them in about 8.5% of chances (Marcus 1995). Collective nouns name a group: flock, bunch, swarm (L.2.1a).

**Roots, inflections and clues.** An inflection changes a word's grammar (plural, tense, comparison) and keeps it the same word; *dogs* and *better* are inflections (DfE glossary). A root word stands alone; endings attach to it. Common Core asks for roots and inflectional forms (look, looks, looked, looking; L.1.4c) and for inflections and affixes as clues to unknown words (L.K.4b, L.1.4b, L.3.4b). Explicit morphology teaching has small to moderate benefits for reading and spelling (Bowers, Kirby and Deacon 2010). Clue words used: *dibbled* (dibble, a hand tool for planting holes; to plant with one), *unripe*, *unwashed*, *trowels*.

## The game or activity

Every screen: *Spelling rules usually work. A dictionary has the last word.* No score, timer, lock or mastery label. Taps only; a word grows as a picture of more than one, or of the action. Wrong endings get a hint and stay tryable.

- **(a) The Plural Bed (Sprout, Seedling; first slice).** Eight seed cards (seed, pot, box, bush, bench, fox, berry, tray); tap a card, then -s, -es or "y to i, then -es". The bed grows the plural with a picture of more than one. Sort: one, or more than one.
- **(b) The Ending Bed (Sprout, Seedling).** Doing words (fill, fix, pick, weed, water) take -s or -es, -ing or -ed; describing words (tall, long, fresh) take -er and -est on a three-sunflower picture.
- **(c) The Changing Bed (Seedling, Sapling).** Four tools: *no change*, *snip the e*, *swap y for i*, *double it*. The child picks a tool, then an ending; a tool that does not fit the word shows its rule's reach. Sort nine joins into the four trays.
- **(d) The Odd Plot (Sapling).** Picture pairs for the odd plurals; a "rhyme trap" card (mouse, mice; house, houses); three group-word labels (flock, bunch, swarm).
- **(e) The Root Path (Sprout to Sapling).** Hob's note: tap a part (-s, -ed, un-) to light its clue, then choose a meaning. Root finder: take an ending off and undo its change (hopping, hop; hoping, hope; cried, cry; berries, berry).
- **(f) The Long-Word Corner (Keeper's Desk).** A grown-up says each word and you clap its beats together (no audio asset, nothing recorded): beginning, forgetting against gardening, visited, watering. The **honest exceptions shelf**: hoeing, being, argument, said, stomachs, potatoes and pianos, roofs, labeled and labelled.
