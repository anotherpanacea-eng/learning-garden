## Purpose

[Tenths Table](81-tenths-table.md) (`#numbers/tenths`) makes 0/10 to 10/10 on one fixed strip and names each amount as a fraction and a one-place decimal. [Hundredths Bridge](85-hundredths-bridge.md) (`#numbers/hundredths`) redraws the same whole as a 10×10 square and shows 0.3 = 0.30 and 0.35 = 0.30 + 0.05 for six supplied cases. [The Measuring Desk](92-measuring-desk.md) keeps two real jug readings and compares the entered numbers. None of them claims a Marble topic, and nobody yet teaches **thousandths**, **the value of each digit**, **comparing decimals of different lengths**, **dividing by 10 and 100**, **fraction-decimal equivalents beyond a few pictures**, **decimals in measures and money**, or **rounding decimals**. This pack takes twenty-one Decimal and Fraction topics there.

A child comes to understand that tenths, hundredths and thousandths are equal parts that nest by ten, so a thousandth is a tenth of a hundredth and smaller for having a bigger denominator; that 3 ÷ 10 is 3/10; that each digit is worth its number times its place and a zero holds an empty place open; that a decimal and a fraction can name one amount, read from the last place; that a decimal's length says nothing about its size; that dividing by 10 or 100 moves the digits while the point stays still, and a bigger unit needs a smaller number; that rounding picks the nearer post, once; and (Keeper's Desk) that every fraction's decimal either ends or repeats, while numbers like pi and √2 do neither, and there is always room for another number between two.

**Builds on, never re-teaches:** spec 81's fixed ten-part strip and fraction/decimal pairs (here: recapped in T1, then counting past one and sharing); spec 85's square of hundredths and 0.3 = 0.30 (here: recapped in T3 and T5, then a tenth of a hundredth, regrouping and adding tenths to hundredths); spec 92's real-jug readings (here: the River Post's made-up readings stay labeled as made up, and its "keep the digits you can report" note returns in F6). Sibling [spec 188](188-long-count.md) owns multiplying and dividing whole numbers by 10, 100 and 1000 and powers of ten; sibling [spec 191](191-long-orders.md) owns multiplying and dividing decimals by 10, 100 and 1000 and decimals times whole numbers; sibling spec 200 owns percentages. Here the free-standing ÷ 10 and ÷ 100 cases stay with one- and two-digit numbers; unit changes also use × 1000 and ÷ 1000 for their authored measures. **Not targeted:** percentages and *Decimal & Percent Notation* (`mt_W17Kbwm0-u`, spec 200), *Multiplying and dividing (age 10+)* (`mt_wPgpMJ0-PA`, spec 191), negative powers of ten (spec 188 suggested this pack; no topic asks for it), multiplying or dividing by a decimal, and any proof that a number is irrational.

## The knowledge

**Tenths, and past one.** A tenth is one of ten **equal** parts of one whole; ten tenths recompose that one whole (10/10 = 1, never "ten"). Counting on in tenths crosses one cleanly: 0.8, 0.9, 1.0, 1.1. Sharing makes tenths: 3 ÷ 10 = 3/10 = 0.3 (cut each of 3 rods into tenths, 30 tenths among 10). The whole stays visible and the same size.

**Hundredths and thousandths by subdivision.** A hundredth is a tenth cut into ten (1/10 ÷ 10 = 1/100); a thousandth is a hundredth cut into ten (1/1000). Ten thousandths make a hundredth, 100 thousandths a tenth, 1000 the whole. On a meter rod: 0.1 m is 10 cm, 0.01 m is 1 cm, 0.001 m is 1 mm. More parts make smaller parts: the bigger denominator names the **smaller** piece. The Measuring Rod's zoom performs each split; the reference chart only labels it.

**Tenths as hundredths.** 3/10 = 30/100, so 3/10 + 4/100 = 30/100 + 4/100 = 34/100 (convert before combining; never 7/110, never 3/100).

**Place value to thousandths.** A digit is worth its number times its place: in 4.378, 3 tenths (0.3), 7 hundredths (0.07), 8 thousandths (0.008). Each place is one tenth of the place to its left (a factor of ten, never "ten less"). A placeholder zero is worth nothing and holds a place open: 4 ones, 0 tenths, 7 hundredths, 3 thousandths = 4.073, not 4.73. A decimal is read by its last place: 2.035 is "two and thirty-five thousandths" (or digit by digit, "two point zero three five").

**Decimals and fractions both ways.** The last place names the denominator: 0.62 = 62/100, 0.07 = 7/100, 0.4 = 4/10, 0.025 = 25/1000. Fraction to decimal: 9/100 = 0.09, 3/10 = 0.3. Benchmarks grounded on the hundred grid: 1/2 = 0.5, 1/4 = 0.25, 3/4 = 0.75, 1/5 = 0.2; at Keeper's Desk 1/8 = 0.125 (half of a quarter). **The fraction bar means divide**, top by bottom: 3/8 = 3 ÷ 8 = 0.375 (not 8 ÷ 3); back again, 0.375 = 375/1000 = 3/8.

**Ends, repeats or neither (Keeper's Desk).** A fraction in simplest form ends exactly when its denominator is built only from 2s and 5s, the factors of ten (7/20 = 35/100 = 0.35); otherwise its decimal repeats forever (1/3 = 0.333...; 22/7 = 3.142857142857...). Ending and repeating decimals are **rational**: fractions of whole numbers. The test is repetition, never length: 0.333... is rational. Some numbers' decimals never end and never repeat: √2 and pi are **irrational**, kept in symbols; 3.14 and 22/7 are close, never equal. The root sign alone does not decide (√9 = 3). **Density:** between 0.12 and 0.13 sit 0.125, 0.121, 0.1205 and endlessly more; there is no "next" number after 0.12. The whole numbers, fractions and decimals each go on forever. Positioning negative integers is sibling spec 189's.

**Comparing and ordering.** Same length first: compare from the biggest place, and the first place that differs decides ($4.75 > $4.57, never right-to-left). Then ragged decimals, where both faulty rules show: **longer is larger** (0.45 > 0.5 "because 45 > 5") and **shorter is larger** (0.4 > 0.87 "because tenths beat hundredths"). Line up the places, filling zeros only once the meaning is clear (0.5 = 0.50 = 0.500), and compare from the left: 0.312 < 0.38 < 0.4 < 0.5. A length bar (ribbons on the rod) and the scale's tip show size; digit strings do not.

**Dividing by 10 and 100.** Every digit becomes ten (or a hundred) times smaller and moves one (or two) places right; **the point stays still**. 37 ÷ 10 = 3.7 (3 ones and 7 tenths); 4 ÷ 100 = 0.04, where a zero must hold the tenths. "Take a zero off" fails on 37; "move the point" hides why; neither is taught as the method.

**Decimals in measures and money.** Unit size decides the direction: a bigger unit needs a smaller number. 235 cm ÷ 100 = 2.35 m; 3250 g ÷ 1000 = 3.25 kg; 0.45 km × 1000 = 450 m; check that the answer makes sense. Adding and subtracting: line up the points, pad with zeros, work place by place, exchange across the point: 2.50 − 0.75 = 1.75 m; 1.500 − 0.375 = 1.125 kg; 1.000 − 0.264 = 0.736 (a complement to one comes from subtracting from 1.000, never from making each place up to ten). A fraction of an amount is division: 1/4 of $3.20 = 320 cents ÷ 4 = $0.80. Estimate first.

**Rounding.** Rounding picks the **nearer** of two posts on a line; the digit after the target place reports which half the number sits in. 3.2 → 3 (not up just because something follows the point), 3.7 → 4 (not chopped to 3). The exact midpoint is equidistant, so 6.5 → 7 by an agreed rule, introduced only after "nearest". Round **once** from the original: 3.47 → 3 to the nearest whole (3.47 → 3.5 → 4 is double rounding) and 3.5 to one place. Keep the target place: 12.95 → 13.0 to one place (the carry ripples; not 12.10, not 13). Rounding to any place: 3.748 → 3.75 to two places. **Significant figures** (Keeper's Desk) count from the first nonzero digit; leading zeros never count, trailing and placeholder zeros keep size and record precision: 0.04863 → 0.049 (2 sf) but 0.05 (2 dp); 35,482 → 35,000 (2 sf, not 35). Choose the accuracy for the job and the tool (F6). Whole-number rounding is spec 188's (recapped only).

**Real anchors (see audit).**
- **Al-Uqlidisi's stroke.** The *Kitab al-Fusul fi al-Hisab al-Hindi* by Abu'l-Hasan al-Uqlidisi, written in Damascus in 952-953, is the earliest known text with a direct treatment of decimal fractions. He marked the units digit with a small stroke above it. The only known copy was made in 1157; almost nothing else is known of his life.
- **Stevin's *De Thiende*.** Simon Stevin published *De Thiende* ("The Tenth") in Flemish in 1585, and his own French version, *La Disme*, the same year: a short practical account of decimal fractions for daily work, addressed to surveyors, wine-gaugers, mint-masters and merchants among others. He did not invent decimals; he spread their everyday use. He wrote a circled number after each digit for its place (2⓪3①5② for 2.35) and predicted decimal coins, weights and measures. The US Coinage Act of 1792 named a tenth-of-a-dollar coin the "disme", later "dime". The claim that Norton's 1608 English translation inspired Jefferson rests on secondary sources and stays out of child text.
- **Al-Kashi's pi.** Jamshid al-Kashi's *Treatise on the Circumference* (1424, Samarkand) gave 2π to nine sexagesimal places, equivalent to 16 decimal places, unsurpassed for almost two centuries; his *Key of Arithmetic* (completed 1427), a teaching text, uses decimal fractions. Pi's irrationality (Lambert, 18th century) appears in child text only as "later mathematicians proved", without a name or year.

## The game or activity

**(a) The Measuring Rod, model `tenth-rod-v1`.** One meter rod drawn the same size on every screen. **Cut into tenths** makes ten equal parts; **Put back together** recomposes them, and the count pauses at "ten tenths, one whole" before going on to 1.1. **Zoom** opens one tenth to its ten hundredths, then one hundredth to its ten thousandths, each split performed by the child; a size-compare tray drops one tenth beside one hundredth. **Share mode**: drag (or tap-to-place) 1-3 rods among ten traders; the rods must be cut first. *A made-up model: perfect equal cuts on a one-meter rod; the place values are real.* Recap of spec 81's strip, not a replacement.

**(b) The Place Board, model `place-board-v1`.** Columns ones · tenths · hundredths · thousandths around a fixed point. Tapping a digit reveals "7 hundredths, 7 × 0.01 = 0.07". **Build mode** gives value cards (4 ones, 0 tenths, 7 hundredths, 3 thousandths) to place, and an empty column cannot be skipped. **Regroup** turns one tenth into ten hundredths (and back) on a strip under the board. **Say it** reads the decimal by its last place. *A made-up model: tiles on a board; the places are real.*

**(c) The Two Names Board, model `two-names-v1`.** A hundred grid beside a 0-1 number line; shading moves the point, and both names appear only after the child shades and predicts. **Divide** runs top ÷ bottom digit by digit (3 ÷ 8 stops at 0.375; 1 ÷ 3 shows the same remainder returning and offers **Keep going**). **Simplify** divides 375/1000 by a common factor the child picks. Keeper's Desk adds the **Ends or repeats?** sort (a denominator-factor flap, simplest form first), the **Squeeze one in** zoom between two decimals, and the H3 card. *A made-up model: a picture grid and line; the amounts are real.*

**(d) The Market Scales, model `market-scales-v1`.** Two pans and a ribbon rack. The child **predicts** which jar is heavier, then **Weigh** tips the scale and **Show ribbons** lays each weight as a length on a rod; **Line up the places** writes both to the same number of places. Ordering sets of three or four tiles, ragged on purpose. The **Ledger** (Sapling up) lays prices and measures in aligned columns with zero padding and exchange arrows for money and measure problems in dollars and cents, meters and kilograms. *A made-up model: invented jars, labels and prices.*

**(e) The Slide Shelf, model `slide-shelf-v1`.** A place shelf from thousands to thousandths with the point nailed down. **÷ 10** and **÷ 100** (with **× 10**, **× 100** to undo) slide tiles one or two places; gray zeros fill empty places between the point and a digit, and a line states how far every digit moved. **Change unit** pairs cm and m, g and kg, mL and L, m and km, asks "bigger or smaller unit?" before sliding, and shows a sense check ("3.25 kg: a bit over 3 kg"). The g/kg, mL/L and m/km unit steps slide three places (× 1000 or ÷ 1000); cm/m slides two. Authored starts and offered operations keep both start and result in 0-9999.999, exactly representable with at most three decimal places on this shelf; undo offers the inverse of that same step. The model never offers a slide that would drop a nonzero digit beyond either end. If spec 188's Slide Chart ships first, this bench may reuse its renderer with this pack's limited preset and frozen sources; the models stay separately versioned. *A made-up model: tiles on a shelf; the rule is real.*

**(f) The River Post, model `river-post-v1`.** A tall post with a zoomable scale. A reading sits between two candidate posts; **Show the halfway mark** draws the midpoint; the child taps the nearer post, then the rounded value appears with its kept places. Precision switch: nearest meter, tenth or hundredth from the same reading, always from the original. Keeper's Desk adds **Highlight significant figures** and an accuracy chooser with the bank-sign case. *A made-up model: invented readings; the rounding rules are real.*

No timers, scores, streaks, medals or locks. Every bench is open from the first visit.
