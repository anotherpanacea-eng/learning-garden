## Purpose

The game's real history so far sits in Paris 1910 and one Egyptian writing pack (spec 95). H2-II and H2-III ask for everyday life, geography and artifacts from other periods, and the Marble *Ancient Greece & Rome* domain has no home at all in the built game. The story's own theme, keeping the ways open between towns, has a real ancestor in the Roman road and aqueduct network, and the story's valley sits in the Pyrenees, a region the Romans crossed with the Via Domitia.

A child comes away understanding that:

- Romans built roads in layers, with a curved top and ditches so rain ran off; not every Roman road was paved with big flat stones, and builders used whatever stone or gravel was nearby.
- Milestones gave distances in Roman miles of 1,000 paces, written in Roman numerals. A pace is two steps, about 1.48 m, so a Roman mile is about 1.48 km.
- Aqueducts moved water with no pumps: the channel sloped gently downhill all the way, so gravity did the work. Most of an aqueduct's length ran in covered channels at or below ground. Arches were built only where the channel had to cross low ground.
- The Pont du Gard carried the aqueduct to Nîmes across the river Gardon. The whole channel dropped only about 12 to 17 m over about 50 km, an average of about 25 cm per km by the official site's figure.
- We know these things from surviving structures, inscriptions such as milestones, Roman writers such as Vitruvius, and archaeology, and each kind of evidence leaves questions open.

## The knowledge

Adult prose for reviewers. Every claim is sourced in the source audit; `[check]` marks what could not be confirmed in this pass.

**Roads.** On many Roman roads, builders dug two shallow ditches and piled the earth into a raised bank, the *agger*. They rammed in large stones or cobbles as a base and packed finer gravel or pebbles on top. The surface was cambered (curved, higher in the middle) so rain ran off into the ditches. Materials depended on what was nearby; on soft ground a road might sit on timber piles and brushwood (English Heritage). Soldiers did much of the road building, especially on campaign and near army bases; other roads were let out to contractors, who used hired, enslaved and convict labor (sources differ on the balance). Quality varied: one stretch of Dere Street at Catterick was little more than spreads of gravel (Historic England). Roman road types ranged from earth tracks through gravel roads to paved roads, so "Roman road" does not mean "paved with big flat stones". The four named layers in many textbooks (statumen, rudus, nucleus, summa crusta) come from a later reading of Vitruvius and vary from site to site; the spec does not teach those names.

**Milestones and the Roman mile.** A Roman mile was *mille passus*, 1,000 paces. A pace (*passus*) was two steps, five Roman feet, about 1.48 m, so a Roman mile was about 1.48 km (sources differ slightly because the exact Roman foot is uncertain). Milestones usually named the emperor with his titles and gave the distance in Roman miles from a named town, in Roman numerals. A milestone of Hadrian found near Thurmaston in 1771 (RIB 2244, now in Leicester) gives the distance as two miles from Ratae (Leicester). Its date, 119 to 120 CE, comes from the emperor's titles carved on it. It was found about 375 m farther out than two Roman miles from Leicester's east gate, and its editors discuss where the distance was measured from. Inscriptions often wrote four as IIII rather than IV; today IV is the usual form. Carved numbers can be hard to read: one milestone in Britain (RIB 2283) is read by some scholars as LIII (53) and by others as LIIII (54). Roman numerals have no zero and no place value.

**The Via Domitia.** The first Roman road built in Gaul, it linked Italy with Spain through what is now southern France, from the Alps (Montgenèvre pass) to La Junquera in Spain, passing Narbonne. It is named after Gnaeus Domitius Ahenobarbus and dated to about 120 to 118 BCE (sources differ). A milestone with his name and the number XX (20) was found in 1949 at Treilles, 20 Roman miles south of Narbonne; a French scholar calls it probably the oldest Latin inscription in Gaul. That is an expert judgment, not a settled fact, and the text says so. It crossed the Pyrenees at the Col de Panissars, near Le Perthus, where excavations in 1984 found wheel ruts cut in the rock and foundations identified as Pompey's trophy of 71 BCE (English-language sources say "probably" for the trophy).

**Aqueducts.** An aqueduct carried water from a spring to a town in a channel that sloped gently downhill the whole way, so gravity moved the water with no pumps. Most of the length ran in covered channels and tunnels at or below ground; arches were built only where the channel crossed a valley or low ground. For the city of Rome, National Geographic gives about 12 percent of the channel length on arches, and Britannica (via search summary) about 50 km out of about 420 km. Where a deep valley lay across the route, some aqueducts used sealed pipes that carried water down one side and up the other, and the far end still had to be a little lower than the start (the Gier aqueduct to Lyon had four such crossings). Vitruvius, a Roman architect writing in the late first century BCE and dedicating his book to Augustus, gave a rule for the smallest slope of a channel; surviving copies and editions of his book disagree on the number (one reading, *sicilicus*, gives about a quarter of a Roman inch per 100 feet; another, *semipede*, half a foot per 100 feet), which is itself a lesson about texts.

**The Pont du Gard and the Nîmes aqueduct.** The Pont du Gard is the bridge that carried the aqueduct to Nîmes across the river Gardon in southern France. It has three tiers of arches, about 49 m high; its top tier is about 275 m long and carried the water channel. The official site gives the aqueduct's length as about 50 km and its average gradient as about 25 cm per km; the straight-line distance from spring to town is only about 25 km, so the channel wound along the hills. Total drop figures in sources range from about 12 m (which fits 25 cm per km) to about 17 m (spring near the Fontaine d'Eure at Uzès to the distribution basin in Nîmes, which gives about 34 cm per km). The text teaches the range and why it exists. The slope was not even: some sections were far gentler than others. Date: the official site gives about 50 CE, under Claudius or Nero; newer excavations point to the middle of the first century CE (about 40 to 60 CE in some secondary accounts), while older books said about 19 BCE under Agrippa. It supplied Nîmes for about five centuries. UNESCO World Heritage listing, 1985. Estimated flow figures (up to about 40,000 cubic meters a day) are modern estimates and stay at the Keeper's Desk only.

**Water in a Roman town.** In Nîmes the aqueduct ended at a round distribution basin (*castellum*) whose outlets fed pipes to different parts of town. Aqueduct water supplied public fountains, where most people collected water, public baths and latrines, and some private homes whose owners paid. Vitruvius (8.6.1-2) advised a three-part tank so public basins and fountains would not run short, with baths and private houses fed separately; whether real towns built it that way is debated (one study of Pompeii's castellum found its outlets on one level). The child text attributes the rule to Vitruvius and does not claim Nîmes followed it. The text does not claim every house had piped water, and it does not claim continuous flushing of Nîmes's latrines specifically.

**How we know.** Surviving structures (the Pont du Gard, road sections such as the one uncovered in Narbonne in 1997), inscriptions (milestones), Roman writers (Vitruvius on aqueducts) and archaeology (newer excavations that moved the Nîmes aqueduct's date to the middle of the first century CE; the coin evidence often cited for this is reported only in secondary sources, so the child text does not mention coins). Each leaves gaps: a milestone tells a distance and an emperor, not who walked the road.

**Pompeii** is left to spec 139. This pack does not mention the eruption.

**What is fiction.** Les Saules, its bridge, the miller's mill race and Bramble's ribbons are fiction. The Channel Bench's valley and its rules are a made-up model.

## The lesson

Each card follows Jane's **teach, guided, practice** order: one idea and a worked example revealed a step at a time; a first item guided with one to four visible steps; then practice.

| Card | Idea | Worked example | Contrast | Top misconceptions designed against |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Building a road | Layers and a curved top keep a road dry. | Rain falls on a flat top and a curved top. | A flat road that holds puddles. | All Roman roads were paved with flat stones; a road is just stones laid on grass. |
| 2. Reading a milestone | Roman numerals add, except a smaller one before a bigger one subtracts. | XIV = 10 + (5 − 1) = 14, shown on a stone. | XVI = 16. IIII on old stones. | Reading XIV as 16; thinking there is a Roman zero; IL for 49. |
| 3. Paces and miles | 1 pace = 2 steps; 1,000 paces = 1 Roman mile, about 1.48 km. | II on a stone: 2 × 1.48 ≈ 3 km. | A kilometer is shorter than a Roman mile. | A pace is one step; a Roman mile equals a modern mile; adding instead of multiplying. |
| 4. Water walks downhill | A channel must drop a little all the way. | 1 m down over 4 km is 25 cm each km. | A flat channel, an uphill channel. | Aqueducts used pumps; aqueducts were mostly arches; steeper is always better. |
| 5. Water in town | The aqueduct fed fountains, baths, latrines and some paying homes. | Following water from basin to fountain. | A house with a private pipe. | Every house had a tap. |
| 6. How we know | Each source tells some things and not others. | Hadrian's milestone: tells a distance, not who walked. | A guess that goes past the source. | A source tells everything; an expert's "probably" is certain. |

**The "I'm stuck" ladder** on every item: (1) a hint; (2) a worked example of an easier similar case plus how to start this one; (3) skip for now, with no penalty and no record beyond "skipped". Help used is recorded separately from independent attempts.

## The game: the Channel Bench

**Model `channel-v1`, a made-up valley.** A spring sits on a hillside; the town sits lower down, 6 model meters below the spring. Between them are a hill and a valley. The explorer lays a channel in three legs:

| Leg | Choice | Length (model km) | Work (made-up units) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Spring to the valley's edge | Around the hill | 12 | 12 |
| | Tunnel through the hill | 4 | 8 |
| Across the valley | Around the valley head | 14 | 14 |
| | Bridge on arches | 2 | 6 |
| Valley's edge to town | Fixed channel | 4 | 4 |

**Rules, stated on the bench:**

1. The channel always runs downhill. The fall (6 m in the main levels) is spread evenly over the whole length. *(Real channels were not even; the model keeps it simple.)*
2. Drop per km = fall ÷ length.
3. Steeper than 0.5 m per km: **too steep in this model**. The water runs fast and the animation shows the channel wearing.
4. Gentler than 0.25 m per km: **too flat in this model**. The water slows and the animation shows mud settling.
5. From 0.25 to 0.5 m per km, the water **flows** to the town fountain.
6. Work counts what it costs to build: 1 per km of plain channel, 8 for the tunnel leg, 6 for the bridge leg.

**Every result shows:** *This is a made-up model with simple rules. Real Roman channels had many different slopes, and engineers used other tricks too.* The rule-3 and rule-4 limits are game numbers chosen so choices bite; they are not Roman rules and not engineering advice.

**All designs, checked by script** (2026-10-25):

| Fall | Route | Length | Work | Drop per km | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 6 m | Around hill, around valley | 30 km | 30 | 0.20 m | too flat |
| 6 m | Around hill, bridge | 18 km | 22 | 0.33 m | flows |
| 6 m | Tunnel, around valley | 22 km | 26 | 0.27 m | flows |
| 6 m | Tunnel, bridge | 10 km | 18 | 0.60 m | too steep |
| 4 m | Tunnel, bridge | 10 km | 18 | 0.40 m | flows (the only one) |
| 8 m | Any but tunnel and bridge | 18 to 30 km | 22 to 30 | 0.27 to 0.44 m | flows |
| 8 m | Tunnel, bridge | 10 km | 18 | 0.80 m | too steep |

The shortest route is not always best; the right route depends on the fall. Two routes work at 6 m, so the child chooses and says why.

**Levels.**

| ID | Title | What the child does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `c1` | Which way does water go? | Tilt one test channel: flat, gently down, steeply down. Watch. (Sprout) |
| `c2` | Around or through? | Only the hill leg is open; the valley is bridged. Try both; read drop per km. (Seedling) |

**Interaction.** Each leg is a pair of large cards (min 44 px targets). Tapping a card draws that leg on the valley picture. **Run the water** animates the result and fills a small table: route, length, work, drop per km (with its worked division shown on tap), result. At 320 px the picture sits above the cards and the table becomes a list. Narration reads every result.

**Keep my route** (end of `c3` and `c4`) offers stems: *I chose this route because…*, *What it costs is…*, *A worry about it is…*, with the child's own runs shown beside them. Nothing grades the claim.

Optional root `ways` per explorer, absent until a card or level opens:

```
ways: {
  cards: { [cardId]: { source, items: { [itemId]: { attempts: [{ choice, help, at }], skipped } } } },
  bench: { [levelId]: {
    source,                       // frozen model version, legs, fall, rules, notices
    runs: [{ route, fall, result, help, at }],   // latest 12 readable runs
    kept: { route, fall, runs, claim: { because, cost, worry }, at } | null
  } }
}
```

- Store choice codes, route codes and computed results, never generated prose. `source` freezes the understood model or card version (at most 8 KiB). Historical runs render from their saved result and are never recomputed from a later model.
- `help` records support (hint, worked example) separately from independent attempts; skipping records only "skipped".
- An unreadable item, run or unknown version stays exact and exportable; only the affected actions become read-only. One bad entry never closes the shelf or the bench.
- Claims are bounded to 2,000 characters per field and preflight through the existing `prepareProgressImport` limits. Export, import, reset and explorer switching use existing shared behavior. No new storage backend.
