## Purpose

Strand **H2** (historical worlds and encounters) has 25 Medieval Times topics in the pinned Marble catalog and no built encounter for any of them ([six-act strands](../six-act-strands.md), row "Medieval Times ... 25, 0"). The built Travelers' Archive teaches inquiry with one twentieth-century case (Paris 1910); the Egyptian writing book covers the ancient world. Nothing yet covers ordinary life in medieval Europe.

This pack fills that gap with an archive pack in three linked readings set in real medieval Europe, chosen because the story already has a mill, bridges, a mountain valley in the Pyrenees and old Ways between towns:

1. **The mill and the village:** the manor, peasants' obligations and food, the lord's mill (suit of mill and the miller's share, *multure*), and Domesday Book (1086) as a real survey that counted mills.
2. **The road over the mountains:** pilgrimage to Santiago de Compostela, the Pyrenees crossing at Roncesvalles, the scallop badge, pilgrim hospitals, the eleventh-century bridge at Puente la Reina, and the twelfth-century pilgrims' guide in the Codex Calixtinus.
3. **Towns, trade and who did the work:** markets, fairs, guilds, what a castle was for, the "pyramid" as a simplified picture, women's work (alewives) and an abbess (Hildegard of Bingen), and a "legend or history?" sort for Robin Hood and King Arthur.

A child comes to understand that most medieval people were farmers whose year followed the seasons; that a mill was valuable property, and its owner set the rules for its use; that travel on foot was measured in day stages and needed bridges and shelter; that a record such as Domesday Book answers its own questions and is silent on others; and that legends are stories people told, distinct from history with evidence. It also practices fractions of a quantity, distance sums, division and survey tables (strand **L1** through the miller's share and market change, and **S6** lightly through water power, which is left to the mill's own workshop).

Knight stories, sieges and dragons are deliberately left out (knights get one definition card). The Black Death is omitted.

## The knowledge

Checked facts (two search passes, 2026-10-25), with sources in the audit. No open check markers remain; the audit lists what rests on secondary sources and what a subject reviewer should confirm.

**Period label.** "Middle Ages" is a European label for roughly 500 to 1500 CE. Britannica gives about 500 to 1400-1500, with start and end varying by region; the term was first used by fifteenth-century scholars. Other regions have their own periods; Marble's "Medieval Worlds Beyond Europe" is a separate topic not attempted here.

**Village and manor.** Much land was held by lords and farmed by peasants in a manor (village plus fields), with holdings in scattered strips of the open fields. Dependent (villein or serf) holdings owed labor services or produce, often several days' work a week on the lord's demesne, and villeins were bound to their manor. Bread was the main staple, often of rye, oats or mixed grain; pottage (peas, grain, vegetables) and ale completed most meals; peas and beans gave protein and pigs most meat.

**The farming year.** Spring sowing of oats, barley, peas and beans; haymaking in early summer; grain harvest in late summer; autumn plowing and sowing of winter wheat and rye; threshing in the colder months; winter repairs. Medieval calendars (the "Labours of the Months") and treatises such as Pietro de' Crescenzi's (c. 1304 to 1309) show these tasks, but months differ by manuscript and region, so the wheel accepts seasons, with two seasons accepted for harvest and threshing.

**The lord's mill.** Under the custom called *suit of mill*, manorial tenants had to grind their grain at the lord's mill; grinding at home or elsewhere could be fined, and some lords confiscated hand mills. Scholars disagree on whether free tenants were bound as well as unfree ones. The miller took a share of the grain, *multure*, which varied by time and place, from about 1/30 to 1/10 (higher in northern England). The pack's 1/16 is labeled as an example inside that range.

**Domesday Book.** King William I ordered the survey at Gloucester at Christmas 1085; it was carried out in 1086. It records, county by county, who held land and what resources and value it had: plows, categories of people, mills, fisheries and more. The National Archives describes it as a fiscal inventory, a statement of estate structures and a legal record of who held land. The questions are known from the Ely Inquiry, a satellite record: who held the manor; how many plows in lordship and among the men; how many villeins, cottars, slaves, free men and sokemen; how much woodland, meadow and pasture; how many mills and fisheries; what it was worth; each recorded for three times (King Edward's day, when King William granted it, and 1086). The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle's writer complained that not an ox, cow or pig was left out (paraphrased; translations differ). **Mills:** counts differ. A commonly cited figure is 5,624 (described as a low estimate); Cambridge University Press and the Mills Archive say over 6,000; one journal article gives 6,082. Many entries are fractions of a mill, a "mill" may mean a set of millstones, and the survey is incomplete (Devon and Cornwall record strikingly few mills, a puzzle still debated). The pack teaches "more than 5,600, and by some counts over 6,000," and uses the disagreement as a lesson.

**The pilgrim road.** Pilgrimage was a religious journey to a holy place. Santiago de Compostela, believed to hold the tomb of the apostle Saint James, drew pilgrims from the ninth century on; UNESCO's World Heritage listing (1993, extended 2015) describes the French Route (about 738 km in Spain) as the main route from the eleventh century, with hospitals, hostels, monasteries and bridges built to serve pilgrims. The Camino Francés crosses the Pyrenees to Roncesvalles (Roncevaux); the modern Napoleon route from Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port is about 25 km and climbs from about 200 m to the Lepoeder pass at about 1,430 m.

**Hospitals.** At Roncesvalles, Bishop Sancho de Larrosa of Pamplona set up a pilgrim hospital, most often dated 1127 (some sources give 1132 and credit King Alfonso I); an older hospice of San Salvador stood at the Ibañeta pass above. The pack says "around 1127" and states the disagreement.

**The scallop shell.** The scallop was the badge of Saint James's pilgrims; the London Museum says nobody is quite sure why. Shells were first taken from the shrine; metal badges followed as demand grew. The Codex Calixtinus (Book III) refers to pilgrims' shells. The British Museum holds a lead-alloy scallop badge, 1856,0701.2106, which it dates c. 12th to 16th century (a secondary exhibit page says about the 12th century; follow the museum).

**Puente la Reina.** A Romanesque bridge over the River Arga in Navarre, built in the eleventh century for pilgrims, ordered by a Navarrese queen whose identity is debated (Doña Mayor or Doña Estefanía). Six arches are visible; some guides say a seventh lies buried under the first house at the town end, and the Spanish tourism site says five. The pack says six can be seen and some guides report a buried seventh.

**The pilgrims' guide.** The Codex Calixtinus (*Liber Sancti Jacobi*) was compiled around the late 1130s or early 1140s; the oldest copy is dated 1173. Its Book V is a practical guide, generally credited to the French monk Aymeric Picaud (attributed, not certain); the papal attribution to Calixtus II is generally considered false. Book V lists 13 stages from the Cize pass to Santiago, some explicitly on horseback, describes shrines and lodging, and makes hostile remarks about people along the route. The pack teaches this last point as source reading: good evidence of the writer's views, weak evidence about the people he judged. No quotation from the guide is used.

**Daily distance.** Modern walkers on the Camino Francés average about 23 to 25 km per stage. Researchers re-walking a medieval route in period gear found about 25 to 30 km a day a good average, and estimated 25 to 32 km from a fifteenth-century letter. The pack labels 20 to 30 km an estimate. Route-planner legs are rounded modern distances: Saint-Jean to Roncesvalles 25 km, Roncesvalles to Zubiri 22, Zubiri to Pamplona 21, Pamplona to Puente la Reina 24.

**Towns and trade.** In England a weekly market and an annual fair (some lasting two weeks) were licensed by royal charter; the lord holding it took stall rents, tolls and court fines. Guilds regulated quality, prices and training (apprentice, journeyman, master). Historic England describes castles as lords' homes and centers of power, local administration and justice; few were primarily military. The "pyramid of power" is a teaching simplification that historians have criticized since Elizabeth Brown's "The Tyranny of a Construct" (1974); the Church held land alongside it, towns had merchants outside it, and some peasants were free. Knights were mounted fighters serving a lord, often rewarded with land.

**Women.** Judith Bennett's research shows that women brewed and sold much of England's ale before about 1350 (brewsters, alewives); records survive partly because the Assize of Bread and Ale regulated prices and quality; by 1600 men dominated the trade. Hildegard of Bingen (c. 1098 to 1179) was a Benedictine abbess, writer and composer; she founded Rupertsberg about 1150 and Eibingen in 1165; nearly 400 letters survive; she was named a Doctor of the Church in 2012. Peasant women reaped, threshed, made hay, spun, did dairy work and brewed (State Library Victoria, National Archives teaching resource). Women of landholding families sometimes ran estates while husbands were away; Margaret Paston, in fifteenth-century Norfolk, is the best documented English case. Lords feasted in the great hall, and some fed servants and tenants at Christmas.

**Legends.** Robin Hood: the earliest literary allusion is in *Piers Plowman* (c. 1377), where "rhymes of Robin Hood" are already known as idle tales; the earliest surviving ballads are from the later fifteenth century; no real individual is confirmed as his source. King Arthur: Geoffrey of Monmouth's *History of the Kings of Britain* (c. 1136) made Arthur's story famous; by the sixteenth century many of its "facts" were accepted as Geoffrey's invention; Arthur's historical existence is unproven: Gildas (sixth century) does not name him, the first datable mention is the ninth-century *Historia Brittonum*, and Thomas Charles-Edwards wrote that the historian can as yet say nothing of value about him.

## The lesson

Each reading follows Jane's order: **teach** (one idea per card, 30 words or fewer, a worked example revealed step by step where there is a procedure) then **guided** (the first item shows 1 to 4 steps before answering) then **practice**.

- **Reading 1:** cards 1.1 to 1.11; worked example on the miller's share (16 measures at 1/16); guided item (48 measures at 1/16, steps "48 ÷ 16" then "48 − that").
- **Reading 2:** cards 2.1 to 2.9; guided item (75 km at 25 km a day).
- **Reading 3:** cards 3.1 to 3.11; it feeds the record and legend jobs.
- **Each job** opens with its own teach card and worked example (haymaking goes in summer; 22 + 21 = 43 km is a very long day; count houses row by row; "1 mill" says nothing about busy; Domesday Book is in an archive, so it's history).

**"I'm stuck" ladder** on every graded item: (1) the item's hint; (2) an easier worked case plus how to start this one (1/4 of 8; 10 + 10 km; two rows of 3 houses; "find the exact words"; "is there evidence?"); (3) **Skip for now**, with no mark against anything. Support, familiarity and independent attempts are recorded separately.

**Contrasts.** A record says this / a record is silent (Domesday-style entry). History with evidence / legend (Domesday Book against Robin Hood). A stage in a guidebook / a day's walk (13 stages over about 750 km, some on horseback). A bigger fraction / a bigger denominator (1/16 against 1/24). A simplified picture / a real society (the pyramid card's own caveat).

**Top misconceptions designed against.**
1. Medieval people were mostly knights, kings and castle-dwellers. (Most were farmers; the year wheel and the survey count plows and households.)
2. A bigger denominator means a bigger share (A3).
3. A record that doesn't mention something proves it didn't exist (D6: brewing appears in other records).
4. A guidebook's opinion is fact about the people it describes (D9), and its opposite, that a biased source is useless.
5. A stage means one day (B7). Counting places instead of stretches (B3).
6. Legends are history because they're old and famous (Job 6).
7. Domesday Book has one exact mill total (C6 and card 1.10).

## The game or activity

1. **Readings:** a card carousel per reading, Back/Next, read-aloud on every card, a **Details** fold for the longer facts, and the label (*A true thing from history* / *A story from Willowbank*) in a fixed position.
2. **The year of work (`wheel`):** a wheel in four quarters. Eight task cards; drag or tap-then-tap to place. Two cards accept two seasons. A card in an unaccepted season shows its hint once and stays put until moved. **Done for now** always available. No score.
3. **The miller's share (`share`):** problem set A, with a sack-and-measures picture: tapping "the miller's share" lifts the right number of measure tokens out of the pile for the item's numbers. Model line on screen: *A simplified picture. Real shares changed by place and time. 1/16 is an example from inside the real range.*
4. **Plan a pilgrim's days (`route`):** a schematic line map of five places with four rounded legs (25, 22, 21, 24 km) and a mountain shape over the first leg. The child taps places to stop; each day's total appears; a day over 30 km shows "That's a very long day on foot"; **Rest day** adds a 0 km day. **Model:** distance per day is the sum of legs walked that day, in km; assumptions: rounded modern distances, no terrain or weather adjustment, any day length allowed. The model line says it is a simplified picture and medieval paths differed. Problem set B sits beside the map.
5. **Survey Willowbank (`survey`):** a drawing of fictional Willowbank (14 households in rows of 5, 5, 4; three plow teams of 8 oxen; one mill; two eel traps; four groups of 5 pigs; a church). A seven-row survey table with questions paraphrased from the Ely list. A "count from the picture" helper highlights items one at a time. "What is it worth?" is answered *not in the picture*. Problem set C follows. Labeled fiction, with the questions labeled as real.
6. **What the record tells (`record`):** a made-up, labeled Domesday-style entry for Willowbank; statements are sorted into **The record says this** / **The record doesn't say** (two bins), then three real-survey and guidebook items (problem set D).
7. **Legend or history? (`legend`):** six cards into **History** / **Legend**. One card (a dragon at Roncesvalles) is labeled as made up for the sort.

All screens work at 320 px: the wheel becomes four stacked season lists; the route map becomes a vertical list of legs.
