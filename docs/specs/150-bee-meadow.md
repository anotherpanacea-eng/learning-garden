## The knowledge

**What makes an insect (building on spec 62, not re-teaching it).** Spec 62 counts legs on four adults. This pack adds the body plan: head (antennae, eyes, mouthparts), thorax (all six jointed legs and any wings) and abdomen, inside a hard outer skeleton (the exoskeleton), with no bones inside (S22). Spiders are arachnids with two body parts and eight legs, all on the front part (S23). Harvestmen are arachnids too, with one oval-looking body, no silk and no venom; they are not spiders (S24). Wings are not the test: bats and birds fly, and some adult insects, such as female glow-worms, have no wings (S21). The rule is for adults: a caterpillar has three pairs of jointed true legs on its thorax plus unjointed prolegs on its abdomen, and a fly maggot has no true legs at all (S25).

**Two ways to grow (going beyond spec 65).** Spec 65 shows one small white's four stages, without stage functions or other insects. Here:

- **Complete metamorphosis:** egg, larva, pupa, adult. The larva eats and grows; inside the pupa the body is broken down and rebuilt into the adult. Beetles (including the seven-spot ladybug, a ladybird in Britain, common across Europe up to the mountains, S30), flies, butterflies and moths, and bees, wasps and ants grow this way: more than half of all known animal species (S14). A hoverfly larva is a legless, aphid-eating maggot that pupates inside its hardened last skin (S34).
- **Incomplete (gradual) metamorphosis:** egg, nymph, adult, with no pupa. The nymph looks like a small, wingless adult, and its wing pads grow with each molt; the meadow grasshopper has four nymph stages (S26, S37). Grasshoppers and crickets are examples (the meadow grasshopper occurs in the Pyrenees, S31). Dragonfly nymphs live underwater for months to years, then climb a stem, molt one last time and fly (S19). "Incomplete" names a pattern; nothing is missing or broken.
- **Both kinds molt.** An exoskeleton cannot stretch, so every growing insect sheds it. Only complete metamorphosis has a pupa.

**A honeybee colony.** A summer colony holds tens of thousands of workers (often given as up to about 50,000 to 80,000), a few hundred drones and one queen (S6, S7). Workers are sterile females; drones are stingless males whose job is mating (S33); the queen lays eggs, about 1,500 to 2,000 a day at the spring and early summer peak (S6, S38). Development from egg to adult takes about 16 days for a queen, 21 for a worker and 24 for a drone, and eggs hatch after about 3 days (S5). A worker's jobs roughly follow her age: cleaning cells, then caring for young, then building, storing food or guarding, then foraging outside from about three weeks old (S17). The order is a tendency; colonies shift workers as needs change (S17). The queen does not command: her scent influences the colony, and jobs emerge from the workers (S27). Ants also live in colonies with queens and workers; many ants lay scent trails that fade unless more ants keep adding to them, so busy, short routes to food stay strong (S18).

**The waggle dance.** A forager back from good flowers runs a straight line on the comb while waggling, loops back and repeats. Karl von Frisch decoded it, was doubted, was later confirmed, and shared the 1973 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Konrad Lorenz and Nikolaas Tinbergen (S1, S2). The run's angle from straight up on the vertical comb matches the flowers' angle from the Sun's direction: straight up means toward the Sun, straight down away from it (S28). A longer waggle run means farther flowers. Seconds per kilometer differ by bee, colony and landscape, so there is no single conversion (S3, S4). Decoded dances put typical foraging at about 0.5 to 2 km, farther in summer (S35). Bees signal; they do not speak a language with words.

**Pollination.** Bees visit flowers for nectar (sugary food) and pollen (protein food for young). Fuzzy bodies pick up pollen, and some rubs off on the next flower of the same kind of plant, which lets it make seeds and fruit. (Many plants, apples among them, also need that pollen to come from a different plant or variety; spec 149, *The Orchard Year*, owns that plant-side rule, and this pack does not teach it.) The bee is feeding; the plant's benefit is a side effect. Bees see ultraviolet light, and many flowers have ultraviolet patterns that lead to the nectar; red looks dark to most bees (S16, weak sources). Hoverflies, butterflies, moths and beetles pollinate too. Of 115 leading food crops reviewed, 87 depend on animal pollination for some or all of their fruit, vegetable or seed production; measured by the amount of food grown, crops that depend on pollinators make up about 35% (S8). The two numbers measure different things. About 20,000 bee species are described; Britain alone has about 270, nearly 250 of them solitary (S9).

**Hide, warn, copy.** Camouflage hides an insect against its background (a green caterpillar on a leaf, a meadow grasshopper in grass). Warning colors advertise a real defense: a wasp's stripes go with a sting, and seven-spot ladybugs ooze a bitter fluid that is distasteful and toxic to some birds (S29); a predator that has one bad experience learns to avoid the pattern. Mimicry borrows the warning: hoverflies cannot sting but look like wasps or bees. Henry Walter Bates described it from Amazon butterflies (Linnean Society, 1862); Fritz Müller explained in 1878 why two defended species can share one warning (S11). To tell a hoverfly from a wasp, look for one pair of wings (flies) rather than two, short antennae and very large eyes (S10). The insect does not choose its look; these appearances came about over many generations.

**Threats and what helps.** A study of 63 nature protection areas in Germany found a 76% fall in the weight of flying insects caught in traps over 27 years (82% in midsummer); the cause was not explained by the weather or land-use changes the authors measured (S12). In southern England, roadside hedgerows under street lights had 47% fewer moth caterpillars than unlit ones, and grass margins 33% fewer; white LED lights had stronger effects (S13). In 2018 the European Union banned outdoor use of three neonicotinoid insecticides after its food safety agency found risks to honeybees and wild bees (S20). What helps: let some grass grow long and flower, grow flowers that bloom from spring to autumn, avoid sprays, leave dead wood, and shield or switch off outdoor lights at night. Citizen science helps: France's Spipoll (MNHN and OPIE, since 2010) asks volunteers to photograph insects on one flower for 20 minutes (S32).

| --- | --- | --- |

| Two Ways Board | Pupa or no pupa; does the young look like the adult? | Only butterflies change; the pupa rests; nymph equals larva; "incomplete" means broken |
| Hive Window | Different members, different jobs | The queen gives orders; all bees do the same job; drones do the work |
| Dance Floor | The run's angle gives direction; its length gives distance | Bees talk in words; the dance shows only direction; a long dance means more food |
| Pollen Path | Pollen rides from flower to flower while the bee feeds | Bees carry pollen on purpose to help; pollen is nectar or a seed; only bees pollinate |
| Hedge of Tricks | Hide, warn or copy, from the predator's view | Insects choose to hide; bright colors are for looks; the mimic is dangerous too |

## The game or activity

**Body Bench.** A build-an-insect tray: a head, thorax and abdomen snap together; six legs, two antennae and up to four wings sit in a parts dish. Each part can be dropped onto any segment. A wrong-segment build is allowed and gets a kind note. Then a **sort**: eight lifelike meadow cards (ant, ladybug, hoverfly, grasshopper, garden spider, woodlouse, harvestman, caterpillar) go into **Adult insect / Young insect / Not an insect / I can't tell from this picture**.

**Two Ways Board.** Two side-by-side cycle tracks with empty slots. Stage cards for a seven-spot ladybug (egg, larva, pupa, adult) and a meadow grasshopper (egg, nymph, nymph after a molt, adult) are dragged or tapped into place. Then four new insects (dragonfly, hoverfly, honeybee, cricket) are sorted by answering two yes/no questions shown on the card: **Is there a pupa? Does the young look like the adult?** A worker-bee strip (egg 3 days, 21 in all) feeds two number items.

**Hive Window.** A glass-hive cutaway with tappable role tokens (queen, cleaners, nurses, builders, guards, foragers, drones), each opening a job card. A **What if?** card asks about a week of rain with no foraging (no health meter, no harm shown).

**Dance Floor.** A vertical comb with a sun marker above the hive and a simple map of the valley below it. **A simplified picture with a made-up rule:** run angle from up equals the flowers' angle from the Sun (S28); one second of waggling means one kilometer (real timing varies, S3). The Sun's direction is given, not computed. **Read a dance:** the child watches a looped dance (with a half-second run timer as a reading aid, never a time limit) and taps the matching patch on the map. **Make a dance:** the child sets the run's angle (45° steps, 15° steps at Keeper's Desk) and length to send a bee to a named patch. Four authored patches, Sun in the southeast (bearing 135°):

| Patch (fiction) | Bearing | Distance | Run angle from up | Run length (model) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Orchard blossom | 135° | 0.5 km | 0° (straight up) | 0.5 s |
| Hob Pennick's clover | 225° | 1.5 km | 90° right | 1.5 s |
| Lavender by the mill | 45° | 1 km | 90° left | 1 s |
| The far meadow | 315° | 2.5 km | 180° (straight down) | 2.5 s |

**Pollen Path.** Three apple blossoms on three mutually compatible apple varieties flowering together, and a clover head along the path, so every apple-to-apple visit can set fruit with the varieties explicitly compatible, consistent with spec 149's variety rule. The child steers a bee (tap the next flower) to drink nectar; yellow pollen dots visibly stick to its hairs and rub off on the next apple blossom. A blossom that got pollen grows into a small apple on the autumn view; a blossom that got none falls. A hoverfly can take the bee's place for a second run. **A simplified picture:** one visit is enough and summer is skipped. A link line points to the Orchard Year for why most apples need a compatible different variety.

**Hedge of Tricks.** A hedge scene seen through **a hungry robin's eyes**: tap anything you can spot. Three insects are hard to see (a green caterpillar on a leaf, a moth on bark, a meadow grasshopper in grass), two are bold (a wasp, a ladybug), one is a hoverfly. After the spotting, each is sorted into **Hide / Warn / Copy**. A **close look** panel puts the wasp and hoverfly side by side so the child can count wings and compare antennae.

**Keeping the Meadow.** Six threat cards (mowing everything, spraying, all-night lights, no late flowers, tidied dead wood, a changing climate) each pair with a helpful action. The child picks any three; each gets one line on why. Nothing simulates populations.
