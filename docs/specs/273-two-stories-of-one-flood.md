## The knowledge

Sourced in the [audit](../source-audits/273-two-stories-of-one-flood.md); unconfirmed claims were cut.

**Firsthand and secondhand.** A firsthand account is by someone who experienced or witnessed the event (a diary, a letter, an autobiography, an eyewitness report); a secondhand account is by someone who did not, working from others' accounts, records or research (a biography, a textbook, many news reports). The form is a clue, not the test: a reporter on the bridge writes firsthand, a reporter working from phone calls writes secondhand. The Common Core standard RI.4.6 asks children to compare the two and describe the differences in focus and information. A firsthand account usually gives close detail, sequence and feeling from one place; a secondhand account usually gives context, totals and causes, and may smooth away the witness's doubt. What a firsthand writer reports being told is secondhand within the firsthand text.

**Several accounts.** RI.5.6 asks children to analyze several accounts of one event and the points of view they represent. Accounts differ because of where the teller stood (access), what they care about (stake), when they wrote and why. Differing is not lying, and agreement on some facts does not make the rest true. Historians call the habit of asking who wrote a source, when and why "sourcing" (Wineburg 1991).

**Narrator's point of view.** The narrator is the voice that tells a story, not the author. A first-person narrator is a character who says "I" and usually knows only what they see, hear and think. A third-person narrator refers to characters as "he," "she" or "they"; a limited third-person narrator follows one character's thoughts, an omniscient ("all-knowing") narrator can report any character's thoughts. RL.5.6 asks how a narrator's point of view shapes how events are described.

**Unreliable narrators and dramatic irony.** An unreliable narrator is one whose account the reader has reason to doubt: they may lie, be mistaken, or not understand what they see. Clues include contradictions within the telling, slips that reveal more than the narrator admits, and other accounts that disagree. The term comes from Wayne C. Booth's *The Rhetoric of Fiction* (1961). Dramatic irony is the gap when the reader knows something a character does not; RL.8.6 names it as a source of suspense or humor. Unreliable does not mean lying: an honest, mistaken narrator is also unreliable.

**Purpose, audience and context.** A text's purpose (to record, inform, entertain, persuade) and its audience shape its word choice, register, layout and tone; its context (when, where, why written) helps a reader understand it. The KS3 English programme of study asks pupils to know "the purpose, audience for and context of the writing" and draw on it to support comprehension.

**The real pair: the Great Fire of London, 1666.** The fire began early on Sunday 2 September 1666 at the king's baker's house on (or just off) Pudding Lane, was spread by a strong east wind after a long dry summer, burned for about four days, and destroyed more than 13,000 houses and Old St Paul's Cathedral. Samuel Pepys, a naval official, kept a diary from 1 January 1660 to 1669 in Thomas Shelton's shorthand, for himself; it was first deciphered by John Smith and published, in part, in 1825. His entries for 2 and 4 September 1666 record the maid Jane waking the house at about 3 a.m., his going back to sleep, seeing "a lamentable fire" from a boat, pigeons that would not leave their houses, being told where the fire began, and burying his Parmesan and wine. The excerpts follow Henry B. Wheatley's edition (1893-1899, from Mynors Bright's decipherment; Wheatley died 1917), which is public domain; six short excerpts are used, in diary order, each beside a modern line. The *London Gazette*'s printing house burned; it reappeared on Monday 10 September, eight days after the fire began, with an account of the fire, described as for "satisfying the minds" of the king's subjects. Death figures are left out of the pack.

## The game or activity

Every screen: *Comparing accounts isn't picking a winner. Look at what each one shows and what it leaves out.* No score, timer, lock or mastery label. Drags have tap equivalents. All texts are short and on screen; optional read-aloud.

- **(a) Was You There?** Six cards into two trays (*the writer was there* / *wasn't*) with per-card feedback and a clue-words card. Then the storm-night pair side by side: tap a line, then **Only here** or **In both**.
- **(b) The Fire Diary.** Six Pepys excerpts with modern lines, and a newly written history page. Place five detail cards under *Only Pepys*, *Only the history page* or *Both*. Act IV version: page 4 beside *From the Archive*.
- **(c) Many Tellings.** Four storm-night accounts (miller, Hob, orchard keeper, baker). Find two facts all four agree on, then a detail only one gives; for each, **Why so different?** (where they were, or what they care about: both fit, and the bench shows the other). Act IV version: the two *Gazette* pieces and the orchard keeper's account; **What does each leave out?**; Bramble's objection; ends on *What we still don't know*.
- **(d) Who Is Telling?** The ribbon told three ways. Name each narrator; tap what each could know; **Switch the window** to the magpie's "I."
- **(e) The Magpie's Version (Keeper's Desk).** Clue hunt for three contradictions; sort the magpie (fibs) and Pip (honest but mistaken); a card on dramatic irony.
- **(f) Who Is It For?** Four storm texts (the mill book, Dot's notice, a picture-book page, the baker's sign) matched to job and audience. Act III adds Dot's letter to Nuria. Keeper's Desk: Pepys's diary, the *Gazette* and the history page matched to when, who for and why.
