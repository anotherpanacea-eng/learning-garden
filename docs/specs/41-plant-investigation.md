# Seedlings after the storm

First science increment in the next spiral. A small Living Garden study asks how light changes an already-sprouted seedling’s appearance, what a pictured observation can show, and what remains an explanation. It supplies a source-backed qualitative contrast with explicitly invented illustrative observations, never a pretend real experiment or growth forecast.

## Experience

Entry links after the existing Living Garden and planting-plan tasks open `#living/plants`. Four compact pages: predict → inspect observation days → compare → plan. Back, direct step choices and taking a break preserve the exact draft. Everything remains open. The relevant current model, day, conditions, units and selected observation stay beside the immediate prompt; on narrow screens use a compact source panel and one task below. No timer, external account, actual photo/audio/location collection, real-day waiting, inventory charge, mastery label or gate.

The fictional gardener is rebuilding a shelf for **already-emerged cress seedlings**. Compare adequate light with too little light. The same plant variety, starting stage, containers, growing medium, elapsed time, air, suitable temperature and **suitable moisture** are supplied controls. Equal suitable moisture does not assert identical watering volumes. Four relative observation days (0, 2, 4, 6) can be inspected manually; they are model days after emergence, not guaranteed germination dates or a calendar schedule.

Every model/table/carried snapshot prominently says its pictures and numbers were invented for comparing. The biological pattern is informed by cited sources; these are not measured cress results. Two labelled specimens A/B per condition show slight variation. Stem length is measured from the illustrated soil surface in millimetres; more than length stays visible: leaf colour, leaf spread and stem form. The drawings must agree with their numerical lengths and descriptors. No health score. Dim plants can be longer, paler, thinner and have smaller leaves; adequate-light examples are more compact with greener, broader leaves. Do not depict full yellow/no chlorophyll in merely dim light, or teach that shade is bad for every species or more light is always better.

Illustrative data (independently reviewed as a qualitative teaching model, not a measured dataset):

| Model day | Adequate-light A/B stem mm | Dim A/B stem mm |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 20 / 19 | 20 / 19 |
| 2 | 25 / 24 | 34 / 32 |
| 4 | 31 / 29 | 49 / 46 |
| 6 | 38 / 36 | 65 / 61 |

Starting pictures match. Later adequate-light leaf spread increases and remains green; dim leaf spread changes little and becomes paler, with thinner stems. This depicts a qualitative response, not a calibrated species-specific growth model. Independent source/spec review approved these arbitrary values as a labelled illustration; implementation review must still check the drawings and wording.

## Tasks and evidence

**Prediction:** “Which seedlings do you think will have longer stems?” Adequate / dim / similar / not sure are all welcome and unscored. A prediction made after seeing later model days is kept as after-looking, never relabelled before-looking. Observing first remains available; an absent prediction is unobserved. Day-0 source pictures are allowed context before a prediction.

**Inspect:** Choose a model day and inspect four labelled specimens, with exact length/leaf descriptions and an accessible text equivalent. A “Keep this observation” button copies that exact specimen/day into a small table; duplicates do not create rows. Keep which days were actually shown and the notebook selection separate from a prediction or understanding claim. Maximum 16 possible observation rows. The table labels model provenance, day, condition, specimen, millimetres and visible leaf/stem features. Selecting/copying a supplied row is participation, not proof of measurement skill. A more active Field Notebook/chart loop follows as a separate increment.

**Compare:** Compare the same day across the two conditions; day 6 is suggested but any day is available. Two original finite tasks distinguish observation from explanation and question “taller means better.” Example: an illustrated stem length/leaf descriptor is an observation in the model; “the low light caused this” is an explanation that this simplified model illustrates, not a direct real observation. Prompts must state the source and avoid asserting causality from four invented specimens. Feedback points to visible lengths/leaves, not a generic right/wrong mark. Optional human “I see… / I think…” discussion is unscored. Help, a smaller different example, narration, retries and familiar replays remain separate; a supplied answer never becomes independent science evidence.

**Plan:** Choose adequate light / dim / not sure for the fictional cress-starting shelf, then choose one observation to carry. Keep the chosen plan even if it prompts revision. A snapshot includes the exact day/condition/specimen/length/leaf description, model provenance and source support; a garden plan never copies a success label. Show it beside `#regrow` as a planning corner with a return link. The ordinary pretend planting grid remains unchanged; no actual crop spacing/yield/food-growth claim or instant café ingredients.

An optional fair-comparison question asks which **proposed water test** would be useful: same light and other relevant conditions with different measured water amounts, versus changing water and light together. We supply no water-result data. Recognising the controlled comparison is narrower than conducting or independently designing an experiment. Explain a confounded choice and allow revision.
