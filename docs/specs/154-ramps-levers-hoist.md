A child comes to understand that a force is a push or pull one thing gives another; that some forces need touching and some (gravity, magnetism) act across a gap; that arrows show a force's size and direction, and equal opposite arrows mean no change in motion (still **or** steady movement); that gravity pulls everything with mass, and heavy and light things fall together when air resistance is small, which is why a hammer and a feather landed together on the Moon; that air resistance pushes against motion and grows with area and speed; that levers, ramps, pulleys and gears let a smaller force lift a load **in exchange for moving farther**; and that magnets attract only some metals.

## The knowledge

**Forces.** A force is a push or a pull that one object gives another. It can start, stop, speed up, slow down or turn something, or change its shape. A still object can have forces on it. Things stop because something pushes back, never because a push "runs out". The Workshop's and spec 147's words are reused.

**Contact and non-contact.** Contact forces act only when objects touch: a push, a rope's pull, friction, air and water resistance. Non-contact forces act across a gap: gravity, magnetism (static electricity is mentioned once). The sorting question is *do they have to touch?* Gravity always pulls, on everything with mass; magnetism pulls or pushes, on some materials only. Neither needs air.

**Force arrows.** One labeled arrow per force, tail on the object, longer for bigger, in relative squares. **Balanced** forces are equal and opposite and leave the motion unchanged: still stays still, moving keeps the same speed and direction. Balanced never means "no forces" or "stopped". **Unbalanced** forces change the motion toward the bigger side; the leftover arrow is the resultant (Sapling and up).

**Gravity and falling.** Near the Earth, gravity pulls everything toward the center. It pulls harder on a heavier object, which is also harder to get moving, so with little air resistance heavy and light objects fall together. Flat and crumpled paper (same weight) show air's effect. Things fall together **only when air resistance is small**.

**Air resistance and friction.** Friction acts where surfaces slide, against the motion. Air resistance is the same kind of push-back from air: opposite to motion, growing with area and speed; water resistance is its cousin. A falling object speeds up until air resistance matches its weight, then falls steadily (Keeper's Desk; *terminal speed* in the glossary only). Parachutes are designed for a lot of air resistance, streamlined shapes for little.

**The history of falling.** Aristotle's view, long accepted, was that heavier things fall faster. In 1586 Simon Stevin and Jan Cornets de Groot dropped two lead balls, one about ten times heavier, about 30 feet (about 9 m) from a church tower in Delft onto a board; they landed at nearly the same time, and Stevin published it that year in *De Beghinselen der Weeghconst*. The Leaning Tower of Pisa story comes from a biography by Galileo's student Vincenzo Viviani, dated 1654 and first printed in 1717; Galileo's own writings discuss heavy and light bodies falling (his early, unpublished *De Motu*, about 1590, even mentions drops from an unnamed "high tower"), but never say he used the Leaning Tower, and many historians doubt it happened as told. In *Two New Sciences* (1638) Galileo described rolling bronze balls down a long, gentle grooved board to slow the fall, timing each roll by catching a jet of water in a glass and weighing it on a balance; distances in equal time steps went 1, 3, 5, 7, so totals went 1, 4, 9, 16. On 2 August 1971, near the end of Apollo 15's third moonwalk, David Scott dropped a 1.32 kg aluminum geological hammer and a 0.03 kg falcon feather from about 1.6 m; they landed together. Moon gravity is about one sixth of Earth's (NASA: 1.62 against 9.80 m/s²), so the drop looked slow.

**Machines trade force for distance.** A **lever** turns on a pivot (fulcrum); the load is lifted, the effort is your push. Pivot closer to the load: smaller push, longer path for your end. The bench's model balances when kg × distance is equal on both sides (Keeper's Desk preview of *moments*, a later topic). A **ramp** n times longer than its height needs 1/n of the lifting force (no friction). A **pulley** with *n* rope sections holding the load needs load ÷ *n* of pull and lift × *n* of rope; one fixed pulley only turns the pull around. **Gears**: a small gear driving a big one turns more times with less turning force. In every ideal model here, push × distance stays the same; the reason (energy) lives in spec 147. Real machines have friction, so you always need a bit more.

**Real anchors.** Many watermills lifted sacks to the top (bin) floor with a hoist driven by the waterwheel: a control cord engaged the drive, a chain or rope wound onto a drum, and the sack pushed up through trap doors that fell shut behind it; one mill guide says the miller counted the bangs. Archimedes (Syracuse, 3rd century BCE) proved the law of the lever in *On the Equilibrium of Planes* and is credited with the compound pulley. Plutarch's story of him moving a loaded ship and the saying "give me a place to stand and I will move the Earth" (through Pappus, centuries later) are adult background only; this pack has no child card for them.

**Magnets.** Magnets attract iron, steel, nickel and cobalt. Most metals are not attracted: aluminum, copper, brass, gold, silver, zinc, tin. Looks are not the test; testing is. A real counter-example from Europe: the euro 1, 2 and 5 cent coins look like copper but are copper-covered steel, and the Banco de España lists them as strongly magnetic; the 10, 20 and 50 cent coins are a copper alloy and are not. **Attracted** to a magnet differs from **being** a magnet: flip the magnet, and only a magnet is pushed away. Poles appear only in the glossary.

**"I'm stuck" ladder** on every item: (1) the hint; (2) an easier worked case plus how to start this one (a door you push; a ball on a table; two marbles; a seesaw with equal friends; one fixed pulley; a paper clip and a copper coin); (3) **Skip for now**, with no penalty and nothing recorded against the child.

**Contrasts:** as in the teaching-contract table below.

**Top misconceptions designed against:** heavier things always fall faster; everything always falls together; no gravity on the Moon; gravity is magnetism or needs air; a moving thing carries a push that runs out; balanced means no forces or stopped; still things have no forces; arrows floating off the object; air resistance is the same for everything, acts downward or is wind; machines make force from nothing; all metals or all shiny things are magnetic; attracted means it is a magnet.

## The game or activity

**(a) Touch or gap?** Ten storm clean-up cards (cart push, rope on a sack, magnet and nails, falling apple, wind on a sheet, Trundle's brake, a rubbed balloon and Ember's hair, the floor under a sack, a magnet through paper, the brook on a log) into *They touch* or *Across a gap*. Each placement shows feedback; any card can move.

**(b) Arrow Board.** One object per board: hanging sack, even and uneven tug-of-war, Trundle rolling steadily, falling apple, sack on the floor. Arrows come from a tray, snap tail-first to the object, need a label and a length (1-4), and the child then predicts *stay still or keep going the same*, *speed up or turn*, or *slow down*. **Show leftover arrow** at Sapling. *A simplified picture: arrow lengths are relative, not measured.*

**(c) Drop Door.** A loft door 4.9 m up. Pairs: heavy and light balls, flat and crumpled paper, hammer and feather, plus a **Moon** switch that removes air. Predict, then watch an authored slow-motion drop (results in the companion). Keeper's Desk readout: 1.0 s with no air on Earth, about 2.5 s on the Moon. *A made-up model: g = 9.8 m/s² and 1.62 m/s², air left out; real air slows light, wide things much more.* **Galileo's Ramp** side panel (Sapling): a ball in a gentle groove leaves a tick at each equal water-clock step, spaced 1, 3, 5, 7.

**(d) Lever Bench, model `hoist-lever-v1`.** *A made-up model; rules on screen.*
- **Lever:** pegs 1-5 each side; sacks of 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 kg, at most two per side; the beam tips toward the larger total of kg × pegs and levels when equal. Sprout and Seedling get a pivot slider with a push bar that shrinks as the pivot nears the load and a dotted path that lengthens.
- **Ramp:** a 40 kg barrel up 1 m on ramps of 1, 2 or 4 m: push 40, 20 or 10 kg-weight beside the path length.
- **Pulley:** 1, 2 or 4 rope sections; pull = load ÷ sections; rope = lift × sections.
- **Gears:** a 12-tooth handle gear drives a 36-tooth drum gear; the drum winds 0.5 m per turn.
- "kg-weight" means "as hard as holding up that many kg". Assumptions: weightless beam, ropes and pulleys; no friction; point loads; a rigid beam. A **Real hoist** toggle adds a little friction ("you need a bit more than the model says").

**(e) Magnet Sweep.** Twelve mud objects: steel nail, iron hinge pin, steel paper clip, aluminum can, copper pipe, brass key, silver spoon, wooden peg, plastic button, pewter cup (mostly tin), a copper-colored steel coin, a small magnet. Predict, sweep, sort. The small magnet triggers the flip test. *A picture of what real magnets do with these materials, not a test of a real object.*

No timers, scores, streaks, medals or locks. Every activity is open from the first visit.
