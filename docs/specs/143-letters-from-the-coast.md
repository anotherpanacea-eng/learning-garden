## The knowledge

Every fact below has a source in the audit (fact-check pass, 2026-10-25). Where only secondary or educational sources were found, the audit says so.

**What the ocean is.** The ocean is salt water and covers about 71% of Earth's surface, so there is more ocean than land. The ocean holds about 97% of Earth's water. Sea water is not safe to drink; river, lake and tap water are fresh (tap water is also treated). The ocean is home to a huge variety of living things.

**One ocean, five names.** The world ocean is one connected body of water, divided by people into five named oceans: Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Arctic and Southern. The Southern Ocean surrounds Antarctica. The US Board on Geographic Names approved the name "Southern Ocean" in 1999, NOAA says most countries, the US among them, now recognize it as the fifth ocean, and National Geographic began mapping it as an ocean in 2021. Its boundary was proposed to the International Hydrographic Organization but has not been ratified by all members. So the count of oceans is a human naming choice that changed recently. The Pacific is the largest ocean and the Arctic the smallest (and shallowest). A sea is usually a smaller part of an ocean, or joined to one.

**Coasts.** A coast is where land meets the sea. Coasts can be sandy, rocky or muddy. Different animals suit each: limpets and crabs on rock, burrowing worms and wading birds on mud, shells and shorebirds on sand (typical examples, not strict rules): wading birds probe mudflats for worms and shellfish, and sanderlings probe wet sand for small buried animals.

**Tides.** At most coasts the sea rises and falls twice in a little more than a day. High tides come about 12 hours 25 minutes apart on average, and low tide falls roughly halfway between, about 6 hours 12.5 minutes after high tide. Two highs and two lows take about 24 hours 50 minutes, a "lunar day", so tides come about 50 minutes later each day. The Moon's gravity is the main cause; the Sun's pull adds to it. At new Moon and full Moon the Sun and Moon line up and tides are bigger (**spring tides**, a name from the tide "springing forth", nothing to do with the season). About a week later, when the Sun and Moon pull at right angles, tides are smaller (**neap tides**). Spring tides come about twice a month in every season. Exact times and heights vary from place to place, so tide tables are made for each place.

**Big tides and small tides.** The Mediterranean has small tides, generally about 30 cm between high and low water (with larger exceptions in a few places such as the northern Adriatic and the Gulf of Gabès). Atlantic coasts have much larger tides. The French and Spanish Atlantic coast on the Bay of Biscay has mean spring tides of about 6 m at its northern end near Ouessant, falling to about 3.5 m near Biarritz (Britannica). This matters for Willowbank's valley: the Pyrenees run between the Bay of Biscay at their west end and the Mediterranean at their east end, about 430 km long, so two seas a few hundred kilometers apart have very different tides. A tide table for one place does not work for another.

**Waves and currents** (brief, to keep causes separate). Waves are made mainly by wind blowing over the water; the wave's energy travels while the water mostly moves round in place. Currents are large, steady flows that carry water, warmth and nutrients around the world, driven by winds, Earth's spin and differences in temperature and saltiness. Tides, waves and currents have different main causes.

**Rock pools.** When the tide goes out on a rocky shore, water stays behind in hollows. These rock pools are small ocean habitats that change twice a day: at low tide they can get warmer, saltier or less salty (in rain), and lower in oxygen; at high tide the sea covers them again. Shallow pools high on the shore change most. Common animals on European Atlantic shores, each with one coping move:

| Animal | What it does when the tide goes out |
| --- | --- |
| Common limpet (*Patella vulgata*) | Clamps its shell tight to the rock, keeping water in. Returns to its own spot on the rock. |
| Beadlet anemone (*Actinia equina*) | Pulls its tentacles in and becomes a small jelly-like blob, holding water inside. |
| Shore crab (*Carcinus maenas*) | Hides under seaweed or rocks, or in a crack, where it stays damp. |
| Common periwinkle (*Littorina littorea*) | Pulls into its shell and shuts it with a little hard "trapdoor". |
| Barnacle | Shuts the plates at the top of its shell. |
| Common starfish (*Asterias rubens*) | Lives in rock pools and on the lower shore, where water stays; it is an animal, not a fish. |

An anemone is an animal, not a plant. A rock pool is a habitat, not a puddle. Look, don't grab; put back anything moved.

**Herons on the shore.** Gray herons (*Ardea cinerea*) hunt fish and other small animals in shallow water, standing still and striking. They feed on coasts and estuaries as well as rivers and lakes (secondary sources; no RSPB page was returned). Hollis is a fictional heron; this is the real bird's behavior.

**Ocean food chains and webs.** Ocean food starts with **phytoplankton**, tiny drifting plant-like living things that make food using sunlight. A simple chain: phytoplankton is eaten by zooplankton, which is eaten by small fish, which is eaten by a shark. A **food web** joins many chains, because most animals eat more than one thing and are eaten by more than one thing. The web here uses one coherent set (from the Marble record): phytoplankton, zooplankton, small fish, squid, seals and sharks. Links (simplified, from educational sources): zooplankton is eaten by small fish and squid; small fish by squid, seals and sharks; squid by seals and sharks; seals by sharks. Real diets vary by species and place. The arrow points from the eaten to the eater and means **"is eaten by"** (food and energy move that way). If one kind disappears, effects may spread along several paths; outcomes are stated as "may", never guaranteed.

**Whales and dolphins.** Whales and dolphins are mammals: they breathe air at the surface through a blowhole, keep their bodies warm themselves, and feed their babies milk. Fish breathe with gills; that is the most reliable fish clue (many sharks give birth to live young, and some lay eggs). Sharks are fish. Living in water does not make an animal a fish.

**Depth and light.** Light fades with depth. Ocean scientists name zones by light: the **sunlight zone** (top 200 m, where phytoplankton can grow and most ocean life lives), the **twilight zone** (about 200 to 1,000 m, light fades), and the **midnight zone** (below about 1,000 m, no sunlight; down to about 4,000 m, with deeper zones below). Sources agree on these anchors; the boundaries are gradual fades, not walls. Some life lives in the deep dark too. Pressure and deep-sea specifics are left for older topics.

**Protecting the ocean (one gentle card).** Litter on beaches can reach the sea and harm animals; most marine litter starts on land and reaches the sea through drains and rivers. People help by beach cleans and by keeping litter out of drains and rivers. The card describes what people do; it never asks a child what they do at home, and carries no guilt.

## The games

**Tide clock (model `tide-v1`).** A side view of a beach with a rock, a rock pool, a harbor wall with a painted height scale, and a clock. The child drags or taps the clock hands in 15-minute steps (keyboard: arrow keys), or taps **+1 hour** / **-1 hour**. The water level follows the model:

- level = mean + A × cos(2π × (t − t_high) / 745), with t in minutes, period 745 min (12 h 25 min), A fixed per mode;
- **Ordinary** mode (default), **Spring tide** (larger A) and **Neap tide** (smaller A) buttons; units on the scale are unlabeled "marks" in Sprout and Seedling views and meters on the Keeper's Desk view, with A = 2 m ordinary as a made-up example;
- the first high tide is set by the card (06:10 by default; the child may move it);
- markers show "high tide" and "low tide" on the clock face at t_high, t_high + 372.5 min, t_high + 745 min and so on, displayed to the nearest minute;
- the rock pool appears only when the level is below the pool's rim.

Every view shows: *A simplified picture. Real tides vary from place to place and day to day.* The model is a smooth repeating curve; in it the water rises slowest near high and low tide and fastest halfway between (1 : 2 : 1 across the three thirds of a rising tide, checked by script). It leaves out the shape of real coasts, weather and the slow change between spring and neap tides. It gives no safety advice and must never be read as a tide table.

**Rock pool at low tide.** A top-down pool photo-style illustration with six hidden animals (table above). Step 1, **Find**: tap seaweed and rocks to discover each animal (no timer; a "show me where" help button). Step 2, **Close look**: each found animal opens a card with a real photo slot (open-license, audit) and its name. Step 3, **The tide goes out**: the water drops; each animal changes (limpet clamps, anemone closes, crab hides, periwinkle shuts). Step 4, **Sort**: drag each of the five animals with a coping move to that move (clamp tight / close up / hide / shut the door; periwinkle and barnacles share the last). The starfish is not sorted: it stays in the pool. Step 5 (optional, grown-up reads): *Act out one animal's move and say why it helps.* "Look, don't grab" is on the bench's first card.

**Food web builder.** A board with organism cards and an arrow tool. The child first builds a 4-card chain by tapping one card then the card that eats it; the arrow draws from eaten to eater with the label "is eaten by". A **Read it aloud** button reads the chain in the arrow direction. Then a second chain sharing small fish is overlaid, and the web grows from chains (never shown as a finished tangle). **What if?** removes one card and highlights every connected arrow, asking what *may* happen along each path (up and down). A reversed arrow is never marked wrong silently: the bench reads it back ("This says the shark is eaten by the small fish. Is that right?").

**Mammal or fish?** Seven animal cards and two baskets. Each card has a **Check the clues** flap: breathes air or uses gills; feeds babies milk or not. The child sorts, then says why for one card (grown-up reads, not graded). Cards: dolphin, harbor porpoise, gray seal, a whale (humpback), shark, mackerel, seahorse.
