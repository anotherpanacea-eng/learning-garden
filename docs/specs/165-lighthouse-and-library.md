## Purpose

Ancient Egypt and Ancient Greece & Rome (29 pinned topics each) had no reference in `curriculum/topic-coverage.csv`. Since then packs took the river ([134](134-nile-year.md)), monuments and belief (156, [draft PR #142](https://github.com/anotherpanacea-eng/learning-garden/pull/142)), Roman roads ([135](135-roads-and-water.md)), Athens ([136](136-who-gets-a-voice.md)) and Greek and Roman myth (157, PR #142). Nobody teaches how those worlds **met**: Alexander, the Greek-speaking Ptolemies, Cleopatra VII and Rome in 30 BCE, or what Egypt passed on. Alexandria holds all of it, plus Eratosthenes measuring the Earth with shadows.

A child comes to understand that:

- Alexander's conquests put Greek rulers over Egypt, and Alexandria became a city where Greek, Egyptian and other peoples lived side by side;
- some things about the Pharos lighthouse are known, some are estimates, and some (its exact height) nobody knows;
- the Library collected books on purpose, by copying, and faded over centuries; the "one great fire" story is a myth of a slow decline;
- a degree is one of 360 equal turns, and Eratosthenes turned one shadow angle and one distance into the size of the whole Earth, with honest uncertainty about his unit;
- Egypt passed under Rome in 30 BCE, and Egyptian language and ideas carried on after the last pharaoh.

## The knowledge

Checked in the source audit.

**Alexander and the founding.** Alexander of Macedon conquered the Persian Empire, which had ruled Egypt on and off since 525 BCE. He arrived in late 332 BCE; the Persian governor handed Egypt over without a fight. He founded Alexandria, usually dated 331 BCE (some say 332), on the site of an Egyptian settlement, Rhakotis, and left within months. His army reached the Indus region (the Hydaspes, 326 BCE) and turned back. He died in Babylon in 323 BCE, aged 32; his generals divided the empire. The mixed Greek and local world he left is the Hellenistic Age.

**The Ptolemies.** His general Ptolemy took Egypt, made Alexandria his capital and took the title of king in 305/304 BCE. His family, Macedonian Greeks who spoke Greek at court, ruled until 30 BCE and appeared in temples as pharaohs (a Dendera relief shows Cleopatra VII and her son in pharaohs' crowns). Spec 134 owns the Rosetta Stone (196 BCE); this pack points back.

**The Pharos.** A tower on Pharos island at the harbor entrance, linked to Sostratus of Cnidus (architect in Pliny, dedicator in Strabo; child text: "linked") and finished about 280 BCE (sources give 284-246 BCE for the work). Lists of wonders from the 500s CE include it (earlier lists did not). A fire at the top burned at night; a mirror that burned enemy ships is a medieval tale that experts doubt. Height: **nobody knows exactly**; estimates run from at least 100 m to about 140 m. Earthquakes from 956 CE brought it down in the early 1300s (1303 or 1323); in 1480 Sultan Qaitbay built a fort on its base. In 1994 Jean-Yves Empereur's divers recorded hundreds of large blocks in the harbor and a colossal king statue thought to be Ptolemy II. The island's name gave French *phare* and Spanish and Italian *faro* (the Greek word's own origin is unknown).

**The Library and the Museum.** About 295 BCE Ptolemy I charged Demetrius of Phalerum with founding a library and the *Mouseion* (a shrine of the Muses where paid scholars worked); some think the Library was built under Ptolemy II. Books on ships in port were copied; Galen says owners got the copies and the Library kept the originals, listed "from the ships". Galen tells that Ptolemy III borrowed Athens's official copies of Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides, pledged 15 talents of silver, kept the originals and sent back copies. Callimachus's *Pinakes* listed Greek writers and works, an early catalog. Estimates of the collection run from **40,000 to 400,000 scrolls**. **The end was slow.** About 145 BCE Ptolemy VIII drove scholars out. In 48 BCE, in Caesar's war, fire spread from ships in the harbor and burned some books (how many is unclear; work went on). Roman rulers paid it less attention. The Serapeum, a temple holding a daughter collection, was destroyed in 391 CE. A story that Arab conquerors burned it in the 640s first appears over 500 years later (about 1203 and 1227); Britannica calls it a fabrication. No single fire ended it; neglect, lost money and many shocks did.

**Eratosthenes measures the Earth.** Eratosthenes of Cyrene (about 276 to 194 BCE) headed the Library from about 240 BCE. Greek scholars already held the Earth to be round; he asked how big. His method survives mainly through Cleomedes, writing later:
1. At Syene (modern Aswan), at noon on the longest day, an upright pointer cast no shadow: the Sun was overhead.
2. At Alexandria a pointer's shadow showed the Sun 1/50 of a circle from overhead: 7.2° (Greeks of his day did not use degrees).
3. He assumed the Sun's rays arrive parallel, that Syene lies due south of Alexandria, and that the two are 5,000 stadia apart.
4. If 7.2° is 1/50 of the circle, 5,000 stadia is 1/50 of the way around: 50 × 5,000 = **250,000 stadia** (Strabo gives 252,000).

**Honest uncertainty.** Nobody knows which stadion he used. At 157.5 m, 250,000 stadia is 39,375 km (1.6% under the modern 40,008 km pole-to-pole); at 185 m (Attic), 46,250 km (16% over). Child text: "between about 39,000 and 46,000 km". His inputs were rounded: Aswan is about 0.4° north of the tropic of his time (computed) and about 3° of longitude east of Alexandria; the real north-south gap is about 790 km; the latitude difference, 7.11°, is within 1.3% of 1/50. Some errors cancel. The method was sound; the answer was in the right range.

**Hypatia** (older depth only). Hypatia (about 355 to 415 CE) taught mathematics, astronomy and philosophy in Alexandria; her father Theon wrote on Ptolemy's astronomy; her commentaries on Diophantus and Apollonius are lost or survive in part. One Keeper's note. Her violent death is only in the grown-up panel, in one plain line.

**Cleopatra VII and the end.** Cleopatra VII ruled from 51 BCE, at first with her younger brother; she was the last Ptolemy with real power (her son and co-ruler Caesarion was killed weeks after her; child text leaves him out). Her first language was Greek; Plutarch says she spoke many languages and was the first of her family to learn Egyptian (scholars read his list cautiously). She allied with Roman leaders to keep Egypt independent. Octavian beat her fleet and her ally Mark Antony's at Actium in 31 BCE and took Alexandria in 30 BCE, the year she died. Egypt became Rome's, governed by a prefect the emperor chose; its grain fed Rome. A ruler's story, without romance framing or a description of her death.

**Before and after (Keeper's Desk).** Egypt was conquered by Assyria (Esarhaddon took Memphis from the Nubian pharaoh Taharqa, 671 BCE), Persia (525 BCE), Alexander (332 BCE) and Rome (30 BCE), and Kushite kings from Nubia had ruled as pharaohs before Assyria; Persian, Greek and Roman rulers took pharaohs' titles and images. The culture went on: **Coptic**, the last stage of Egyptian, is still used in church services. One of a pair of Luxor Temple obelisks has stood in **Paris**'s Place de la Concorde since 1836 (spec 46's Paris shelf); its twin is at Luxor. The 365-day calendar (spec 134) and Alexandrian geometry and astronomy passed to later scholars (Euclid taught in Alexandria about 300 BCE).

## The game or activity

**Readings** open at Short, Longer or Keeper's note, with read-aloud and labels. A **map card** (original schematic, "simplified picture, not to scale") shows Macedonia, Alexandria, Syene, Memphis, Athens, Babylon, the Indus and Rome, with Alexander's route as one arrow and no borders.

**Know or Guess Board** (`#archive/alexandria/pharos`). Ten cards about the Pharos and the Library; four columns: **We know**, **Experts think**, **Nobody knows**, **A story people told**. Cards can move back; after sorting each flips to show its source. Two cards test underclaiming. No timer.

**Shadow Bench** (`#archive/alexandria/shadows`), model `shadow-v1`, labeled **"A simplified picture: a perfectly round Earth, a Sun so far away its rays arrive parallel, and two towns on one north-south line. Sizes are not to scale."**

1. **Sweep a ray.** Drag a ray around a circle's center; a counter ticks one degree at a time as a wedge fills, with rim marks at 90°, 180°, 270°, 360°. **Longer arm** stretches the ray; the count holds.
2. **The two sticks.** A slice of Earth, sticks at Syene (no shadow) and Alexandria, parallel rays. The child turns a protractor arc at Alexandria to read 7.2°.
3. **Stack the wedges.** A 7.2° wedge is copied around the center until the circle closes; the count shows 50.
4. **Times the distance.** 50 × 5,000 = 250,000 stadia. A **Stadion** toggle shows the 157.5 m and 185 m results side by side, never one "right" number.
5. **Flat contrast.** A **Flat ground** switch lays both sticks on a flat strip under the same rays: both shadows match. "Different shadows mean the ground curves."
6. **Made-up towns** (Keeper's Desk). Two invented towns on a line; the child sets angle (3.6°, 6°, 9°, 14.4°) and distance and sees the circumference. Every result: *A made-up model. Real measurements have errors.*

Formula: circumference = (360 ÷ angle) × distance. No real Sun position is computed.

**Off-screen (adult-led, optional):** on a sunny day a grown-up stands a stick upright in open ground and marks the tip of its shadow every half hour; the shortest shadow is near local noon. Never look at the Sun. Nothing is recorded.
