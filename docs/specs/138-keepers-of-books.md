## Purpose

H2-VI asks for "optional source-reviewed historical packs with substantive knowledge, beyond Europe included". The Travelers' Archive holds Paris in 1910 ([spec 46](46-travelers-archive-first-visit.md)) and the Library holds ancient Egyptian writing tools ([spec 95](95-egypt-writing-book.md)). Marble's Medieval Times domain (25 topics) has no lesson placements in the game, and nothing in the game reaches the medieval world outside Europe. This pack fills that gap with three real places where people kept records and knowledge for centuries:

1. **Timbuktu and the Mali Empire** (West Africa, 1300s onward): Mansa Musa's pilgrimage, gold and trade, Timbuktu as a city of scholars, and the manuscripts families have kept for generations.
2. **Baghdad under the Abbasids and al-Khwarizmi** (800s): scholarship and translation, the book that gave us the word *algebra*, the name that gave us *algorithm*, and numerals that traveled from India through the Islamic world to Europe.
3. **Song China** (900s to 1100s): paper, woodblock printing, Bi Sheng's movable type and Su Song's water-driven clock tower.

A child comes to understand that the medieval world had many centers of learning; that ideas and objects traveled along trade routes; that printing and place-value numerals came to Europe late, from elsewhere; that what we know about these places comes from particular sources made by particular people; and, through the story, that keeping a record safe is real work that ordinary families and librarians have done.

Cross-strand links: the **numeral bench** gives M1 a historical contrast (Roman and Egyptian numerals against place value with zero), and the **procedure cards** give C1 its first look at an algorithm as a list of exact steps, named after a real person.

## The knowledge

All facts below are checked in the source audit; anything still marked `[check]` is listed there under Open checks.

### 1. Timbuktu and the Mali Empire

- The **Mali Empire** was a large West African empire. Its wealth came largely from gold mined in the regions of **Bambuk** and **Bure**, and from taxing trade, including salt coming south across the Sahara.
- **Mansa Musa** (*mansa* is a Mandinka title meaning king or ruler) governed Mali in the early 1300s. His reign is usually given as about 1312 to about 1337, but only 1324 is firmly dated, so child text says "the early 1300s". He made the pilgrimage to **Mecca** in **1324-1325**, crossing the Sahara with a very large company carrying gold. He stopped in **Cairo** in 1324.
- **al-ʿUmarī**, a writer in Cairo, recorded about twelve years later that Musa's company spent and gave away so much gold that its value in Cairo fell and stayed low: by his report, a mithqal of gold that had been worth at least 25 dirhams was worth 22 or less (Levtzion and Hopkins translation). The pack presents this as one writer's report, with its numbers, and does not convert Musa's wealth to modern money or call him "the richest person ever".
- The **Catalan Atlas**, a map made in Majorca (conventionally dated 1375; possibly a few years later), shows a West African ruler identified with Mansa Musa holding a gold object. It is held by the Bibliothèque nationale de France. It shows that Mali's gold was famous far away; it is not a portrait from life.
- **Timbuktu**, on the edge of the Sahara near the Niger River, became a trading city and a center of Islamic scholarship. Its great mosques, **Djinguereber**, **Sankore** and **Sidi Yahia**, are linked with that learning; tradition connects Djinguereber with Mansa Musa after his pilgrimage. The mosques were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage site in 1988. The pack avoids the popular phrase "University of Sankore" as if it were a modern campus: scholars taught students in mosques and homes.
- **The manuscripts.** Families in Timbuktu and the region have kept private libraries of handwritten books for generations. Subjects include law, religion, history, grammar, astronomy, medicine and mathematics, along with letters about trade and legal matters. Most are in Arabic; some write local languages such as Songhay and Fulfulde in Arabic script (called *Ajami*). Many were kept in trunks and chests. Rain, damp, termites, mice and dust have damaged some.
- **Keeping them safe, 2012-2013.** When armed groups took control of Timbuktu in 2012, librarians and families, led by the library keeper **Abdel Kader Haidara**, quietly carried hundreds of thousands of manuscripts (about 350,000 is the most often given figure) south to **Bamako**, the capital, about 700 km away in a straight line. Some manuscripts left behind at the Ahmed Baba Institute were burned. Many have since been photographed or scanned, and in August 2025 Mali's government began flying manuscripts back to Timbuktu (AP reported about 28,000 in that operation, mainly the Ahmed Baba Institute's; the 350,000 figure counts the family libraries too). The pack tells this in two sentences at the Seedling length and adds a little more at the Keeper's note; it names no casualties, shows no fighting, and centers the keepers.
- **Ibn Baṭṭūṭa**, a traveler from Tangier in Morocco, visited Mali in about **1352-1353**, when Mansa Sulayman (Musa's brother) ruled, and passed through Timbuktu. His account was written down later in Fez by the scholar **Ibn Juzayy**, from Ibn Battuta's telling, in about 1355-1356. It is the most detailed eyewitness account of Mali's court, and it is one traveler's view, remembered and written down years later.

### 2. Baghdad under the Abbasids, and al-Khwarizmi

- In the 800s, the Abbasid caliphs ruled from **Baghdad**. Rulers and wealthy families paid scholars to translate books from Greek, Persian, Syriac and Sanskrit into Arabic, and scholars wrote new works on mathematics, astronomy and medicine.
- Books often name the **House of Wisdom** (*Bayt al-Ḥikma*) as the center of this work, linked with the caliph al-Maʾmūn. **Historians still discuss what it was**: a palace library, a place where scholars worked, or something larger. We know much more about the scholars and their books than about the building. The pack says this plainly and never describes rooms, staff numbers or a "first university".
- **Muḥammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī** (about 780 to about 850) worked in Baghdad under al-Maʾmūn. His book on calculation by *al-jabr* and *al-muqābala* (about 820s to 830, dates differ) gave us the word **algebra**. *Al-jabr* is usually translated "restoration" or "completion" and *al-muqabala* "balancing" (the book's title is often given as *The Compendious Book on Calculation by Completion and Balancing*): steps done to both sides of a problem to keep it level.
- He also wrote about calculating with the **Indian numerals**. That book's Arabic original is lost, but a Latin translation in the 1100s began with his name as *Algoritmi*. From that Latin name came the words *algorism* and later **algorithm**: a list of exact steps for doing something.
- **Numerals from India.** Scholars in India wrote numbers with nine digits, a sign for zero, and place value. The oldest dated inscription that everyone agrees uses zero this way is from 876 (Gwalior, India). Brahmagupta's book of 628 treats zero as a number. These numerals passed into the Islamic world, where al-Khwarizmi wrote about them.
- **Into Europe.** **Leonardo of Pisa**, later called Fibonacci, learned the "method of the Indians" as a young man in **Bugia** (Béjaïa, in present-day Algeria), where his father worked for Pisa's merchants. His book **Liber Abaci** (1202) taught Hindu-Arabic numerals and place value to European merchants. Europe took several more centuries to adopt them widely.

### 3. Song China

- **Paper.** Chinese histories say the official **Cai Lun** presented a paper-making method to the emperor in 105 CE. Archaeologists have found older paper from the Western Han period, 2nd century BCE, so Cai Lun improved or standardized paper rather than inventing it from nothing. Paper-making later spread west: to Samarkand, to Baghdad by the 790s, across North Africa to Spain by the 1100s, and on into Europe (the story that it began in Samarkand with prisoners taken in 751 is told by a later writer and is disputed).
- **Woodblock printing** came first: a whole page carved into one block of wood. The **Diamond Sutra** printed in 868 (British Library) is the earliest complete printed book that carries a date.
- **Movable type.** In the 1040s, a craftsman named **Bi Sheng** made separate characters from clay, hardened them by fire, and set them in an iron frame to print a page, then reused them. We know about Bi Sheng almost entirely from **Shen Kuo**'s *Dream Pool Essays* (completed about 1088). Shen Kuo noted that the method was slow for a few copies and very fast for hundreds or thousands. This observation is the heart of the copying bench.
- **Su Song's clock tower.** In **Kaifeng**, the Song capital, the official **Su Song** led the building of an astronomical clock tower about 12 m tall, finished around 1092. A water wheel drove it, with an **escapement** that let the wheel turn one step at a time, so it kept steady time. It turned a bronze armillary sphere and a star globe and showed the hours with small figures. It used a chain drive. Su Song's book about it was written in 1092 and first printed in 1094; its drawings survive in later editions. The escapement idea itself was older (Yi Xing and Liang Lingzan, 725). The tower was taken apart after the Jurchen took Kaifeng in 1127. Link: [spec 123 Clockwork Mill](123-clockwork-mill.md) for gears and escapements.

### 4. The comparison card: later printing elsewhere

- **Korea:** the *Jikji*, printed with movable metal type at Heungdeok Temple in Cheongju in **1377**, is the oldest surviving book printed this way (one volume, held in the Bibliothèque nationale de France; UNESCO Memory of the World, 2001).
- **Europe:** **Johannes Gutenberg** in Mainz built a printing press with metal movable type around **1450**; his Bible was finished about **1455**. His system combined metal type cast in a hand mold, an oil-based ink that stuck to metal, and a screw press adapted from wine and olive-oil presses, and printing spread fast across Europe. It was a later, separate development, and an important one for Europe; printing had begun in China centuries earlier.

### 5. Paper care (science)

- Paper is made of plant fibers. Water swells the fibers and weakens the sheet. Damp paper kept warm and still can grow **mold** within about two days. Conservators **air-dry** wet books with moving air and absorbent paper, and freeze them if they cannot dry them in time. They avoid direct heat, which can speed mold in wet paper and can warp or scorch it.
- Things that harm paper over time: **damp**, **insects** such as termites, **mice**, **fire**, **dust**, and light, especially sunlight, which fades ink and yellows and weakens paper. Things that help: dry, steady air; closed boxes or chests; spreading collections across several places; and making copies (by hand, by print, or by photograph and scan).
- The story's windowsill is warm, not hot, and air moves past it. That is the gentle science tie: warm, moving, dry air dries paper; an oven is far too hot.

## The game or activity

**Reading room.** Three traveling books. Each opens at the reader's chosen length (Short, Longer, Keeper's note), with read-aloud. Every page carries its label (A true thing from history). A **map card** (an original modern schematic, labeled "simplified picture, not to scale") shows Timbuktu, Cairo, Mecca, Baghdad, Kaifeng, Bugia and Mainz, with routes drawn as simple arrows. Each reading points to its practice set: Timbuktu to Set C, Baghdad to Set A, Song China to Set B.

**Numeral bench** (`#archive/keepers/numerals`). Three trays side by side: **Roman**, **Egyptian** and **Place value**. The child builds one number in all three with tap-to-add tiles (Roman: I, V, X, L, C; Egyptian: the stroke, the hobble (often called a heel bone) and the coil of rope for 1, 10 and 100, reusing the tiles of [spec 134](134-nile-year.md)'s number tray, whose audit checks the signs; Place value: digit wheels in hundreds, tens and ones). Then **Add them**: two numbers appear in each tray; the child combines symbols, swaps ten of a kind for one of the next (Egyptian, Roman with V, L handled as groups), or adds columns with a carry. The bench counts symbols written in each tray and shows it ("Roman: 5 symbols. Place value: 2 digits."). A **zero card** asks the child to write 105 and 15 in each tray, showing that only the place-value tray needs a sign for "nothing here". Builds up to 199 at Seedling, years up to 1500 in Roman at Keeper's Desk, whose expanded palette and first learn card add D = 500, M = 1000, CD = 400 and CM = 900 before those tasks. No timer.

**Procedure cards** (`#archive/keepers/steps`). A deck of numbered step cards. The child **traces** a procedure: taps "next step" and fills one blank per step. Two procedures: **halve and double** (multiplication by halving one number and doubling the other, adding rows where the halved number is odd) and **balance both sides** (find the missing number by doing the same thing to both sides of a balance). A third activity, **Fix the steps**, shows four step cards out of order or with a vague step; the child orders them or chooses the clear version. The cards say the name "algorithm" comes from al-Khwarizmi; they do not claim he invented halve-and-double. The card names no inventor and notes only that scribes in ancient Egypt multiplied by repeated doubling (Rhind papyrus), linking to spec 134.

**Copying bench** (`#archive/keepers/copying`). Model `copy-v1`, labeled **"made-up numbers to compare two ways of making books"**:

- A copyist makes one whole copy of the book in **30 days**.
- Printing first needs the blocks carved: **60 days**. Then a printer makes **20 copies a day** (part days count as a whole day).
- Days to copy *n* books by hand: 30 × *n*. Days to print *n* books: 60 + (*n* ÷ 20, rounded up).
- Assumptions shown on a Details card: one worker each way; the same book; no breaks or mistakes; the numbers are invented for the comparison and are not historical rates.

Slider for copies (1 to 200). Two bars grow. Results: 1 copy, 30 and 61 days; 2 copies, 60 and 61; 3 copies, 90 and 61 (printing is faster from 3 copies); 10 copies, 300 and 61; 100 copies, 3,000 and 65. Every result shows: *This is a made-up model with simple numbers. Real copyists and printers worked at many different speeds.* The point the bench makes is Shen Kuo's own observation, which is sourced.

**Care sort** (`#archive/keepers/care`). Twelve cards (damp cellar, closed chest, termites, a candle left by the pages, dusty shelf, sunny window, a copy kept in another house, a scan, a fan and paper towels, an oven, many families each keeping some books, a dry steady room). Two bins, **harms paper** and **helps keep paper**, and a third, **it depends**, for the sunny window and the chest (a chest helps against dust but can trap damp). After sorting, one "say why" card for a grown-up to read.

**Off-screen (adult-led, optional):** dry two damp sheets of scrap paper, one flat on a rack near moving air, one in a pile, and compare after an hour. No heat sources; a grown-up handles any water; nothing moldy is handled by a child.
