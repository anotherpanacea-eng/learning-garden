## The knowledge

Sourced in the [audit](../source-audits/262-which-hat.md); terms follow the DfE grammar glossary, simplified, with US words in child text.

**Adjectives and adverbs.** An adjective modifies a noun, before it or after *be* as its complement; an adverb modifies a verb, an adjective, another adverb or a whole clause (DfE glossary, which warns that "describing word" does not separate the classes). After linking verbs (*be, seem, look, feel, taste*) an adjective describes the subject: *the cake tastes sweet*. *Looked careful* (seemed) and *looked carefully* (examined) differ in meaning. Many adverbs are adjective + *-ly*, but *friendly, lovely, lonely* are adjectives, and *fast, hard, soon, well* are adverbs without *-ly*. Flat adverbs such as *go slow*, *come quick* and *go slower* have long standard use (Merriam-Webster, *slow*); school writing practices the *-ly* form, and no item offers a standard flat adverb as a wrong choice. CCSS L.2.1e and L.3.1a.

**Comparatives and superlatives.** A comparative compares two (*bigger, more colorful*), often with *than*; a superlative picks one from three or more (*the biggest, the most colorful*) (CCSS L.3.1g). One-syllable adjectives take *-er/-est*; longer ones take *more/most*; two-syllable adjectives vary (*quieter* or *more quiet*; *-y* words take *-ier*). Spelling: in one-syllable words ending in a single consonant letter after a single vowel letter, double the consonant (*big, bigger*; not *cool* or *clean*), except *w, x, y* (*newer, grayer*); final *e* takes *-r* (*wider*); consonant + *y* becomes *i* (*tinier*). Irregular: *good, better, best; bad, worse, worst*. Adverbs: *fast, faster*; most *-ly* adverbs take *more* (*more carefully*). "The best of the two" occurs in speech; two for *-er* is the school convention.

**Abstract nouns.** Abstract nouns name ideas, qualities, feelings and states (*patience, kindness, bravery, childhood*) rather than things perceived by the senses; they still behave as nouns, taking determiners and serving as subject or object (CCSS L.3.1c). Many sit in word families with an adjective and adverb (*patient, patiently, patience*); the DfE glossary defines a word family by shared form, grammar and meaning. The senses test is a rule of thumb; some nouns are borderline.

**Expanded noun phrases.** A noun phrase has a noun as its head; everything else modifies it (DfE glossary). A determiner (article, demonstrative, possessive such as *my* or *Dot's*, quantifier) comes before any modifiers. Modifiers include adjectives, nouns used as modifiers next to the head (*party hat, stone bridge*) and, after the head, preposition phrases (*with a gold ribbon*): DfE Year 4's *the strict maths teacher with curly hair*.

**Prepositional phrases.** A preposition with its noun phrase (*under* + *the bench*) (DfE "preposition phrase"; CCSS L.4.1e). Modifying a noun it tells which one (*the scarf under the bench*); modifying a verb or clause it is an adverbial telling where, when or how (*Puff slept under the bench*). An adverbial can also be an adverb or a subordinate clause (DfE glossary; Year 4 term).

**Adjective order.** Cumulative adjectives before a noun follow a strong conventional order (CCSS L.4.1d: *a small red bag*, not *a red small bag*): opinion before fact, then roughly size, age and shape, color, origin, material, purpose, with describing nouns nearest the head. Published lists disagree in the middle (Cambridge's puts shape before age, popular lists the reverse), so this pack uses only size, age and color, where they agree, plus opinion first. Experiments find that less subjective adjectives sit nearer the noun (Scontras, Degen and Goodman 2017). Coordinate adjectives of one kind can swap and take a comma or *and*; other orders remain understandable and are used for emphasis. Taught as "the usual order", never as a law.

**Pronouns for clarity.** A pronoun stands for a noun already named; it repeats less but must point to one antecedent (DfE Year 4: choosing pronoun or noun for cohesion and to avoid repetition; CCSS L.6.1d on vague pronouns, L.3.1f agreement). Fixes: name the noun, or reorder so only one candidate is near. Reflexive pronouns point back to the subject (*Pip helped himself*; *Pip helped him* is someone else), and also add emphasis (CCSS L.2.1c, L.6.1b). The DfE treats *my, your, her* before a noun as possessive determiners and *mine, yours, hers* as possessive pronouns; many US texts call the first set possessive pronouns or adjectives. For singular *they*, Merriam-Webster records *themself* (sense added 2019); child text never asks for the librarian's reflexive. *Myself* in subject position (*Dot and myself went*) is common in speech; the pack teaches the written convention in the grown-up panel without scolding the spoken form.

**Year 3 and 4 terms.** DfE English Appendix 2: Year 3 preposition, conjunction, word family, prefix, clause, subordinate clause, direct speech, consonant letter, vowel letter, inverted commas; Year 4 determiner, pronoun, possessive pronoun, adverbial. Myhill and colleagues' trial supports teaching such terms in the context of writing choices, which is how every term here arrives.

## The game or activity

Every screen: *These are made-up sentences. Real speakers and writers sometimes do it differently.* No score, timer, lock or mastery label. Drags have tap equivalents. Supplied tiles and sentences only: no free-text entry, no grading of the child's own writing.

- **(a) Which Hat? (Seedling, Sapling, Keeper's Desk).** Six hats on the café pegs, a made-up model varying in size, age, color and ribbon. An owner points at a peg; the child builds a description from tiles. A **fits counter** says how many hats the description fits after each tile (4, then 2, then 1 for Dot's). Seedling drops tiles into labeled size, age and color slots; Sapling places them freely and adds ribbon phrases. **Read it back** names the usual order or says the other order is understood but unusual. *Lovely* fits every hat and is flagged as an opinion. At Keeper's Desk, **The finished notice** labels a notice with the Year 3 and 4 terms.
- **(b) Can It Go on the Shelf? (Seedling).** Eight cards into *could go on the shelf* or *nobody can hand it back*; then tap the word that names the quality in four word families.
- **(c) How Did It Go? (Seedling).** Six gaps in Ember's thank-you notes; **two pictures** for *looked careful* and *looked carefully*.
- **(d) Bigger and Biggest (Seedling, Sapling).** Tap two or three-plus market goods and pick the sign; a **spelling bench** adds *-er* to *big, hot, wide, tiny, new, good*; two adverb signs.
- **(e) Where Exactly? (Sapling).** Build *under the bench* from preposition and noun phrase tiles; **Which job?** sorts four sentences into *which one* or *where it happened*.
- **(f) Whose Is It? (Sapling, Keeper's Desk).** **Who is she?** taps unclear pronouns and picks the clearer note; **Mine, yours, hers** finishes return notes; **Himself or him?** matches two pictures.
