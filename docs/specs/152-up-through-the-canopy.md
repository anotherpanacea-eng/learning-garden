The six-act strand plan says rainforest knowledge "needs substantive source-reviewed content, not a reskinned local garden," and S4-II/III ask for climate and location, adaptations, water and nutrients, informed cultural accounts and human relationships. This pack is that content.

A child comes to understand what tropical and temperate rainforests are and where they grow (and that Europe has only the temperate kind); that light separates four layers; that plant features solve specific problems; that trees put water back in the air and help make the Amazon's rain; that the richest forest grows on thin soil; that real Indigenous peoples live in rainforests today and hold tested knowledge of them; that familiar foods began in tropical forests, not always where they are grown now; and, at the oldest depth, who needs what when forests are cleared.

## The knowledge

Checked against the sources in the audit (search excerpts; direct page fetches were blocked). A second fact-check pass on 2026-10-25 settled every open item; none remain. The companion holds the child wording; this is the adult summary.

**What and where.** Tropical rainforests are warm all year (usually above 20 °C) and wet (often more than 2,000 mm of rain a year, spread fairly evenly). They form a belt near the Equator: the Amazon (nine countries), the Congo Basin and Southeast Asia, with smaller ones in Central America, Madagascar and northern Australia. Estimates of their share of land run from about 6% to 8%, holding roughly half of known species; the pack teaches both as ranges. **Temperate rainforests** grow on cool, very wet coasts: the Pacific Northwest (the Hoh, about 3,300 mm a year at the ranger station; other sources give more), southern Chile, New Zealand, Tasmania, Norway and the fragmented Atlantic ("Celtic") rainforests of western Britain and Ireland, rich in mosses, liverworts and lichens. Marble's "every continent except Antarctica and Europe" holds for tropical rainforest only, and the pack says so.

**Layers.** Floor, understory, canopy, emergent (often about 40 to 60 m; the kapok reaches about 60 m). The canopy catches most light; only about 1 to 5% reaches the floor (educational sources give 1 to 2%; one measured Amazon site about 5%). Most animals and fruit are in the canopy.

**Plant adaptations.** Drip tips shed water so fungi, algae and bacteria do not grow on wet leaves. Buttress roots brace tall trees in shallow soil, spreading wide, not deep. Epiphytes (orchids, bromeliads) grow on other plants without feeding on them. Lianas root in the ground and climb to the canopy. Adaptations are inherited, not chosen.

**Water cycle.** Roots take up water, leaves release vapor (transpiration), it rises, cools and condenses, rain falls; ocean moisture also arrives. About one-third of Amazon rainfall originates within the basin (Staal and colleagues, 2018); a 2025 Tapajós study found up to about 70% in the dry season (child text: "up to about 7 in 10"). Fewer trees can mean less recycled rain.

**Soil and nutrients.** Typical rainforest soils (oxisols) are thin and nutrient-poor. Most nutrients sit in living biomass; warm, wet conditions make decomposition fast, and a surface root mat with mycorrhizal fungi takes nutrients back before heavy rain leaches them. Cleared land often farms well briefly, then declines. One food-arrow card reuses the garden's convention from specs 66 and 88 (the arrow points from food to eater).

**Indigenous peoples and knowledge** (present tense; no portraits or costume images by default). **Yanomami:** about 35,000 people (estimates 32,000 to 38,000) in the Amazon of Brazil and Venezuela; the ethnonym was formed by anthropologists from the Yanomami word *yanõmami* (in *yanõmami thëpë*, "human beings"), and Yanomami organizations such as the Hutukara Associação Yanomami (founded 2004, led by Davi Kopenawa) use it today; in many villages one large round communal house (*yano* or *shabono*); gardens of plantains, bananas and peach palm with hunting, fishing and gathering; Milliken and Albert (1996), working with one community, recorded at least 113 medicinal plant and fungus species. **Baka** (their own name; the outsider label "Pygmy" is avoided as pejorative): Congo Basin forest in southeastern Cameroon, northern Gabon, the northern Republic of the Congo and the southwestern Central African Republic; gather wild yams, honey, nuts and mushrooms, fish and snare; the Baka language has a large vocabulary for edible and medicinal plants and honey collecting; one survey recorded 88 wild food plants; many families now also farm and live by roads, partly because colonial-era policy moved them, so the card shows a changing present in which forest gathering continues. The cards name agency (the people's own organizations, knowledge and choices) and avoid both a timeless "guardians of nature" picture and a victims-only picture. **Shifting cultivation:** small plots farmed a few years, then rested, against large permanent clearing. **Land evidence:** lower deforestation in Indigenous territories (about 66% lower in one 1982 to 2016 study), stronger where recognized; clearing inside territories rose 129% from 2013 to 2021, mostly from illegal mining, logging and ranching by outsiders. **Curare** (Keeper's Desk): a dart preparation of Amazonian hunters (Achuar and Canelos Quichua named in one source); in 1942 Griffith and Johnson first used a curare preparation to relax muscles in surgery.

**Foods.** *Cacao:* pods grow on the trunk, about 40 beans each (varies); wild diversity centers on the upper Amazon; in 2018 Côte d'Ivoire grew 37% and Ghana 18%. *Banana:* a giant herb with a pseudostem of leaf sheaths, probably first domesticated in New Guinea. *Vanilla:* an orchid vine from Mexico and Central America, grown for centuries by the Totonac, whose Papantla communities still grow it; elsewhere it set few pods without its native pollinators (which insects these are is debated, so the card does not say "bees") until Edmond Albius, an enslaved boy of about 12 on Réunion, devised hand pollination in 1841; Madagascar is the largest producer. *Brazil nut:* an emergent tree; pods of about 10 to 25 seeds; pollinated mainly by large orchid bees; agoutis bury seeds; most harvest is wild. Coffee is left out (not checked).

**Futures (Keeper's Desk).** In Brazil, cattle pasture covers more than three-quarters of deforested land; the 2006 soy moratorium sharply cut soy's role. In Indonesia and Malaysia, logging followed by palm oil and pulpwood plantations drives most clearing. Four needs cards (a ranching family, an Indigenous community, a government, distant buyers) speak for no one, and the page ends on evidence, not doom.

## The game or activity

- **Map table:** schematic world map, Equator, green belt, three tappable blocks, dots for smaller rainforests, blue temperate pins at Sapling. Line: *A simplified map. Not to scale.*
- **The climb:** a vertical scene; **Up** and **Down** buttons (keyboard and switch friendly) move one layer at a time; a light meter beside it shows a simplified share of sunlight; four cards to place on layers (free placing, not checked). Line under the meter: *A simplified picture.*
- **Leaf bench:** two leaves under a rain spout. Predict which sheds water faster, then tap **Rain**; the drip-tip leaf drains and the round leaf pools. Model: authored animation, no timing claims. Line: *A made-up model.* Then four structure → problem rows revealed one per tap.
- **Loop bench:** five arrow cards to place in a circle; a **Trees** dial (many / half / few) changes the cloud and a gauge. Model `canopy-loop-v1`: gauge fill is authored in three steps (full, about two-thirds, about half of the "many" level), made up to show direction only, no units. Line: *A made-up model. Real rain depends on winds, the ocean and much more.*
- **Nutrient bank:** ten tokens per side. Rainforest 8 living / 2 soil; Willowbank's wood 4 / 6. **Clear the trees** removes the living tokens and shows what stays. Proportions are invented to show the pattern; line on screen. A **cleared plot** strip shows made-up harvests 10, 6, 3 baskets.
- **People cards** with a map pin and a forest or plant photo (no portraits by default); the shifting cultivation cycle as four tappable steps. **Café table:** tap a food on Ember's table for its plant and map pins. **Four needs cards:** read-only, then one say-why prompt.

No timers, scores, medals or locks. Help use, familiarity (a page opened before) and independent attempts are recorded separately.
