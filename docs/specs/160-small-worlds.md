## Purpose

**Organisms & Life Processes** has 63 topics on the pinned Marble graph and one declared reference (What Plants Need to Grow); none is about cells. The whole cell strand (using a microscope, cells as the unit of life, cell parts, plant against animal cells, single-celled organisms) has no reference, and The Human Body's **Cells, Tissues & Organs** is met only by spec 148's short Keeper's zoom ladder. Strand S2 needs this foundation before photosynthesis and body systems can be explained below the level of organs: spec 149 lists **Plant Cells vs Animal Cells** as a documented gap.

A child comes to understand that a microscope reveals what was always there; that "40 times" means forty times longer, and two lenses multiply; that every living thing is made of cells; that plant cells share an animal cell's parts and add a wall, a big vacuole and often chloroplasts; that cells build tissues, organs and systems; and that one cell can be a whole life (pond swimmers, the baker's yeast, helpful bacteria). At older depth: Hooke and Leeuwenhoek as the first looks, and handwashing as hygiene science.

## The knowledge

Plain adult statements, each sourced in the [audit](../source-audits/160-small-worlds.md).

**Using a light microscope.** Light from a mirror or lamp passes up through a thin specimen on a glass slide, then through an objective lens and an eyepiece. Total magnification is eyepiece × objective (10 × 4 = 40; 10 × 10 = 100; 10 × 40 = 400). Procedure: a thin specimen in a drop of water (a wet mount); lower the cover glass at an angle to push air out; start on the lowest power; coarse focus while watching from the side so the objective does not hit the slide; fine focus; then center and increase power. Air bubbles (round, dark-edged, empty) are the classic artifact read as cells. Magnification = image size ÷ actual size. A light microscope cannot resolve detail finer than about 0.2 µm (0.0002 mm), at roughly 1,500× at best, so higher magnification beyond that adds blur, not detail.

**Sizes used (typical, "about").** Paramecium about 0.2-0.3 mm long for the common *P. caudatum* (the genus spans about 0.05-0.32 mm); baker's yeast cells about 5-10 µm across; *E. coli*, a typical bacterium, about 1-2 µm long; a human red blood cell about 7 µm (spec only). Onion epidermal cell sizes in teaching sources conflict, so the bench uses invented sizes for onion (B5 is labeled made-up).

**Cells.** All living things are made of one or more cells (cell theory: Schleiden for plants, 1838; Schwann for animals, 1839; Virchow added in 1855 that cells come from cells). The typical plant and animal cells pictured here have a membrane (a boundary that controls what enters and leaves), cytoplasm and a nucleus (holding the instructions, DNA, that control the cell). Plant cells add a cellulose cell wall outside the membrane, a large permanent vacuole of cell sap that helps keep the plant firm, and, in many cells, chloroplasts for photosynthesis. Animal cells have no wall and no chloroplasts; among the six parts taught, none is animal-only (the child line says "of these six parts"; centrioles come later). Onion bulb epidermis has no chloroplasts (the inner scale leaves get no light); pondweed leaf cells show them. Mitochondria and ribosomes are named only in the grown-up panel as later work.

**Levels.** Similar cells working together form a tissue; tissues form an organ (a leaf, a root, the heart, the stomach); organs working together form a system (the shoot and root systems of a plant; the circulatory system); systems make the organism. "Tissue" here never means a paper tissue.

**Single-celled organisms.** Some organisms are one cell that does every life process. A paramecium moves with beating cilia; a euglena has chloroplasts, a whip-like flagellum and a light-sensitive eyespot; an amoeba moves and engulfs food by pushing out pseudopods. Baker's yeast (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*) is a single-celled fungus that reproduces by budding; its wall is mostly glucans with a little chitin, not cellulose. Bacteria are single cells with no nucleus.

**Helpful bacteria and germs (older depth).** Many microbes are harmless or helpful: soil bacteria help break down dead matter; *Rhizobium* bacteria in root nodules of beans and peas turn nitrogen from the air into a form the plant can use; *Lactobacillus* and related bacteria ferment milk into yogurt. A few microbes cause illness. CDC guidance: wet, lather, scrub about 20 seconds, rinse under clean running water, dry with a clean towel or air; lather and scrubbing lift dirt, grease and microbes from the skin and rinsing carries them away; plain soap is enough outside healthcare. No other health advice, no illness names, no vaccines or immune system.

**Robert Hooke (Keeper's Desk).** Hooke (1635-1703), English, published *Micrographia* in London in 1665 (its imprimatur is dated 23 November 1664). In Observation XVIII he cut "an exceeding thin piece" of cork, lit it from above on a black plate, and saw it "all perforated and porous, much like a Honey-comb", calling the pores "cells" and "little Boxes"; he counted "about threescore" cells in an eighteenth of an inch, hence "neer eleven hundred" per inch and over a million per square inch. Cork cells are dead at maturity: he saw empty walls. He used a compound microscope (two lenses) made in London; a 2025 study argues some fine plates relied on small single-lens microscopes too. No magnification figure is taught for his instrument.

**Antonie van Leeuwenhoek (Keeper's Desk).** Leeuwenhoek (1632-1723), a Delft draper, ground small single lenses. He probably first saw protozoa in 1674 and in mid-September 1675 saw tiny swimmers in rainwater (his Dutch *dierkens*, "little animals", which Oldenburg's English rendered "animalcules"); his letter of 9 October 1676 to the Royal Society was printed in *Philosophical Transactions* in 1677. He later saw bacteria. He made hundreds of microscopes; about eleven survive. The strongest surviving one, at Utrecht's University Museum, magnifies about 270× (measurements range 266-275×); the child text says "about 270 times".

## The game or activity

Every screen: *These are simplified pictures. Real cells vary in shape and size.* No score, timer, lock or mastery label. Every drag has a tap-to-place equivalent.

- **(a) Brass Microscope Bench.** Six labels onto a drawn microscope. **Set it up:** order six cards, then perform them on the bench (slice, water, slanted cover glass, lens turret, big knob with a side-view inset, small knob). A focus slider shows blur clearing coarse then fine. **Slide tray:** onion skin, pondweed leaf, a prepared animal-cell slide (a generic supplier slide, never anyone's own cells), thin cork, baker's yeast, a wet-corner drop; each slide has authored pictures at 40× and 400× (the drop is best at 100×). **Cell or bubble?** One onion slide holds a bubble; tapping it gets the artifact line.
- **(b) Times Bigger Strip.** Pick 40× or 400×; a ruler shows the real object and its picture. **Model:** picture length = real length × magnification, with the three objects at the audited typical sizes. **Stated simplification:** real optics blur at high power; the Keeper's note gives the resolution limit.
- **(c) Two Cells Side by Side.** Six part cards sorted into *Both* or *Plant only* (two columns, always side by side). **What if it were missing?** removes a part and shows one line. Wrong sorts get tagged lines and stay movable.
- **(d) Zoom Ladder.** Five tiles (leaf cell to whole plant), ordered either way; a revisit link to spec 148's heart ladder; at Sapling, place four new examples on the right step.
- **(e) Pond Drop.** Three swimmers drawn as "like a paramecium / euglena / amoeba"; match five callouts to five life jobs. A line contrasts one cell doing every job with many specialized cells sharing them.
- **(f) Keeper's Desk: First Looks.** Hooke's cork plate (public-domain *Micrographia* image), Leeuwenhoek's letter card, an evidence / interpretation / unknown sort for Hooke, the cell-theory card and three Long Line cards.
