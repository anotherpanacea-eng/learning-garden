## Purpose

The game teaches evidence and disagreement (H1) through Paris 1910, the Egyptian writing board and Follow the Source Trail. It has no pack on how people in the past made decisions together, and the Ancient Greece and Rome domain (H2, 29 Marble topics) has no coverage at all in the [strand map](../six-act-strands.md). Act IV of spec 120 ends in a town meeting, so the question "who gets a voice?" arrives in the story on its own.

This pack opens one archive drawer on one real meeting place: the assembly of Athens on the Pnyx hill. A child comes to understand:

- In Athens, from about 508/507 BCE, adult male citizens met in an assembly, spoke, and decided by a show of hands. Many officials, including a council of 500, were chosen by lot.
- Most people living in Athens could not take part: women, enslaved people and foreign residents (metics). How many people that was is an estimate, and historians' estimates differ.
- What survives (sherds with names scratched on them, pieces of an allotment machine, inscriptions, later writers) is evidence. What we say it means is interpretation. Some disagreements, like the 6,000-vote rule for ostracism, come from two ancient writers saying different things.
- The rule for who counts can change the result of the same vote.
- Myths about gods and Troy are stories people told. They are a different kind of thing from the sherds in the drawer.

Athens is presented as neither a model to copy nor a villain. It tried something new and left most people out. Both are true, and the pack says so.

## The knowledge

All of this is checked in the source audit. Items marked `[check]` there are not yet verified and are flagged in the text.

**Beginnings.** Athens' government was reorganized in about 508/507 BCE under Cleisthenes. He divided the citizens into 10 new tribes, each drawing on different parts of Attica (the land around Athens). The word democracy is Greek: *demos*, the people, and *kratos*, power or rule; the word was coined in the mid-400s BCE. Athens is often called the birthplace of democracy. The pack says "one of the earliest and best-recorded" and leaves the "first ever" claim as a question for older readers.

**The assembly (ekklesia).** Adult male citizens could attend, speak and vote. It met on the Pnyx, a hill in Athens, about 40 times a year in the 300s BCE (four regular meetings in each tenth of the year). Decisions were usually made by show of hands, a simple majority deciding. Attendance was far smaller than the number of citizens: by one modern estimate the first meeting place held about 6,000 (later rebuilds held more), and some decisions needed 6,000 present. This is direct democracy: the citizens present decided themselves.

**The council of 500 (boule).** 50 men from each of the 10 tribes, at least 30 years old, chosen by lot for a year. Each tribe's 50 took a turn as the standing committee for a tenth of the year and set the assembly's agenda. Lot is used here for the classical period; whether lot was used for the council from 508 itself is a question for the subject reviewer.

**Jury courts (dikasteria).** Each year about 6,000 citizen men aged 30 or over were chosen by lot as a jury pool. Juries were large, usually an odd number: 201 or 401 for private cases, and 501, 1,001 or 1,501 in public ones.

**Lot and election.** Choosing by lot gives every eligible man a chance and makes it hard to bribe or plan who serves. Athens still elected some officials: its 10 generals were chosen by vote, because military skill mattered. Modern countries mostly elect representatives: that is representative democracy.

**The kleroterion.** An allotment machine: a stone slab with rows of slots, one column for each tribe. Each juror's bronze name ticket (pinakion) went into a slot in his tribe's column. Black and white balls (some translations say cubes) dropped from a tube beside the slab in random order; a row lined up with a white one served, a row with a black one did not. Because every row held one man from each tribe, the tribes always got equal numbers. The machine is described in the *Constitution of the Athenians* (chapters 63 to 66), written in the 320s BCE. Fragments survive from the Agora excavations; the marble kleroterion in the Agora Museum (inventory I 3967) is dated by the Greek Ministry of Culture to the 200s BCE, so allotment machines stayed in use after Aristotle's time. The pack's model uses the white-serves rule and labels itself simplified.

**Ostracism.** The assembly first voted on whether to hold an ostracism. If it did, citizens scratched a name on a broken piece of pottery (an ostrakon; plural ostraka). The man named most had to leave Attica within 10 days for 10 years. He kept his property. The *Constitution of the Athenians* says Cleisthenes introduced it; the first recorded use was in 488/487 BCE. It fell out of use after probably 417 BCE.

**The 6,000 question.** Two ancient writers disagree. Plutarch says an ostracism counted only if at least 6,000 sherds were cast in total. A fragment of Philochorus says at least 6,000 had to name the same man. Most modern scholars follow Plutarch; at least one recent scholar argues there was no quorum at all. The pack teaches both readings and runs the same tally under each.

**What survives.** More than 10,000 ostraka have been found in Athens: over 9,000 in the Kerameikos (most in one deposit of about 8,500) and well over 1,000 from the Agora excavations. One group of 191, found in a well on the north slope of the Acropolis, almost all name Themistokles and are written in a small number of handwritings. The sherds and the handwriting are evidence; why so few hands wrote so many is a question of interpretation, and the pack leaves it open as the model case of evidence and interpretation.

**Later writers.** The *Constitution of the Athenians* was written in about 328 to 325 BCE by Aristotle or one of his students; who wrote it is debated. It was almost lost: the main text survives on a papyrus copy from about the late 1st century CE, found in Egypt, acquired by the British Museum about 1888 to 1890 (accounts differ) and first published in 1891, now in the British Library. Plutarch (born about 46 CE, died after 119 CE) wrote more than 500 years after the first ostracisms.

**Who was left out.** Women (including citizens' wives and daughters), enslaved people, and metics (free foreigners living in Athens) could not vote. From 451/450 BCE, a law proposed by Pericles limited citizenship to people with two Athenian parents. Children could not vote anywhere.

**Population, as ranges.** No full, trusted count survives. One ancient figure (from a count under Demetrius of Phalerum, about 317 to 307 BCE) gives 21,000 citizens; one modern historian argues for about 30,000 adult male citizens in the 300s BCE. Modern estimates for enslaved people run from about 20,000 to as many as 150,000; the same ancient count's 400,000 is generally not believed. The pack uses round practice ranges inside these estimates and says so: **citizen men 20,000 to 30,000; everyone living in Attica 150,000 to 300,000.** The share of everyone who could vote then lies between 1/15 (about 7 in 100) and 1/5. Marble's topic says "roughly 30% of adults"; the share of adults is bigger than the share of everyone, because children are counted in "everyone". The pack teaches that difference instead of a single percentage.

**Theater (short reading).** In Athens, plays were performed at festivals for the god Dionysus, the biggest being the City Dionysia in spring. Tragedies and comedies competed for prizes. All actors were men, wearing masks, which also helped the back rows see. The stone Theater of Dionysus held perhaps 14,000 to 17,000 people in the 300s BCE (estimates differ). Whether women watched is debated.

**The first Olympics (short reading).** The Olympic Games were held at Olympia, a sanctuary of Zeus. The traditional date of the first Games is 776 BCE; historians treat it as a tradition and say the Games began some time in the 700s BCE or earlier. The first recorded event was a short foot race (the stadion), and the first recorded winner was Koroibos. Only free Greek men competed. A separate festival for Hera had races for girls. A woman, Kyniska of Sparta, is listed as a victor because she owned winning chariot horses. One ancient writer says married women could not watch; historians debate that passage.

**Myths and Troy (short reading).** Greeks told stories about gods on Mount Olympus (a real mountain) and heroes. The *Iliad*, a long poem credited to Homer, tells of a war at Troy; it was probably composed between the mid-700s and early 600s BCE. At Hisarlik in Turkey, Heinrich Schliemann began digging in 1870 or 1871 (sources differ) and found a city with many layers (nine main periods). Most scholars identify the site with Troy. Whether a war like the *Iliad*'s happened is still argued. A real city does not make a poem's story true, and a story can remember a real place.

## The lesson

Jane's order: **teach, then guided, then practice.**

1. **Teach card 1, the hand count:** one idea (more hands up wins) and a worked example revealed step by step: 7 for, 4 against, count, compare, 7 is more.
2. **Teach card 2, evidence and interpretation:** one sherd, one sentence about it. "It has a name scratched on it" is evidence. "People disliked him" is interpretation.
3. **Teach card 3, who is in the room:** the same question, two different rules, two different answers.
4. **Guided item:** sort four cards into "What survives" and "What people think it means", with up to four visible steps.
5. **Practice:** the drawer activities and problem sets, any order.

**"I'm stuck" ladder** on every practice item: a hint; then a worked example of an easier similar case plus how to start this one; then "Skip for now", with no penalty and nothing recorded as failure.

**Contrasts:** direct democracy (Athens' assembly) and representative democracy (elected members); lot and election; evidence and interpretation; a share of everyone and a share of adults; a myth and an archaeological find; Athens' rule and Willowbank's rule.

**Top misconceptions designed against:**
- "Everyone in Athens voted." (Women, enslaved people and metics could not.)
- "Democracy has always meant one adult, one vote."
- "Choosing by lot is the same as an election."
- "The numbers are known exactly." (They are estimates, in ranges.)
- "If an old writer says it, it is a fact." (Plutarch and Philochorus disagree.)
- "A surviving object tells you what people thought." (Objects need interpretation.)
- "Troy was found, so the *Iliad* is history."
- "Athens was simply good" or "Athens was simply bad."
- "A majority of those present is a majority of everyone."

## The game or activity

Three stations in the drawer, open in any order. Every station shows its label: **A true thing from history**, **A made-up model**, or **A story from Willowbank**.

**Station 1, Sort the drawer.** Ten cards: six describe things that survive (an ostrakon naming Themistokles; the 191 sherds in one well; a kleroterion fragment; the stone law against tyranny of 337/336 BCE, Agora Museum I 6524, with its carving of Democracy crowning the People; the papyrus of the *Constitution of the Athenians*; a passage of Plutarch) and four are interpretations. The child drags or taps each into "What survives" or "What people think it means". Some survivors are themselves later writers, so a second sort asks "Made at the time, or written later?". Feedback per card. Each survivor card has a "Who is missing?" flip: what this source cannot tell us about women, enslaved people or metics.

**Station 2, The model kleroterion (simplified).** A slab with 5 columns (tribes, color-coded) and 10 rows. The child places tickets (numbered, no names) into slots, one per tribe per row; a "Fill for me" button does it. A tube holds 10 beads: 4 white, 6 black, shuffled. Each tap drops one bead: white means that row serves, black means it does not. After 10 drops, 4 rows serve: 20 people, exactly 4 from each tribe, whatever the order. The child can redraw as often as they like. **Model line, always shown:** *A simplified model. The real machine had more slots and tribes, and some of its details are still argued about.* Units: tickets and beads; the model chooses rows, never named people.

**Station 3, Who is in the room? (a made-up town).** Thistle Ford, a made-up town, must choose: mend the old footbridge, or dig a new well. 42 people, in four groups, each with its own split shown as counted dots: town-born men 8 bridge / 4 well; town-born women 4 / 8; newcomer adults 2 / 6; children 8 / 2. The child picks a rule and the hall fills:

| Rule | Who votes | Bridge | Well | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | Town-born men (like Athens' assembly) | 8 | 4 | Bridge |
| B | All town-born adults | 12 | 12 | Tie: ask again next week |
| C | All adults who live here | 14 | 18 | Well |
| D | Everyone, children too | 22 | 20 | Bridge |

The child then answers "Which rule seems fairest to you, and why?" by choosing a rule and a sentence stem. Nothing grades it. **Model line:** *A made-up town with made-up numbers. Within every group, people disagree.* The model has no enslaved people in it on purpose: enslavement is real history, taught in the drawer, never turned into a game piece.

**Three short readings** (theater, Olympics, myths and Troy), each one screen at three lengths, with two questions each in the problem sets.
