## The knowledge

Sources in the audit; computed numbers say so.

**Words and nesting.** A star makes its own light. A planet goes around a star and shines by reflected light; a moon goes around a planet. Dwarf planets, asteroids and comets also go around the Sun. A "shooting star" is a meteor, a speck of space rock glowing as it rushes into the air. Moon → Earth → Sun → Milky Way → universe.

**Names.** The five planets seen without a telescope carry Roman gods' names. William Herschel found Uranus on 13 March 1781; he wanted *Georgium Sidus* for King George III; Johann Bode proposed Uranus (the sky god of Greek stories, in Latin form); Britain's *Nautical Almanac* kept George's name until about 1850. Neptune (1846) has a Roman name. *Planet* is from Greek *planētēs*, "wanderer". Roman weekday names for the Moon and five planets survive in Spanish and French: *lunes/lundi*, *martes/mardi*, *miércoles/mercredi*, *jueves/jeudi*, *viernes/vendredi* (Moon, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus). Gods are labeled stories people told.

**Structure and kinds.** Rocky inner planets; the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter, mostly empty (spacecraft cross it without dodging); giant planets; then a far icy region with Pluto and many comets. The IAU's 2006 tests for a planet: it goes around the Sun, its gravity has made it nearly round, it has cleared its neighborhood. A dwarf planet passes only the first two and is not a moon: Ceres (largest in the belt), Pluto, Eris. The third test has no number, so some still argue. A comet is icy; near the Sun its tails are pushed away from the Sun, so a comet leaving the Sun goes tail first.

**Distance and year** (NSSDC mean distance ÷ 149.6 million km; NSSDC sidereal period ÷ 365.25; computed): Mercury 0.4, 0.24 yr (88 days); Venus 0.7, 0.62 (225 days); Earth 1, 1; Mars 1.5, 1.9 (687 days); Jupiter 5.2, 11.9; Saturn 9.6, 29.4; Uranus 19, 84; Neptune 30, 164.8 (child text: about 165). The planetary table's 59,800 days for Neptune is the tropical period; the sidereal 60,189 days is used. A farther planet has a longer path and moves more slowly along it. Keeper's Desk: Kepler's third law in these units, year × year = distance³ (within 2% for all eight, computed).

**Gravity.** Every mass pulls every other mass. Newton told William Stukeley in 1726 that a falling apple, about 60 years before, set him wondering; labeled as Newton's own late memory. The same pull holds the Moon around Earth and the planets around the Sun. Newton's cannonball (*Principia*): fired sideways from a high mountain fast enough, the ground curves away as fast as it falls, so it falls all the way around. Floating is spec 164's, revisited only. **Mass and weight:** weight = mass × g; with g = 9.8, 1.62 and 3.71 m/s² (Earth, Moon, Mars; NSSDC rounded) a 30 kg sack weighs 294 N, 48.6 N and 111.3 N and is 30 kg in all three; on the Moon it feels like about 5 kg (computed). Jupiter's g is left out (no surface; quoted at a cloud level). **Neptune by math:** Uranus strayed from its predicted path; Urbain Le Verrier computed where an unseen planet must be; Johann Galle and Heinrich d'Arrest found Neptune in Berlin on 23 September 1846 within about 1°. John Couch Adams had computed independently.

**Brightness and distance.** Apparent brightness (how bright it looks here) is separate from actual brightness (how much light it gives). A lantern looks dimmer farther off because its light spreads over more area; it has not weakened. The Sun looks brightest only because it is by far the nearest star. Sirius, the brightest night star, is about 8.6 light-years away and gives about 25 times the Sun's light. Rigel looks a little fainter but gives tens of thousands of times the Sun's light or more; its distance is usually given as about 850 light-years (Hipparcos; Gaia cannot measure a star this bright), but estimates differ by a hundred or more, and the spread is taught. Keeper's Desk: twice as far spreads light over 4 times the area, a quarter as bright; three times, a ninth (a point light, stated as simplified).

**The Long Zoom.** A light-year is how far light goes in a year, about 9.46 trillion km (computed). Light time: Moon 1.3 s, Sun 8 min 19 s (spec 141), Neptune about 4.2 h (computed). Proxima Centauri, the nearest star after the Sun, is 4.24 light-years away (about 40 trillion km). The Milky Way is about 100,000 light-years across (NASA; wider estimates exist); the Sun is about 26,000 light-years from the center; more than 100 billion stars (estimates to 400 billion). Andromeda is about 2.5 million light-years away. Galaxy counts run from hundreds of billions to about 2 trillion (a 2016 estimate), so the child text says "billions". **On spec 141's 20 cm-Sun walk** (computed): Neptune 649 m; Proxima about 5,770 km, about 9,000 times farther, a little less than the Pyrenees to New York (about 6,000 km); the Milky Way about 136 million km across. **Look-back:** we see the Sun as it was 8 minutes ago and Andromeda as it was about 2.5 million years ago.

**History cards.** Henrietta Swan Leavitt, one of the low-paid women "computers" at Harvard College Observatory, measured star brightness on photographic plates. On plates of the Small Magellanic Cloud (taken at Arequipa, Peru) she found that slower-pulsing Cepheid variables are brighter: early results 1908, the 1912 paper on 25 Cepheids (signed by director Edward Pickering, stating it was prepared by Miss Leavitt). Her key step: the cloud's stars are about equally far, so brighter-looking meant really brighter. On the night of 5-6 October 1923, with Mount Wilson's 100-inch Hooker telescope, Edwin Hubble found a Cepheid in the Andromeda "nebula"; with Leavitt's rule he estimated a little under 1 million light-years (about 900,000; modern about 2.5 million), far outside the Milky Way. Cecilia Payne's 1925 thesis, *Stellar Atmospheres*, showed stars are mostly hydrogen and helium; Henry Norris Russell judged it impossible, and her thesis itself (her words, after his objection) called the hydrogen and helium abundance "almost certainly not real"; later work, including Russell's own in 1929, confirmed it. Sources disagree on which "first" she was, so none is claimed.

**Life of a star.** Gravity pulls a cloud of gas and dust (a nebula) together until the center is hot enough for nuclear fusion, joining hydrogen into helium and releasing energy; it is not fire. In the long steady stage, the outward push balances gravity. A Sun-like star swells into a red giant, sheds a glowing shell (a planetary nebula, unrelated to planets) and leaves a white dwarf that slowly cools. Stars of about 8 times the Sun's mass or more (models give 8 to 10) live shorter, brighter lives and end in a supernova, leaving a neutron star or black hole. The Sun formed about 4.6 billion years ago and will become a red giant in about 5 billion years; it is too small for a supernova. Astronomers assemble the story from many stars at different stages. Most elements heavier than helium were made in stars; the carbon in living things was made by helium fusion, mostly in aging giant stars. Child text keeps the Sun's future calm and does not mention the inner planets' fate.

**The Sun rule** (fixed, from spec 162; never a model result): never look at the Sun, with sunglasses, through cloud, or through binoculars, a telescope, a camera or any lens.

## The game or activity

**(a) Word Wall.** Nine word-picture cards flip to one feature each. A nest picture takes drag or tap-to-place cards. A **Names** drawer holds the Uranus and Neptune cards and the weekday table in three languages, gods labeled *A story people told*.

**(b) Planet Table.** Eight planet cards; the child places each on a chart (distance across, year up) and the dots curve upward. **Sort by size** shows Jupiter below Neptune on years. **What kind of thing?** sorts Ceres, Pluto, a comet, an asteroid, the Moon and Jupiter with the three tests as tick boxes. Model line: *Simplified numbers rounded from NASA's table; real orbits are slightly oval.*

**(c) Newton's Hill.** A drawn mountain on a small round world. Four speeds play authored paths: lands near, lands far, falls all the way around, leaves. Gravity's arrow always points to the center. A **Moon scale** weighs the 30 kg sack on Earth, Moon and Mars; the mass label never changes. Model line: *A made-up model: tiny world, no air, drawn paths.*

**(d) Lantern Lane.** The bridge at night, Ember and two lanterns. **Steps away** (1, 2, 3) moves a light; a squares panel (Keeper's Desk) shows 1, 4 or 9 squares. A **True strength** tag never changes. A strong lantern at 2 steps and a weak one at 1 step look the same. Model line: *A simplified picture: a point of light, no fog, made-up strengths.*

**(e) The Long Zoom.** A one-axis scroll strip with five stops (Earth and Moon, solar system, nearest stars, Milky Way, galaxies), each with a light-time readout. A **20 cm Sun** toggle extends 141's walk; Leavitt and Hubble sit at the galaxy stop.

**(f) Star Story.** Cards from cloud to long steady life, then a fork by a **Mass** switch (like the Sun / much heavier), with balance arrows (gravity in, push out). An orchard panel shows trees of all ages; Payne's card sits at the steady stage. Model line: *A simplified story: real stars take millions to billions of years; not to scale.*
