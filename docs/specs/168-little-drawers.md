## The knowledge

Plain adult statements, each tied to a source in the [audit](../source-audits/168-little-drawers.md).

**The particle model** (always labeled a model). Spec 145's picture (solid, liquid, gas arrangements; empty space, not air, between gas particles; particles never change size) is assumed and used to explain: **Squashing:** sealed air pushes in (empty space between particles); sealed water hardly moves `[check: textbook-standard, no source fetched]`. **Density:** the same particles spread through far more space make a gas much less dense. **Ice floats:** freezing locks water molecules into an open pattern, so ice takes about 9% more space (density about 0.917 g/cm³ against about 1.00); mass is unchanged. **Gas pressure** (Keeper's Desk): particles hitting the container walls.

**Changing state.** Melting, freezing, boiling, condensing and sublimation (solid straight to gas; frost is the reverse) rearrange the same particles. On a pure substance's **heating curve**, temperature rises within a state but stays flat during melting and boiling: the energy loosens the particles' hold on each other instead of speeding them up. Water's flats are spec 145's 0 °C and 100 °C; mixtures such as chocolate melt over a range. **Evaporation** happens at the surface at any temperature; **boiling** happens through the liquid at the boiling point, and its bubbles are the substance's own vapor, not air. Freezing removes energy; it does not "add cold."

**Atoms, elements, compounds.** An **element** has one kind of **atom** (118 are named); a **compound** has two or more kinds joined in a fixed ratio; a **mixture** is substances together, not joined (spec 145 owns separating them). O₂ is still an element. A **formula** counts atoms: H₂O is two hydrogen and one oxygen; the small number counts only the atom before it. A compound's properties differ from its elements': sodium (a soft metal, violent with water) and chlorine (a poisonous gas) make table salt, which simply dissolves. Sieving or filtering cannot split a compound. **Simple atom picture** (Keeper's Desk): a tiny nucleus of protons and neutrons with electrons around it. **History:** John Dalton outlined his atomic theory in 1803 and published it in *A New System of Chemical Philosophy* (first part 1808), with symbols for the elements' atoms.

**The periodic table.** Rows are **periods** (7); columns are **groups** (18). A group's elements behave alike (group 1 metals react with water, more strongly down the group; the last group's gases hardly react). **Mendeleev**'s first table (1869) ordered elements by atomic weight, started new rows so similar ones lined up, and left gaps. His "eka-aluminum" (described 1871) had atomic weight about 68 and density about 5.9 to 6.0 g/cm³ (sources differ). In 1875 Paul-Émile Lecoq de Boisbaudran found gallium (named for Gallia, Latin for France); his first density was lower, and rechecked it was 5.9. Gallium's atomic weight is 69.7. Scandium (1879, Lars Fredrik Nilson) and germanium (1886, Clemens Winkler) filled two more gaps. The modern table is ordered by **atomic number**, after Henry Moseley's X-ray work (1913 to 1914). **Marie and Pierre Curie** announced polonium (for Poland, her birth country) and radium in Paris in 1898; she shared the 1903 Physics Nobel and won the 1911 Chemistry prize alone. The brothers Juan José and Fausto Elhuyar isolated tungsten at the Royal Seminary in Bergara in 1783.

**Metals and non-metals.** Metals tend to be shiny, bendable, good conductors and solid; non-metals dull, brittle, poor conductors, many of them gases. A **cluster**, not one test. Exceptions: mercury is liquid at room temperature (melts at −38.83 °C); graphite (carbon) conducts; gallium melts at about 29.76 °C. Shine alone proves nothing. Magnets (spec 154) and conduction (spec 163's Tester Bench) are cited, not re-taught. Metals sit left and center, non-metals upper right, metalloids on a staircase between. Keeper's note: metal oxides tend to be alkaline, non-metal oxides acidic.

**Physical and chemical changes.** Physical: the same particles stay intact and only move (melting, dissolving, bending). Chemical: atoms regroup into new substances with new properties (burning, rusting, cooking). Clues (spec 145) are checked by comparing properties before and after. **Mass is kept in both** because atoms are only regrouped, as long as every gas is counted. Iron rusts when both oxygen and water reach it; rust is a new, flaky substance. Copper weathers to a green layer (copper sulfates or carbonates, depending on the air) that protects the metal below.

**Acids, alkalis and pH.** pH runs 0 to 14: below 7 acidic, 7 neutral (pure water at 25 °C; a value, not "no reading"), above 7 alkaline. Lemon juice and vinegar are about 2 to 3, baking soda solution about 8 to 9; bleach and oven cleaner sit near the top and are corrosive, like strong acids. pH measures acidity, not danger. An **indicator** changes color with pH: red cabbage contains anthocyanin, which turns red or pink in acid, stays purple near neutral, and goes blue, green, then yellow as alkalinity rises. Litmus is red in acid, blue in alkali. Mixing an acid with an alkali moves pH toward 7 (**neutralization**). Søren Sørensen introduced pH in 1909 at the Carlsberg Laboratory in Copenhagen. Keeper's note: each whole pH step is a tenfold change in acidity.

**Energy in and out.** Giving energy to the surroundings warms them (**exothermic**: burning, freezing, iron hand warmers); taking it in cools them (**endothermic**: melting, baking soda with vinegar, cold packs that work by dissolving). Classify by the thermometer's direction. Needing heat to start differs from taking heat in overall. Qualitative only.

## The game or activity

Every screen shows: *These are simplified pictures. Real materials can behave differently.* No score, timer, lock or mastery label.

**(a) The Squeeze Box (Seedling to Keeper's Desk).** Sealed syringes of air, water and sand with plunger sliders: air goes from 60 mL to 30 mL; water and sand barely move. Particle windows show spacing; a **counting window** over each state of one substance is a qualitative density meter. **The Ice Jar:** 100 mL of water freezes; the level rises to about 109 mL; the scale stays at 100 g; the particle window shows an open pattern; the ice cube floats in a glass beside. **The Pan Curve** (Sapling, Keeper's): pan picture, particle window and a self-drawing temperature-time graph. Model: ice from −10 °C reaches 0 °C at 2 minutes, stays flat until 8, reaches 100 °C at 18, stays flat while boiling. *A made-up model with tidy numbers; real pans heat unevenly.* An adult heats in the picture; the child only taps "next minute."

**(b) The Snap Tray (Seedling to Keeper's Desk).** Atom tokens (H, O, C, N, Na, Cl, Fe) snap together; a formula reads off live (H₂O, CO₂, O₂, NaCl). Sort models into **Element / Compound / Mixture**. "Try to separate" works on a mixture and splits nothing in water. Keeper's Desk: tap an atom for its simple picture (nucleus, electrons; not to scale). *Tokens are a model: real atoms are not colored balls.*

**(c) Mendeleev's Cards (Sapling, Keeper's Desk).** Twenty element cards (lithium to chlorine excluding neon, then potassium, calcium, zinc, arsenic, selenium, bromine; symbol, name, atomic weight, two property tags such as "soft metal, fizzes in water"). Zinc's tag, "burns to a white powder," puts it in magnesium's column, as Mendeleev did. The child orders them by weight, starting a new row when a property repeats. Blanks appear under aluminum and silicon; the child predicts each from its neighbors (metal or not, weight between). Léon's letter reveals gallium. **Metal or not?** sorts eight property-cluster cards (copper, iron, mercury, sulfur, graphite, oxygen, gallium, and the magpie's glass bead, which is not an element at all) and lights each element's place on the shaded 118-drawer cabinet. Rows are labeled period, columns group.

**(d) The Color Jar (Sapling, Keeper's Desk).** Picture-only red cabbage water in a row of glasses. Drop in lemon juice, vinegar, plain water or baking soda water; each turns its authored color (baking soda water: blue-green) and the child slides it onto a 0-14 strip. **Neutralize:** add baking soda water drop by drop to a pink glass and watch it pass purple. **The thermometer:** vinegar at 20 °C, baking soda added, 16 °C; a hand warmer pack 20 °C to 50 °C; both made-up readings. **The atom window:** for melting ice, dissolving sugar, rusting iron and baking soda with vinegar, a window shows molecules intact or atoms regrouping; the child sorts **Physical / Chemical**. **The balloon scale** (Keeper's): a bottle with a balloon over its neck reads 250 g before and after; an open bottle reads 250 g, then 247 g, as gas leaves. *On screen only. Never seal a real fizzing bottle.*
