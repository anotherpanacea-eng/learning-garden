## The knowledge

Sourced in the [audit](../source-audits/268-old-keepers-spellings.md). DfE is England's *English Appendix 1: Spelling* (2013, Years 5 and 6 unless noted).

**Silent letters.** A silent letter is written but not said. DfE: some letters no longer sounded "used to be sounded hundreds of years ago", as the k in knight and its gh, once the sound of Scottish *loch*. Once said: knoll (Old English *cnoll*), knife, knight, knead, write, wrist, gnaw, gnat, lamb (the b sound lost in Middle English), high (gh), which (wh, once /hw/; some speakers in Scotland, Ireland and the US South keep it). **Added by writers, never said in English:** island (*iland*; s inserted in the 1500s by confusion with unrelated *isle*); debt (*dette*) and doubt (*doute*), b inserted in the late 1400s and 1500s after Latin *debitum*, *dubitare*; receipt (*receite*, p after Latin *recepta*); aisle (s about 1700, copying *isle*; grown-up note). DfE's own examples list doubt and island beside "used to be sounded", and Marble's evidence treats psalm as once pronounced; the pack teaches the two histories apart and leaves psalm out. **Family words say the letter:** sign, signal; muscle, muscular; bomb, bombard; solemn, solemnity; autumn, autumnal; doubt, dubious; debt, debit. Silent t in thistle, castle, listen is shown without a history claim.

**-cious and -tious** (DfE). A -ce root gives -cious: space, spacious; grace, gracious; vice, vicious; malice, malicious. Exception: anxious. Learn: precious, delicious, conscious. A -tion family usually gives -tious (caution, cautious; ambition, ambitious; nutrition, nutritious; infection, infectious; fiction, fictitious); this family clue is the pack's, stated as "usual".

**-cial and -tial** (DfE). -cial is common after a vowel letter (official, special, artificial) and -tial after a consonant letter (partial, confidential, essential). Exceptions: initial; financial, commercial, provincial (from finance, commerce, province).

**-ant and -ent** (DfE). -ent after soft c or g and qu (innocent, decent, intelligent, frequent); -ant where the c or g is hard (significant, elegant) or an -ation word is in the family (observant, hesitant, tolerant). Many words must be learned (assistant, obedient, independent). Child text teaches the hard and soft letters; the -ation clue appears in bench pairs.

**-able and -ible** (DfE). -able is far more common; it usually follows a whole word (dependable, comfortable, enjoyable, reasonable; reliable with y to i) or an -ation family (adorable, considerable, tolerable, applicable). -ible usually follows a part that is not a word (possible, horrible, terrible, visible, incredible); sensible is the named exception. A -ce or -ge root keeps its e before -able (noticeable, changeable, manageable); -ible keeps c and g soft without it (forcible, legible). -able and -ible become -ably and -ibly. Latin *-abilis*, *-ibilis* are the source; *visible* is spelled alike in English, Spanish and French.

**Doubling after -fer** (DfE). Double the r when fer keeps the stress (referring, referred, referral, preferring, preferred, transferring); not when it moves (reference, referee, preference). Offer and suffer are stressed on the first part (offering, suffering). Stress has no letter form, so it is clapped with a grown-up, as in 254.

**Hyphens, ei and ough.** DfE allows a hyphen when a vowel-final prefix meets a vowel (*co-ordinate*, *re-enter*, *co-operate*, *co-own*); Merriam-Webster and Chicago close most such words (*coordinate*, *cooperate*, *reenter*), so this UK convention is a grown-up note only. A hyphen that separates meanings works in both: re-cover and recover, re-sign and resign, co-op and coop. DfE: "i before e except after c" applies only where ei sounds /iː/ (receive, ceiling, deceive, perceive); exceptions seize, protein, caffeine. In science and ancient, ie after c is not /iː/. ough spells several sounds: though, dough; through; thought, bought; tough, rough; cough; bough (the curriculum's *plough* is *plow* in US spelling).

**Homophones** (DfE list). Nouns in -ce, verbs in -se: advice, advise; device, devise; prophecy, prophesy. Affect is usually a verb and effect usually a noun (effect as a verb means bring about). Led is the past of lead; lead is also the metal. Aisle, isle; aloud, allowed; heard, herd; guessed, guest; past, passed; steal, steel; complement, compliment; principal, principle; stationary, stationery. Story pairs: need, knead; ways, weighs; road, rode; which, witch; where, wear. Practice/practise and licence/license are UK noun/verb pairs; US uses *practice* and *license* for both, so neither is keyed. Farther/father and draught are left out (accent and UK spelling).

**The Years 5-6 word list** (statutory in England; a reference only here). Sorted by trick: double letters (accommodate, committee, embarrass, necessary, recommend; harass has one r), silent letters (muscle, yacht, rhythm, foreign), hidden vowels found in a family word (definite, define; desperate, desperation; familiar, family, DfE's own example), endings (conscious, sufficient, relevant, existence, available), ie and ei (achieve, mischievous, leisure). US forms replace *criticise*, *marvellous*, *neighbour*, *programme*, *recognise*.

**Long words by rule (Keeper's Desk).** Split into parts, spell each by a known rule, check the whole in a dictionary. Greek parts often spell /f/ as ph and /ɪ/ as y (photosynthesis). *Algorithm* came from al-Khwarizmi's name through Latin *algorismus*; in the 1600s its spelling was reshaped after Greek *arithmos*, number, a mistaken link. Flood words: catchment, embankment, permeable (family: permeation), precipitation.
