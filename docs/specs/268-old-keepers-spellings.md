## The knowledge

Sourced in the [audit](../source-audits/268-old-keepers-spellings.md). DfE is England's *English Appendix 1: Spelling* (2013, Years 5 and 6 unless noted).

**Silent letters.** A silent letter is written but not said. DfE: some letters no longer sounded "used to be sounded hundreds of years ago", as the k in knight and its gh, once the sound of Scottish *loch*. Once said: knoll (Old English *cnoll*), knife, knight, knead, write, wrist, gnaw, gnat, lamb (the b sound lost in Middle English), high (gh), which (wh, once /hw/; some speakers in Scotland, Ireland and the US South keep it). **Added by writers, never part of ordinary speech:** island (*iland*; s inserted in the 1500s by confusion with unrelated *isle*); debt (*dette*) and doubt (*doute*), b inserted in the late 1400s and 1500s after Latin *debitum*, *dubitare* (a few 1590s pedants urged saying it, which Shakespeare mocks, so child text says people "kept saying the words without them" rather than "never said" for these two); receipt (*receite*, p inserted later after Latin); aisle (spelled like *isle*, then reshaped after French *aile*; spec only, not in child text). DfE's own examples list doubt and island beside "used to be sounded", and Marble's evidence treats psalm as once pronounced; the pack teaches the two histories apart and leaves psalm out. **Family words say the letter:** sign, signal; muscle, muscular; bomb, bombard; solemn, solemnity; autumn, autumnal; doubt, dubious; debt, debit. Silent t in thistle, castle, listen is shown without a history claim.

**-cious and -tious** (DfE). A -ce root usually gives -cious: space, spacious; grace, gracious; vice, vicious; malice, malicious. Exception: anxious. Learn: precious, delicious, conscious. A -tion family usually gives -tious (caution, cautious; ambition, ambitious; nutrition, nutritious; infection, infectious; fiction, fictitious); this family clue is the pack's, stated as "usual".

**-cial and -tial** (DfE). -cial is common after a vowel letter (official, special, artificial) and -tial after a consonant letter (partial, confidential, essential). Exceptions: initial; financial, commercial, provincial (from finance, commerce, province).

**-ant and -ent** (DfE). -ent after soft c or g and qu (innocent, decent, intelligent, frequent), usually (pageant and sergeant keep a soft g with -eant); -ant where an -ation word is in the family (observant, hesitant, tolerant). Hard c or g takes -ant (significant, elegant) because e would soften it; that clue is Marble's evidence and English letter-sound rules, not DfE text. Many words must be learned (assistant, obedient, independent). Child text teaches the hard and soft letters; the -ation clue appears in bench pairs.

**-able and -ible** (DfE). -able is far more common; it usually follows a whole word (dependable, comfortable, enjoyable, reasonable; reliable with y to i) or an -ation family (adorable, considerable, tolerable, applicable). -ible usually follows a part that is not a word (possible, horrible, terrible, visible, incredible); sensible is the named exception. A -ce or -ge root keeps its e before -able (noticeable, changeable, manageable); -ible keeps c and g soft without it (forcible, legible). -able and -ible become -ably and -ibly. Latin *-abilis*, *-ibilis* are the source; *visible* is spelled alike in English, Spanish and French.

**Doubling after -fer** (DfE, taught as "usually"). Double the r when fer keeps the stress (referring, referred, referral, preferring, preferred, conferred); not when it moves (reference, referee, preference, conference). DfE's *transferring* is left off the benches: many US speakers stress TRANS-fer, and US *transferable* keeps one r. Offer and suffer are stressed on the first part (offering, suffering). Stress has no letter form, so it is clapped with a grown-up, as in 254.

**Hyphens, ei and ough.** DfE allows a hyphen when a vowel-final prefix meets a vowel (*co-ordinate*, *re-enter*, *co-operate*, *co-own*); Merriam-Webster and Chicago close most such words (*coordinate*, *cooperate*, *reenter*), so this UK convention is a grown-up note only. A hyphen that separates meanings works in both: re-cover and recover, re-sign and resign, co-op and coop. Dictionaries also record unhyphenated *recover* for "cover again" (*recover a chair*; Wiktionary, a separate etymology), and *resign* for "sign again" as a proscribed variant, so the pack teaches the hyphen as the clearest spelling, never the only one: C3 asks which spelling makes "cover again" clearest, and the hyphen avoids confusion with "get back". DfE: "i before e except after c" applies only where ei sounds /iː/ (receive, ceiling, deceive, perceive); exceptions seize, protein, caffeine. In science and ancient, ie after c is not /iː/. ough spells several sounds: though, dough; through; thought, bought; tough, rough; cough; bough (the curriculum's *plough* is *plow* in US spelling).

**Homophones and other words often confused** (DfE's list has both). Not homophones, though often confused: nouns in -ce and verbs in -se, advice /ədˈvaɪs/ and advise /ədˈvaɪz/ (Wiktionary US IPA); device, devise; prophecy, prophesy. The pack teaches advice and advise by sound (an s sound, a z sound) and by part of speech, and never calls them homophones. Affect is usually a verb and effect usually a noun (effect as a verb means bring about); they are homophones only with the weak-vowel merger (Wiktionary: GA effect /ɪˈfɛkt/, affect /əˈfɛkt/), so the pack calls them a mixed-up pair. Led is the past of lead; lead is also the metal. Aisle, isle; aloud, allowed; heard, herd; guessed, guest; past, passed; steal, steel; complement, compliment; principal, principle; stationary, stationery. Story pairs, each a homophone in US English by Wiktionary IPA: need, knead, kneed; ways, weighs, way's; road, rode, rowed; high, hi, hie; there, their, they're; to, too, two; led and lead the metal; which, witch and where, wear, ware for speakers with the wine-whine merger (most US speakers; the grown-up note names it). Practice/practise and licence/license are UK noun/verb pairs; US uses *practice* and *license* for both, so neither is keyed. Farther/father and draught are left out (accent and UK spelling).

**The Years 5-6 word list** (statutory in England; a reference only here). Sorted by trick: double letters (accommodate, committee, embarrass, necessary, recommend; harass has one r), silent letters (muscle, yacht, rhythm, foreign), hidden vowels found in a family word (definite, definition; desperate, desperation; familiar, family, DfE's own example), endings (conscious, sufficient, relevant, existence, available), ie and ei (achieve, mischievous, leisure). US forms replace *criticise*, *marvellous*, *neighbour*, *programme*, *recognise*.

**Long words by rule (Keeper's Desk).** Split into parts, spell each by a known rule, check the whole in a dictionary. Greek parts often spell /f/ as ph and /ɪ/ as y (photosynthesis). *Algorithm* came from al-Khwarizmi's name through Latin *algorismus*; in the 1600s its spelling was reshaped after Greek *arithmos*, number, a mistaken link. Flood words: catchment, embankment, permeable (family: permeation), precipitation.

## The lesson

Jane's **teach -> guided -> practice**: sixteen teach cards, one idea each, with a worked example revealed one step per tap; each set's first item shows 1-3 visible steps. **"I'm stuck" ladder** on every item: hint -> an easier worked case plus how to start this one (knee; grace; re-FER; ate and eight; family and familiar; sun + flower) -> skip for now, no penalty.

**Contrasts:** once said vs added by writers (knoll, island); silent in one family word, said in another (sign, signal; signing still silent); -ce root vs -tion family; vowel letter vs consonant letter before -cial/-tial; hard vs soft c (significant, magnificent); whole word vs part (depend, poss-); keep e vs drop e (noticeable, adorable); beat on fer vs moved (preferring, preference); fer-final but first-stressed (offering); recover vs re-cover; ee after c vs ie not ee (receive, science); noun -ce vs verb -se, heard as an s or a z (advice, advise: a mixed-up pair, not homophones); past anchor vs present (led, lead); real Greek parts vs a reshaped spelling (photosynthesis, algorithm).

Every screen: *Rules usually work. A dictionary has the last word.* No score, timer, lock or mastery label. Taps only; every drag has a tap equivalent. Spelling choices are shown, never read aloud (they sound alike); scenes, cards and prompts have read-aloud. No audio asset; brief 55 is not needed.

- **(a) The Stained Map (Seedling, Sapling; first slice).** Eight stained labels: pick the missing letter or "nothing missing" (the Sponge, where no letters were stained out or lost; the pack never says every letter of a word is sounded, since sponge's e is silent). **Once said, or added?** sorts eight cards by the word-history note printed on each, so the sort rests on what the card says. **Hear it in the family** pairs seven silent letters with family words that say them.
- **(b) The Endings Shelf (Sapling).** The librarian's newly written letter to Vadoalto with seven ending gaps across four trays; flip pairs (observant, observance; visible, visibly). One Keeper's note sets English, Spanish and French *visible* side by side (fluent readers to check).
- **(c) The Beat Bench (Sapling).** Six -fer cards clapped with a grown-up (nothing recorded), two look-alikes (offer, suffer), three hyphen pairs by meaning, four ei-after-c words, and an ough wall sorted by sound with a grown-up.
- **(d) Pip's Copy (Seedling, Sapling).** Tap a slip, then choose its fix from three. **Deck 1** (any time): Pip's postcard to Léon, three slips and one correct line to leave alone. **Deck 2** (Keeper's Desk, Act IV, after page 3 is free; spec 120 §5 sets a page's original text at the Keeper's Desk): Pip's by-ear copy of page 3's first four sentences with eight homophone slips.
