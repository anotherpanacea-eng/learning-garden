## Purpose

Learning Garden already makes tenths on one strip (spec 81) and hundredths on a 10 × 10 square of the same whole (spec 85, which explicitly leaves out percentages). Draft PR #126's Keeper's Desk uses percentages twice without teaching them (spec 118 Case 1 step 2 gives 25% and 5% beside fractions; Case 3 step 3 turns rooting results into 75% and 80%). Nobody yet teaches **what a percent is**, **why 50% is not 50 of anything**, **how to find a percent of an amount**, **how percent changes behave**, or **how to read a percent claim honestly**. This pack takes eight untouched topics there.

A child comes to understand that percent means "out of every hundred" and the % sign names it; that 100% is the whole you start from, and percents can go past it; that a fraction, a decimal and a percent can name one amount (40% = 40/100 = 2/5 = 0.4), and small percents keep their zero (9% = 0.09); that a percent is always a percent **of something**, so the same percent of a bigger whole is more; that any whole-number percent can be built from 10% and 1% pieces; that one amount is written as a percent of another by putting it out of the whole and restating it out of 100; that every percent change multiplies the amount at that moment, so up 10% then down 10% lands lower; and that an honest claim names its whole, its count and its starting size.

**Builds on, never re-teaches:** spec 85's same-whole 10 × 10 square and its 0.3 = 0.30 work (here: the same square, now named in percents); spec 81's tenths (here: 10% as one tenth); spec 52's fractions on a line (here: the landmark strip); spec 118 Case 3's "percent of what?" learn card (here: taught in full; Case 3 becomes a purposeful revisit). Proposed sibling spec 199 owns decimal place value and fraction-decimal conversion (1/8 = 0.125 by division); this pack recaps only the hundredths it needs. Proposed sibling spec 201 owns ratio notation, sharing in a ratio and scale factor; this pack uses one ratio only to contrast part-to-part with part-to-whole.

**Not targeted, and narrowed:** *Fractions, Decimals & Percentages* (`mt_hlGKg5M7qJ`) is a META "use structure" topic whose evidence also includes angle facts and polygons; this pack makes a **partial claim**: it teaches the fraction-decimal-percent and factor-pair (25 × 4 = 100) parts, two of the four structures the description names. Angles on a line and polygon angle sums are taught by sibling spec 182 (angle rules) under its own targets, which does not claim this id, so the atlas should record this topic as partial until one place covers all four. *Percentages (age 9+)* (`mt_ESgc4YBw-a`) is a vocabulary topic that also names scale and scale factor; this pack teaches percent, proportion, ratio, part-to-whole, part-to-part, out of and equivalent, and leaves scale factor to spec 201. *Percentages (age 12+)* (`mt_iEXqN48w3x`) is taught in full as its description reads (increase, decrease, the original value after one change, simple interest); compound interest lies outside that description and is only named to fence it off, so this one is not a narrowing.

## The knowledge

**Percent.** *Per cent*, "for each hundred". 40% means 40 out of every 100. On a 10 × 10 square of one whole, one little square is 1% and one row is 10%. 100% is the whole; 50% is half; 0% is none. **Over 100%:** 100% is the whole you started from, never a ceiling: one full square and half of a second is 150%, and 520 baskets against last year's 400 is 130%.

**Three names for one amount.** 25 out of 100 is 25/100 = 1/4, 0.25 and 25%. Landmarks: 100% = 1; 75% = 3/4 = 0.75; 50% = 1/2 = 0.5; 25% = 1/4 = 0.25; 20% = 1/5 = 0.2; 10% = 1/10 = 0.1; 1% = 1/100 = 0.01. Keeper's Desk adds 40% = 2/5, 80% = 4/5, 12.5% = 1/8 = 0.125 (half of a quarter) and 150% = 3/2 = 1.5. **Small percents keep their zero:** 9% = 9 hundredths = 0.09; 0.9 is 90%. Read 0.09 as "nine hundredths" so the place stays visible. **Simplest form:** write the percent over 100 and divide top and bottom by a common factor: 40% = 2/5, 35% = 7/20. **Notation:** write "10% of 420 is 42", never "10% = 42"; "9% = 0.09" is an equivalence and is fine.

**Words for shares.** A **ratio** compares part to part (1 cup of juice to 4 of water, 1 : 4). A **proportion** compares a part with the whole (1 out of 5). A percent is a proportion out of 100 (20%). Reading 3 red : 2 green as "150% red" mistakes part-to-part for part-to-whole; red is 3 out of 5, 60%.

**Percent of what?** A percent acts on a whole. 50% of 20 is 10; 50% of 200 is 100. A bigger percent of a smaller whole can be less: 80% of 60 is 48, while 10% of 500 is 50.

**A percent of an amount.** Build from benchmarks: 10% is the whole ÷ 10, 1% is the whole ÷ 100, 5% is half of 10%, 20% is double 10%, 25% is a quarter, 50% is half. 15% of 360 = 36 + 18 = 54; 36% of 400 = 3 × 40 + 6 × 4 = 144. A common slip finds 5% as ÷ 5 (that is 20%). **Pick the handiest name:** 75% of $80 is fastest as 3/4: 80 ÷ 4 × 3 = 60; 25 × 16 = 25 × 4 × 4 = 100 × 4 = 400 uses the same 25 × 4 = 100 hinge. **Discount and price:** 25% off a $40 ladder is $10 off and $30 to pay.

**One amount as a percent of another.** Put the part over the whole, then restate out of 100: 12 of 30 low fields = 12/30 = 4/10 = 40%; 18 of 25 = 72%, which beats 70 of 100. The whole decides: the same 12 fields are 40% of the 30 low fields and 15% of the 80-field orchard. Swapping part and whole (30 out of 12, 250%) is the classic slip.

**Percent change.** Up r% multiplies by (1 + r/100): up 10% is × 1.1, up 15% is × 1.15 (× 1.5 would be up 50%). Down r% multiplies by (1 − r/100): 25% off is × 0.75. **Up then down:** $2.00 × 1.1 = $2.20, then × 0.9 = $1.98, because the second 10% is of the bigger price; together × 0.99. **Back to the price before:** after 20% off a ladder costs $64; $64 is 80% of the old price, so $64 ÷ 0.8 = $80 (adding 20% of $64 gives $76.80, the classic error). **Simple interest:** the same percent of the first amount each year: $500 at 3% for 4 years earns 4 × $15 = $60 (interest on interest would be about $62.75; that is compound, named only to fence it off).

**Reading a claim honestly.** Ask: percent of what? how many is that? from what start? "Apple sales up 50%!" is true for 4 to 6 baskets and is only 2 baskets. "Wet fields up 5%" for 8 to 12 of 80 fields is 5 **percentage points** (10% to 15%) and a 50% rise in wet fields. A claim that names its whole and its count can be checked; one that does not, cannot.

**Real anchors (see audit).**
- **The centesima.** Ancient Rome taxed goods sold at public auction at one part in a hundred, the *centesima rerum venalium* ("the hundredth on things for sale"). Augustus assigned its proceeds to the military treasury (*aerarium militare*) for veterans; Tacitus records that Tiberius later halved it to one part in two hundred (the *ducentesima*). When it began is disputed (some sources link it to Augustus or the civil wars, Britannica to Julius Caesar), so child text says only what it taxed, where the money went under Augustus and that Tiberius halved it.
- **The % sign.** Italian merchants wrote *per cento*, abbreviated *p 100* and *p cento*. In pages added around 1435 to an Italian arithmetic of 1425, a scribe wrote "pc" with a small loop (reported by David Eugene Smith in 1908); the loop and letters slowly became the modern sign over the following centuries. Child text says "an Italian arithmetic book from the 1400s" and gives no exact year.

Both anchors are European, since percent notation grew from Roman and Italian commerce; the review may suggest a further anchor from another tradition (owner question 3).

**"I'm stuck" ladder** on every item: (1) the hint; (2) an easier worked case plus how to start this one (the companion lists fourteen); (3) **Skip for now**, no penalty, nothing recorded against the child.

**Contrasts:** percent vs count; 0.09 vs 0.9; 4/10 vs 2/5 (equal vs simplest); ratio vs proportion; same percent, different whole; discount vs price; × 1.15 vs × 1.5; adding a percent back vs dividing by the multiplier; simple vs interest on interest; percentage points vs percent change; low fields vs the orchard.

**Top misconceptions designed against:** 50% means 50; 24% means 1/24; 9% = 0.9; 100% is the most there can be; a bigger percent is always more; 5% of a whole is the whole ÷ 5; 25% off means $25 off; the discount is the new price; part and whole swapped; part-to-part read as part-to-whole; a percent change is adding a plain number; up then down by the same percent cancels; reverse a discount by adding the percent of the sale price; simple interest grows on itself; "5% up" for a points change; withholding a conclusion the counts already support.

## The game or activity

**(a) Hundred Square, model `hundred-square-v1`.** One 10 × 10 square of one stated whole, with a second square that appears on **Add a second square** (to 200%). Native buttons shade or clear a row (10%) or one square (1%); the passive grid is never 100 tiny touch targets (spec 85's rule). A names panel updates in lockstep: "40 out of 100 · 40/100 · 0.40 · 40%". **Sprout mode** offers All of it · Half of it · None of it · More than all, with no digits. *A made-up model: one little square is one hundredth of the stated whole; the whole can be anything.*

**(b) Three Names Shelf, model `three-names-v1`.** Jars each hold one amount; the child places fraction, decimal and percent labels on the jar they name, with **Show the square** to check on bench (a)'s grid and **Make it simpler** stepping a fraction to simplest form one common factor at a time. The landmark strip (Seedling) and its Keeper's Desk extension sit on the shelf. Nine word cards (percent, fraction, decimal, convert, proportion, ratio, out of, equivalent, simplest form), each with a meaning and a juice-and-water or apple example, become tappable glossary words on every bench. *A made-up model: labels on jars; every label set is authored.*

**(c) Harvest Board, model `harvest-board-v1`.** A bar of the chosen whole cut into ten 10% pieces, with 5% and 1% sub-pieces. Wholes: 20, 40, 60, 200, 360, 400, 500. **Add 10% / 5% / 1%** builds a percent and a running line reads "3 tens and 6 ones make 36% of 400: 144". **Base switch** keeps the percent and changes the whole so the amount visibly changes. A discount readout shows "off" and "to pay" as two separate numbers. **Sprout mode:** two half-full baskets of different sizes. *A made-up model: the orchard's numbers are invented; the bar is the whole.*

**(d) Change Line, model `change-line-v1`.** A price on a strip; each change button (up 10%, down 10%, up 25%, down 20%) draws an arrow tagged "[old] × [multiplier] = [new]". **Undo a change** divides by the same multiplier (the reverse percent made visible). Starts are authored prices in whole cents. A new change is offered and committed only when its exact result is also whole cents; disabled buttons explain "That change makes part of a cent. Try another change." No silent rounding. The six-step limit is a maximum, not a promise that every change is available at every step. Undo reverses the last committed exact step. A future general percent-chain mode needs a separate rounding contract. The cider case runs $2.00 → $2.20 → $1.98. A Keeper's Desk savings strip shows simple interest as four equal $15 steps on $500. *A made-up model: prices and the savings box are invented; each change multiplies the amount at that moment.*

**(e) Claim Desk, model `claim-desk-v1`.** Four Willowbank claim cards ("would flood 40% of the low fields"; "Apple sales up 50%!"; "Wet fields up 5%"; "130% of last year"). Buttons **Find the whole**, **Count it**, **Out of 100** and **Say it fairly** walk each claim to a sentence that names its whole and count, chosen from authored options. Data: orchard 80 fields, 30 low fields, 12 flooded under the Sponge plan; sales 4 → 6 baskets; wet fields 8 → 12 of 80; harvest 400 → 520 baskets. *A made-up model: every claim and count is invented town fiction.*

No timers, scores, streaks, medals or locks. Every bench is open from the first visit.
