# 120: The Keeper of the Ways, the story under the six acts

Status: proposed, 2026-10-23. Writing only: story, structure and the rules a build must keep. Codex owns code, art, film and merge. Child-facing text lives in the companion [120-keeper-of-the-ways-text.md](../content/120-keeper-of-the-ways-text.md). This spec sits under [spec 49](49-six-act-curriculum.md), which keeps ownership of the six acts' teaching purposes; it gives those acts a plot. It keeps the act order and chapter lists from the roadmap's story order and spec 49, and section 15 shows, chapter by chapter, what it keeps and what it adds, along with the Marble topics and Jane conventions it draws on. It builds on the town, seasons and almanac in [spec 33](33-rebuilding-the-town.md) and [its text](33-town-content.md), the [Travelers' Archive](24-travelers-archive.md), the [challenge-band proposal](proposed/challenge-bands.md), the [optional starting flow](114-optional-starting-flow.md) and the [milestone films](../story-milestones-and-films.md). The older-learner destination it names, the Keeper's Desk, is specified in spec 118, drafted alongside this one.

Single device, offline, single player. Every explorer keeps a separate town. Nothing here needs an account, a network call, an age, a grade or a name.

## 1. What is missing

The six acts have purposes (recovery, belonging, exchange, disagreement, authorship, shared agency) and chapter lists. They have no plot. A child playing today meets a storm, a garden, a café, Paris and Bramble's gate, and nothing asks them a question that the next visit might answer. Four problems follow:

1. **No question to carry.** Nothing in Act I points at Act II. A child who finishes the welcome-back supper has no reason to wonder what happens next.
2. **The arc is too long to feel.** Spec 49 spreads six acts across a horizon of about eight years of childhood. A five-year-old cannot feel an arc that ends at thirteen. A 2026-10-21 outside review of the roadmap suggested a repeating seasonal year with the acts as rare large events; nothing has decided between the two.
3. **One story, one level.** Children at different challenge bands share a device. They should be able to talk about the same story while doing different work in it. Today, the story beats that exist are written for the youngest player.
4. **No door for an older learner.** An older explorer sees picture-book entrances. There is no reason in the story for them to be there, and no work in it that matches what they can do.

## 2. The story in one paragraph

On the morning after the storm, Pip and Dot find a waterlogged parcel wedged against their flowerpot. It is addressed **To the Keeper of the Ways, Willowbank, by Willow Brook**, and nobody in Willowbank knows who that is. The pages are stuck together, so they are laid on the bakery windowsill to dry, and one page comes free at the end of each act. The pages tell a slow story: Willow Brook used to wander through a wide marsh called the Sponge; long ago the town drained the Sponge for orchard fields and straightened the brook; floods have come faster since. The pages were written by **Hollis**, an old grey heron who kept the town's flood records and kept its paths open to the towns upstream and down, and who retired to the coast. Willowbank recovers (I), becomes a town of its own (II), makes friends upstream in the town of **Vadoalto** (III), argues honestly about whether to build a higher wall or give the brook back some of its marsh (IV), and lets each explorer chase a question of their own (V). In the last act a long rain comes. Vadoalto sends warning down the river, the town's plan holds, and Hollis comes home to see it. The last page says what the parcel was for: the Keeper of the Ways is whoever keeps the record and keeps the ways open. The explorer has been doing both. A new apprentice arrives with an unfamiliar parcel, and the explorer helps them begin (VI).

## 3. Why this story

- **It grows out of what exists.** The storm, the flowerpot, the brook, the footbridge, the mill's flood boards, the Water Lab, Rain Paths, the weather log, the almanac, the post office and the Paris 1910 flood archive already sit in the game or its accepted specs. The story connects them; it adds one object (the parcel), one offstage character (Hollis), one partner town (Vadoalto) and its correspondent (Nuria).
- **The question is real and has more than one honest answer.** Whether a town should build higher walls, give a river room, or do some of each is a live question in real places. A young child can hold it as "where does the water go?" An older learner can work it with data, sources and trade-offs. Neither group is handed a moral.
- **The mystery pays off with the learner.** The reveal is that the Keeper is a role, and the explorer has been filling it all along by keeping the almanac and the routes. The ending needs no score: it is a recognition of what they did.
- **It never re-breaks the town.** Spec 49 forbids a second disaster. The long rain in Act VI is the payoff of the town's preparation. It damages nothing that the explorer mended.

## 5. The storm parcel

The parcel is the thread a child carries from act to act. It lives in the almanac as a new section, **The Keeper's Pages**.

- **Found:** a short new scene in First Morning, after the storm film and before the three entrances (text in the companion). Explorers who began before this ships see the parcel scene once on their next visit to the garden home, with a one-line framing: "Pip has been keeping something for you."
- **Drying:** the pages sit on the bakery windowsill. One page comes free at the end of each act. Before that, the almanac shows the page as a stuck-together edge with one readable word.
- **Reading lengths:** every page has a short version (one or two sentences, read-aloud available), a longer version, and the original page as Hollis wrote it: water-stained, with some words lost, set at the Keeper's Desk. The original is where an older learner transcribes, reconstructs missing words from context, marks what is uncertain and reads Hollis's numbers.
- **Never a lock:** a page coming free opens nothing. Every destination stays open whether or not a page is dry.

| Page | Comes free | What it holds | The question it leaves |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. The old map | End of Act I (the welcome-back supper) | A hand-drawn map of the valley. Willow Brook wanders through a wide green patch labelled *the Sponge*, where the orchard fields are now. | "There's no marsh there now. Where did it go?" |
| 2. The flood ledger | End of Act II (A Town of Our Own) | Hollis's record of how high the brook rose at the mill's flood post in the years it flooded. In the later years the floods come closer together. A note at the bottom: *Ask upstream.* | "Why would upstream know?" |
| 3. The Ways | End of Act III (Beyond Willow Bridge) | A letter about the Ways: the river path, the bridge road, the high ford, "and the water's ways too, which are where it goes when there is too much of it." Nuria recognises the handwriting. | "Who wrote this, and where are they now?" |
| 4. The year of the fine harvest | Opening of Act IV | Hollis's note: the year the Sponge was drained, the orchard gave its best harvest ever; five years later came the first big flood. | "Did draining the Sponge cause the floods? How would we know?" |
| 5. For the next Keeper | Opening of Act V | Blank pages headed *For the next Keeper*. | "What will I write?" |
| 6. Hollis's letter | End of Act VI | What the parcel was for, and who the Keeper is. | None. The story is complete; play continues. |

Page 4 is designed for Act IV's central contrast between a coincidence in time and a cause. Bramble points out that one flood five years later proves nothing, and she is right; the investigation that follows is what the act teaches.

## 6. Two clocks: the seasons and the acts

The review question was whether the spine should be six linear acts or a repeating seasonal year. This spec uses both, with different jobs.

**The seasons are the rhythm.** The town follows the device calendar, as in spec 33. Each season brings back a festival with its own jobs, and those jobs rotate so that a second year is a different year:

| Season | Festival | What it is for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spring | **Flood Boards and Planting** | Boards in the millrace, seeds in the beds, reading the flood post. |
| Summer | **Market Fair and the Show** | The weekly market at its biggest; the Story Loft's summer show. |
| Autumn | **Harvest Supper** | The café's big gathering; Ember's birthday falls in this season. |
| Winter | **Lantern Night** | Lanterns on the bridge; the almanac is read aloud, which doubles as a recap of the year. |

**Storm Day** is each explorer's own anniversary: the local date of their first visit. When it comes round, a lantern is lit on the footbridge and the almanac offers a one-page "a year since the storm" comparison (weather log this year against last year, places mended, pages dried). It appears for one day, asks for nothing, and is never missed: a later visit simply shows the lantern still lit.

**The acts are the plot.** An act advances by contributions, never by date and never by age. Each act after the first has four chapters. When an explorer has made a story contribution in three of them, the act's ending scene opens; the fourth chapter stays playable. A story contribution is whatever kept, sent or offered outcome the activity's own spec defines. Help, skips inside the activity, a smaller supported plan and work done together all count the same. Act I keeps its existing ending: six served tables at the welcome-back supper.

An act's ending wears the season the explorer reaches it in. A child who reaches the Act II celebration in winter sees it under lanterns.

The festivals keep the town alive between acts. A child who plays for years without pushing the acts sees the seasons turn and the town deepen; a child who plays a lot in one month can reach Act III before the leaves fall. Both are fine.

## 7. The acts

Each act below names the question the child carries, its four chapters with their plot, and what an older learner does there. The teaching purposes and boundaries in spec 49 are unchanged.

### Act I. After the Storm

**Carried question:** What happened, and who is the parcel for?

**Chapters** (as built and as proposed in spec 33): find and feed; deliver and explain; investigate and plant; mend the village. Pip carries the parcel everywhere and asks every neighbor whether they are the Keeper. Nobody is. The miller remembers "an old heron who used to read the flood post" but not their name.

**Ending:** the welcome-back supper. Page 1 comes free on the bakery windowsill while everyone is eating.

**Older learner:** the Keeper's Desk opens in the mended library with spec 118's first case, *Bridge or ford?*, about which crossing to rebuild first.

### Act II. A Town of Our Own

**Carried question:** Where did the Sponge go?

**Chapters:**
1. **The weekly market.** Stalls come back. An orchard keeper sells apples from the fields where the map shows marsh, and is surprised to hear it.
2. **A garden across the seasons.** The garden beds move through the real calendar. The town starts keeping its own records "so next time we'll know," which is the almanac's weather log taking on a purpose.
3. **Ember's birthday.** The café hosts the gathering (the whole-batch recipe work in spec 50 is its kitchen). Bramble arrives with ribbons and admits, when asked, that her grandmother taught her to tie them on the old paths.
4. **The first show.** The Story Loft's first original show for the town. Dot is asked, at the curtain call, whether she will stay. She says she already unpacked.

**Ending:** *A Town of Our Own* (the milestone film). Page 2, the flood ledger, comes free. The courier brings a letter from beyond Willow Bridge: Vadoalto's flood post rose in the same storm, and their apprentice keeper would like to compare notes.

**Older learner:** chart Hollis's ledger; decide what the gaps between floods show and what they cannot show; write the town's first "what we know so far" almanac entry.

### Act III. Beyond Willow Bridge

**Carried question:** What does upstream know?

**Chapters:**
1. **The floating market.** Vadoalto's traders come downstream by boat. Prices, change and exchange run both ways.
2. **The seed exchange.** Vadoalto grows a reed that likes wet ground. Willowbank grows apples. Each wants something the other has.
3. **The route by the high ford.** The courier finds the old path to Vadoalto, marked here and there by faded ribbons. Map scale, timetables and route choice live here.
4. **Letters with Nuria.** A correspondence in English and Spanish, at three lengths, in the existing letter-and-reader pattern. French readers keep their Paris correspondence; this is a second, parallel door.

**Ending:** *Both Ways Across Willow Bridge*. A parcel goes up, a gift comes down. Page 3 comes free, and Nuria recognises Hollis's handwriting: Hollis taught her to read a flood post before they retired to the coast.

**Older learner:** Vadoalto's flood post readings set beside Willowbank's; upstream and downstream timing (water reaches Willowbank some hours after Vadoalto); optional Spanish source reading in the archive's Valencia pack (section 9).

### Act IV. The Town Has Two Stories

**Carried question:** Did we make the floods worse, and what should we do now?

**Chapters:**
1. **The old map and Hollis's account.** Page 4 sets the year of the fine harvest beside the first big flood. Bramble: "Five years apart. My hat and my boots both got wet that year too; that doesn't mean my hat did it." She is right that it proves nothing yet.
2. **A puzzling change at the bottom of the orchard.** One low corner of the orchard is always wet now. Reeds are growing there that nobody planted. The Water Lab and Rain Paths model what happens to rain on a sponge, on packed ground and in a straight channel. Model effects are labelled as model effects.
3. **The newspaper's two explanations, and what other places did.** The Willowbank Gazette prints both stories side by side: "Just a bad storm" and "We made it worse." The Travelers' Archive then offers real floods and real choices (section 9): a flood people depended on, a city that moved its river and made a garden of the old bed, and a country that gave its rivers room.
4. **The proposal for the low fields.** The town meets about the low fields. Three plans are on the table: **the Wall** (a higher flood wall along the brook; the orchard stays as it is), **the Sponge** (let the lowest orchard fields go back to marsh; the orchard keepers lose some trees and get the reed trade with Vadoalto), and **Some of Each** (a lower wall upstream of the mill and a smaller wet meadow at the low corner). Each plan has a real cost and a real benefit. Bramble speaks for the Wall and for the orchard families, and her worries about them are fair.

**Ending:** *The Map We Changed*. The explorer keeps one plan, or a plan with a stated worry. Someone at the meeting changes their mind because of evidence, and says which evidence. The meeting does not need to agree, and the story does not announce a winner. The kept plan belongs to this explorer's town and returns in Act VI.

**Older learner:** the full dossier at the Keeper's Desk: ledger data, model runs, archive sources, the orchard keepers' account and Bramble's objection; a written recommendation with an objection answered and an uncertainty stated. The writing is human-read; nothing grades it.

### Act V. A Question of Your Own

**Carried question:** What will I write on the blank pages?

**Chapters:** spec 49's authored project paths, each started from a page headed *For the next Keeper*: a night garden; a Willowbank newspaper; a sky-watching night; a travelling show to Vadoalto; a new courier route. A child may also continue the flood question (a reed bed survey, a better flood post, a guide to the Sponge for visitors).

**Ending:** *A Question of Our Own*. The explorer's kept project, with its before-version and one stated limit, is bound into the Keeper's Pages. A small project is a whole ending.

**Older learner:** a sustained project at the desk: their own question, a plan, a first version, a response, a revision and an honest limit.

### Act VI. The Opening of the Ways

**Carried question:** Is the town ready, and who is the Keeper?

**Chapters:**
1. **Choose a partner and a purpose.** Willowbank and Vadoalto agree to keep a regular exchange: goods down, news and seeds up.
2. **Compare routes and keep a plan.** Who carries what, how often, on which Way, and what happens when a Way is closed. The explorer keeps a workable exchange plan, or a smaller companion-supported one.
3. **The long rain.** Days of rain upstream. Nuria reads Vadoalto's post and sends the courier down the river ahead of the water. Willowbank has hours to prepare. The explorer's Act IV plan meets the water. Each of the three plans has its own short scene, and each holds with an honest cost (companion text). The bridge, the mill and everything the explorer mended stay whole.
4. **The first journey, and someone new.** The rain passes and the first regular exchange goes up the river under the kept plan, with help available. On the way back, Hollis comes home up the river path to see the brook, and stays for supper.

**Ending:** *The Opening of the Ways*. Page 6, Hollis's letter, explains the parcel. Bramble ties a ribbon on each Way. A new apprentice arrives with an unfamiliar parcel, and Dot gets there first. The explorer's guide stays alongside them as they help the apprentice begin. Play continues: festivals, exchanges, investigations and new parcels. The town is never broken again to make a new beginning.

**Older learner:** plan the exchange with real constraints (routes, timetables, quantities), and write the first page of their own for the next Keeper.

## 8. One scene, many jobs

Children at different bands play the same story. The rule:

1. **Every chapter has one shared scene.** Same event, same characters, same jokes, same page. Reading length and read-aloud vary; the plot does not.
2. **Every chapter offers jobs at more than one depth.** The [challenge-band](proposed/challenge-bands.md) names (Sprout, Seedling, Sapling) describe the task, never the child. A job at the Keeper's Desk is a destination open to everyone, written for the most experienced explorers.
3. **Any job is a whole contribution.** A Sprout job advances the act exactly as much as a desk case. Nobody's story waits for harder work.
4. **Mixed strengths stay possible.** A child can take a Sapling map job and a Sprout reading job in the same chapter.
5. **Siblings can compare notes.** Two explorers on one device have separate towns, but the same scenes. "Which plan did you keep?" is a conversation the game sets up without any shared state.

Example for one chapter, Act IV's "puzzling change at the bottom of the orchard":

| Depth | Job | Built on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sprout | Pour the same rain on a sponge and on a flat stone in the Water Lab model. Which one holds the water? | Water Lab |
| Seedling | Read three short neighbor notes about the wet corner. Sort what each person saw from what they think caused it. | Water Evidence Shelf; Field Notebook |
| Sapling | Run Rain Paths with and without the Sponge for the same storm. Compare how fast water reaches the mill, and say what the model leaves out. | Rain Paths; Fair Test |
| Keeper's Desk | Set the ledger's flood heights against the years after draining. Decide what the pattern supports, what it does not, and what record would settle it. | Spec 118 desk pattern |

The companion text gives a job table for one chapter in every act.

## 9. The archive's flood thread

The Travelers' Archive already holds one real flood, Paris 1910. The story gives the archive a purpose: when the town asks what other places did, the archive answers with real history, clearly separated from Willowbank's fiction. Candidate packs, each needing its own source review before authoring:

| Pack | Why it belongs | Act |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Paris, 1910** (built) | A great city flooded, measured the river, studied the flood and recovered. | I, III |
| **The Nile and the Nilometer** | A flood people depended on: the yearly rise brought the soil for farming, and its height was measured on stone gauges. Too low or too high both meant trouble. It ties to the built Egyptian writing objects. | II, IV |
| **Valencia, 1957, and the Turia gardens** | After a disastrous flood, the city moved its river to a new channel south of the city. Citizens later campaigned to make the dry old riverbed a long park instead of a motorway. A flood that became a garden, with Spanish source reading for older learners. | IV, V |
| **Room for the River, the Netherlands** | A country famous for dikes chose, in many places, to lower floodplains and move dikes back to give rivers room. The Wall and the Sponge as a real national debate. | IV |

Packs must leave out recent floods with many deaths. Facts about wetlands and channels in child text stay modest and checkable: a marsh can hold water and slow it; a straight channel moves water faster; a wall protects what is behind it and can send water elsewhere. None of these settles the town's plan by itself.

## 10. Arriving later

The starting flow in spec 114 offers preferences and a first activity. This spec adds a story arrival, offered once when an explorer has no story progress:

- **Begin with the storm.** Act I, as today.
- **Arrive after the mending.** Act II. The village is shown mended, the parcel already found and page 1 dry. A short recap (companion text) tells what happened before the explorer arrived.
- **Arrive with the letter from upstream.** Act III. The explorer arrives as a newcomer travelling with the courier who carries Nuria's letter. Pip meets them at the bridge with the parcel and pages 1 and 2. This is the natural entrance for an older learner, and the Keeper's Desk is pointed out on arrival.

Arriving later marks earlier acts as "before you came." Their places and activities stay open and fully playable, and nothing in them is recorded as done by this explorer. Earlier pages are readable in the almanac. Act IV and later have no arrival point, because their questions depend on pages the explorer should have time to wonder about.

## 11. Rules the story keeps

- **No locks.** Pages, acts and festivals open scenes. They never open or close a destination.
- **No re-breaking.** The long rain damages nothing the explorer mended. Weather can be wet; the town stays whole.
- **No score for story.** Contributions are kept, sent or offered work under each activity's own rules. The story never reads correctness, speed or help.
- **Fiction and history stay labelled.** Willowbank, Hollis, the Sponge and Vadoalto are fiction. Archive packs are history, with sources. A model run is a model.
- **No moral handed down.** The Wall, the Sponge and Some of Each are all defensible. The story shows each one's cost. Bramble is allowed to be right.
- **Story progress is not learning evidence.** Reaching an act says nothing about what a child knows.
- **No pressure from the calendar.** Missed festivals and days leave nothing undone. Storm Day cannot be missed.

## 12. State sketch for the build

Codex owns the design. The sketch below only fixes the rules from section 6 and from AGENTS.md.

Per explorer, additive: `story:{arrival, acts:{[actId]:{chapters:[chapterId], ended}}, plan, stormDay, seenScenes:[sceneId]}`. `arrival` is one of `storm | mended | letter`. `plan` is one of `wall | sponge | some-of-each` once kept. `stormDay` is the local day of first story visit. Pages are derived from ended acts and never stored. Contributions record ids only. Unknown ids are dropped from reads without rewriting the save; a missing act or chapter reads as not yet reached. Changing the number of chapters in an act later must not reopen or close an ending already reached.

## 13. Open questions for Joshua

1. **Seasons and acts together** (section 6): festivals as the yearly rhythm, acts advanced by contributions. The alternative is acts only.
2. **The marsh twist**: the town's own past choice made the floods worse, investigated in Act IV with no official answer. The alternative is a storm with no human cause and Act IV about a different disagreement.
3. **Vadoalto in Spanish**: the upstream partner town gives Spanish a home in the story, in parallel with Paris for French.
4. **Storm Day** on each explorer's first-visit anniversary.

### Jane: what was studied and what was adopted

Studied: Jane [`1927912`](https://github.com/kentlangley/Jane/tree/1927912b8d9cd4f7df6e7fbe8416521194743060) `LESSONS.md` (quests, review gates, teach-before-boss, finished-quest locking, hook length, coverage), its glossary and its kid prompts for the weather and hazard topics above, 2026-10-23.

Adopted as design conventions:

- **A quest is a hook, a path of stages and a finale.** Each act here has a carried question, four chapters and an ending scene.
- **Short hooks** (Jane caps hooks and intros at about 30 words). The short version of every page and act opening in the companion text keeps to one or two sentences.
- **"You have to do something from the past to make it to the future."** Earlier pages and kept plans come back in later acts (the ledger in IV, the Act IV plan in the long rain), and spec 74's Town Remembers errands can draw on them. Here they never stand as a gate.
- **Teach before the finale.** An act's ending asks nothing the act's own chapters did not offer.
- **A finished quest stays finished, and the quest's story is locked once started.** A reached act ending stays reached, and page text the explorer has read is kept as read, even if the wording is later revised.
- **Visit a new area.** Act II deliberately brings in S7 weather records, a strand with no current references.
- **Tap a word.** Jane's glossary convention fits the Keeper's Pages: *marsh*, *flood post*, *ledger*, *upstream*.

Not adopted: medals, bosses, hearts, points, one-quest-at-a-time queuing, age and grade settings, the nightly author and Leitner gates. Jane's kid prompt for Designing for Weather Hazards has a single right answer ("Which design best protects a house from floods?"); Act IV's plan choice deliberately keeps three defensible answers, while factual jobs inside the act can still have right answers. No Jane code, prose or assets are incorporated, and Jane's license layers are untouched.
