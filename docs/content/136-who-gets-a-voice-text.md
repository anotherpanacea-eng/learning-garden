## The story scene (Act IV, before the town meeting)

*A story from Willowbank.*

**Short:**
> The night before the meeting, the librarian opens an archive drawer. "A real meeting, long ago." Bramble asks who got to speak. Dot asks if newcomers count.

**Longer:**
> The night before the town meeting, the librarian sets a long, shallow drawer on the library table. "From the Travelers' Archive," they say. "A real meeting place, very long ago. Athens." Inside are pictures of broken pottery with names scratched on them, and a stone slab full of slots.
>
> Pip leans in. "Did everyone get a say?"
>
> "The men who were citizens did," says the librarian.
>
> Bramble folds her arms. "So most people didn't. Not the women. Not the people who were enslaved. Not people from somewhere else."
>
> "That's right," says the librarian. "Athens tried something new, and it left most people out. Both are true."
>
> Dot straightens her hat. "I'm from somewhere else. Do newcomers count at our meeting?"
>
> Everyone looks at the librarian. "Everyone who lives in Willowbank can speak tomorrow," they say. "You too, Dot."
>
> Bramble nods. "Good. Then I'm going first."

**Keeper's note:**
> Athens' assembly met on a hill called the Pnyx, and citizens decided by a show of hands. Only a small share of the people living there could take part. Historians' estimates of that share differ, so we give a range. Willowbank's meeting is a story; Athens is real history.

**Possible ending line (spec 120, Act IV, after the drawer is kept):** the librarian: "I thought a meeting was fair if anyone could come. Athens let some in and kept most out. Now I ask who's missing."

## Teach cards

**Teach 1. Counting hands** *(A true thing from history)*
> In Athens' assembly, citizens voted by raising their hands. The side with more hands won.

Worked example, one step per tap:
1. Seven hands go up for. Four go up against.
2. Count each side: 7 and 4.
3. 7 is more than 4.
4. The "for" side wins.

**Teach 2. What survives, and what it means** *(A true thing from history)*
> Some things from the past survive. What we say they mean is our interpretation. Keep the two apart.

Worked example:
1. Here is a broken piece of pot with a name scratched on it.
2. "It has a name scratched on it." We can see that. It survives.
3. "People wanted him gone." That's what we think it means.
4. Both can be useful. Only one is what survives.

**Teach 3. Who is in the room** *(A made-up model)*
> Change who may vote, and the same question can get a different answer.

Worked example:
1. Six people choose: picnic or museum.
2. The three in red hats want a picnic. The three in blue hats want the museum.
3. If only red hats vote: picnic.
4. If everyone votes: a tie. The rule changed the result.

## Station 2. The model kleroterion

*A made-up model.*

**Card:** In Athens, a stone machine helped choose jurors by lot. This is a simple model.

**How the real one worked (Details, A true thing from history):**
> Each juror had a bronze name ticket. Tickets went into slots, one column for each tribe. Black and white balls dropped from a tube beside the slab. A row next to a white ball served. Every row had one man from each tribe, so every tribe got the same number. Writers today still argue about some small details.

**Steps:**
1. Put one ticket in each slot. Or tap **Fill for me**.
2. Tap the tube to drop a bead.
3. White: this row serves. Black: this row goes home.
4. Keep going to the bottom row.

**After 10 beads:** Four rows serve. That's {n} people: 4 from each tribe.
**Draw again button:** Shuffle the beads and draw again
**After a second draw:** Different rows this time. Still 4 from each tribe. Why?
**Answer (tap to show):** Each row has one ticket from every tribe. So any row that serves is fair to all five.

**Model line (always shown):** A simplified model. The real machine had more slots and tribes, and some of its details are still argued about.

## Readings

### Theater in Athens *(A true thing from history)*

**Short:** In Athens, plays were part of festivals for the god Dionysus. All the actors were men, in masks.

**Longer:** Every spring, Athens held a big festival for the god Dionysus. Writers put on plays to win a prize: sad, serious plays called tragedies, and funny ones called comedies. All the actors were men. They wore masks, so one actor could play several parts, and people far back could see the faces. The stone theater held thousands, perhaps 14,000 to 17,000. Historians still argue about whether women came to watch.

### The first Olympics *(A true thing from history)*

**Short:** The Olympic Games were held at Olympia, for the god Zeus. The traditional first year is 776 BCE.

**Longer:** The Games were held at Olympia, a holy place for Zeus. The traditional date for the first Games is 776 BCE. Historians call it traditional because nobody can check it. The first event we know of was a short running race, one length of the stadium. Only free Greek men could compete. Girls ran in a separate festival for the goddess Hera. Kyniska, a woman from Sparta, is listed as a winner because her horses won a chariot race. In 1896, the first modern Olympics were held in Athens, inspired by the ancient Games.

### Myths, and a city called Troy *(A story people told, and a true thing from history)*

**Short:** Greeks told stories about gods on Mount Olympus. A poem tells of a war at Troy. Archaeologists found a real city.

**Longer:** Greeks told stories about gods like Zeus and Athena, who lived on Mount Olympus, a real mountain. These are myths: stories people told and loved. A long poem called the *Iliad* tells of a war at a city called Troy. It was probably made in the 700s or 600s BCE. In the 1870s, Heinrich Schliemann dug at a hill called Hisarlik, in Turkey. He found a city built again and again, in many layers. Most experts think it is Troy. Was there a war like the poem's? People still argue. A real city doesn't make every part of a poem true.

## Problem sets

Each item: prompt, answer, the misconception each wrong choice tests, hint, explanation, and per-choice feedback. "I'm stuck" always offers: the hint, then the worked example, then **Skip for now**.

### Set A. Counting hands

**A1 · Sprout · 3-choice** *(A true thing from history)*
**Prompt:** Seven hands go up for. Four go up against. Who wins?
- **For** ✓ Feedback: Yes. 7 hands is more than 4.
- **Against** · misconception: *the smaller group wins*. Feedback: Against had 4. For had 7. More hands wins.
- **It's a tie** · misconception: *any vote with two sides is even*. Feedback: A tie needs equal hands. 7 and 4 aren't equal.
**Hint:** Count each side. Which number is bigger?
**Explanation:** In the assembly, the side with more hands won. Seven is more than four.
**Stuck example:** 3 for, 1 against. Count: 3 and 1. 3 is bigger, so "for" wins. Now count your two sides.

**A2 · Seedling · number**
**Prompt:** A jury has 501 people. How many votes make a majority?
**Answer:** 251.
**Steps:** Half of 501 is 250 and a half. A majority is more than half. The next whole number is 251.
**Hint:** A majority means more than half.
**Explanation:** 251 is more than half of 501. 250 would be less than half.
**Stuck example:** A group of 5. Half is 2 and a half. More than half: 3. Now halve 501.
**Feedback, 250:** 250 is just under half of 501. One more makes a majority.

**A3 · Seedling · 3-choice**
**Prompt:** Why might a jury have 501 people instead of 500?
- **There were 501 tribes in Athens.** · misconception: *mixing up the numbers*. Feedback: Athens had 10 tribes. Try thinking about ties.
- **With 501 voting, the votes can't split evenly.** ✓ Feedback: Yes. 500 could split 250 and 250. 501 can't.
- **Bigger juries always decide correctly.** · misconception: *more voters means always right*. Feedback: Big juries can still be wrong. Think about an even split.
**Hint:** Can 500 split into two equal halves? Can 501?
**Explanation:** An odd number of voters can't tie if everyone votes. Athens often used odd-numbered juries.
**Stuck example:** 4 friends vote: 2 and 2, a tie. 5 friends: 3 and 2, no tie.

**A4 · Sapling · number**
**Prompt:** 6,000 people vote. 3,100 vote for. How many vote against?
**Answer:** 2,900 (if everyone present votes one way or the other).
**Steps:** 6,000 − 3,100 = 2,900. 3,100 is more, so "for" wins.
**Hint:** Take the "for" votes away from everyone voting.
**Explanation:** The two sides add up to everyone who voted: 3,100 + 2,900 = 6,000.
**Stuck example:** 10 vote, 6 for. 10 − 6 = 4 against.

**A5 · Sapling · 3-choice**
**Prompt:** 3,100 of 6,000 voters said yes. Is that most citizens?
- **Yes, 3,100 is more than half.** · misconception: *a majority of those present is a majority of everyone*. Feedback: More than half of 6,000. There were many more citizens than 6,000.
- **We can't say anything at all.** · misconception (underclaim): *uncertainty means no answer*. Feedback: We can say something: it's most of the people who were there.
- **No, it's a majority of those present.** ✓ Feedback: Yes. Most citizens weren't there, so it's a majority of those present.
**Hint:** Were all the citizens at the meeting?
**Explanation:** The meeting place held about 6,000, by one estimate. There were many more citizens. A majority there is a majority of those present.
**Stuck example:** 7 club members come to a meeting, and 4 vote for a picnic. The club has 25. Is 4 most of the club?

**A6 · Sapling · tally under two readings** *(A true thing from history: the two readings. A made-up model: the tally.)*
**Card:** Two old writers disagree. Plutarch: an ostracism counts if at least 6,000 sherds are cast in all. Philochorus: at least 6,000 must name the same man.
**Made-up tally:** Name 1: 2,500. Name 2: 1,900. Name 3: 1,200. Other names: 800.
**Prompt 1:** How many sherds in all?
**Answer:** 6,400. Steps: 2,500 + 1,900 + 1,200 + 800 = 6,400.
**Prompt 2:** Does it count under Plutarch's rule?
**Answer:** Yes: 6,400 is at least 6,000. Name 1 has the most, so he leaves.
**Prompt 3:** Does it count under Philochorus's rule?
**Answer:** No: the top name has 2,500, fewer than 6,000.
**Hint:** Plutarch counts every sherd. Philochorus counts only the top name.
**Explanation:** The same sherds give different results under the two readings. That's why historians care which writer was right.
**Stuck example:** 10 sherds: 4 say Ana, 6 say Bo. "At least 8 in all"? Yes. "At least 8 for one name"? No.

**A7 · Sapling · tally**
**Made-up tally:** Name 1: 6,200. Other names: 300.
**Prompt:** Does this ostracism count under both writers' rules?
**Answer:** Yes. 6,500 in all is at least 6,000, and Name 1 alone has at least 6,000.
**Hint:** Check each rule, one at a time.
**Explanation:** When one name gets a huge vote, the two readings agree. They disagree only on closer tallies.
**Stuck example:** 9 of 10 sherds say Bo. "At least 8 in all"? Yes. "At least 8 for Bo"? Yes.

### Set B. Who could vote?

*A true thing from history: these are round numbers inside historians' estimates for the 300s BCE. Details: About the numbers.*

**B1 · Seedling · number**
**Prompt:** Say 30,000 of 300,000 people could vote. What fraction is that?
**Answer:** 1/10 (1 in 10).
**Steps:** 30,000 ÷ 300,000. Both end in 0,000: 3 ÷ 30. That's 1/10.
**Hint:** How many 30,000s fit into 300,000?
**Explanation:** Ten groups of 30,000 make 300,000, so one group is 1/10.
**Stuck example:** 3 of 30 children wear hats. 30 ÷ 3 = 10, so 1 in 10.

**B2 · Sapling · number, both ends**
**Card:** Our practice ranges: citizen men 20,000 to 30,000. Everyone in Attica 150,000 to 300,000.
**Prompt:** What is the smallest share that could vote? The largest?
**Answer:** Smallest 1/15 (about 7 in 100). Largest 1/5 (20 in 100).
**Steps:**
1. Smallest share: fewest voters, most people. 20,000 ÷ 300,000 = 1/15.
2. Largest share: most voters, fewest people. 30,000 ÷ 150,000 = 1/5.
3. So the share was somewhere between 1/15 and 1/5.
**Hint:** For the smallest share, use the fewest voters and the most people.
**Explanation:** With ranges, the answer is a range too. Some pairings are less likely. If our ranges are right, the true share sits inside.
**Stuck example:** 2 or 3 voters out of 10 or 20 people. Smallest: 2/20 = 1/10. Largest: 3/10.
**Feedback, 1/10 only:** That uses one pair of numbers. Find the smallest and the largest.

**B3 · Sapling · number, both ends**
**Prompt:** 6,000 came to the assembly. What share of 20,000 to 30,000 citizens?
**Answer:** Between 1/5 (of 30,000) and 3/10 (of 20,000).
**Steps:** 6,000 ÷ 30,000 = 1/5. 6,000 ÷ 20,000 = 3/10.
**Hint:** Work it out twice, once for each end of the range.
**Explanation:** Even a full meeting was a fraction of the citizens. Fewer citizens makes the same crowd a bigger share.
**Stuck example:** 2 came, out of 10 or 20. 2/10 = 1/5. 2/20 = 1/10.

**B4 · Sapling · 3-choice**
**Prompt:** Why do we give a range instead of one number?
- **Historians forgot to add it up.** · misconception: *the numbers are known exactly*. Feedback: There's no full count to add. The records don't survive.
- **Any guess is as good as any other.** · misconception: *uncertainty means anything goes*. Feedback: Estimates use evidence. Some guesses fit it much better than others.
- **No full count survives, so historians estimate.** ✓ Feedback: Yes. Different evidence leads to different estimates, so we show the range.
**Hint:** Did anyone count everyone, and keep the list?
**Explanation:** Historians use clues like the council's size and one ancient count. Clues point to a range.
**Stuck example:** You guess beans in a jar: "between 80 and 120". You can't count, but you're not guessing wildly.

**B5 · Keeper's Desk · say why (a grown-up reads it)**
**Prompt:** Is the voting share of adults bigger than of everyone? Why?
**Answer to look for:** Bigger, because "everyone" includes children, and no children voted. Taking them out makes the total smaller and the share bigger.
**Hint:** Who is in "everyone" but not in "adults"?
**Explanation:** Some books say "about 30 in 100 adults" and others give a smaller share of everyone. Both can be honest: they divide by different groups.

### Set C. Chance and the lot

**C1 · Seedling · number** *(A true thing from history)*
**Prompt:** 10 tribes each send 50 men to the council. How many members?
**Answer:** 500.
**Steps:** 10 × 50 = 500.
**Hint:** Ten groups of fifty.
**Explanation:** That's why it was called the council of 500.
**Stuck example:** 3 teams of 5 players: 3 × 5 = 15.

**C2 · Sapling · number** *(A made-up model: the 1,000 is our number)*
**Prompt:** A tribe has 1,000 men who can serve, and 50 seats. Your chance?
**Answer:** 50 in 1,000, which is 1 in 20.
**Steps:** 50/1,000. Divide both by 50: 1/20.
**Hint:** How many seats out of how many men?
**Explanation:** Every eligible man had the same chance. The real number of eligible men was different, and changed over time.
**Stuck example:** 2 prizes, 10 names in a hat: 2 in 10, which is 1 in 5.

**C3 · Sapling · number** *(A made-up model)*
**Prompt:** The model tube has 10 beads: 4 white. What's a row's chance to serve?
**Answer:** 4 in 10, which is 2 in 5.
**Steps:** 4 white out of 10 beads. 4/10 = 2/5.
**Hint:** Count the white beads. Count all the beads.
**Explanation:** Each row gets one bead, and 4 of the 10 beads mean "serve".
**Stuck example:** 1 red marble and 3 blue. Chance of red: 1 in 4.

**C4 · Seedling · 3-choice**
**Prompt:** Why might Athens choose some people by lot?
- **It's hard to bribe a draw.** ✓ Feedback: Yes. Nobody knew who would be picked, so it was hard to fix.
- **To pick the best speakers.** · misconception: *lot is the same as an election*. Feedback: Picking the best is what voting for someone does. A draw can't tell who speaks best.
- **So only rich men would serve.** · misconception: *lot favors the powerful*. Feedback: A draw gives every eligible man the same chance, rich or poor.
**Hint:** Could anyone know ahead of time who a draw would pick?
**Explanation:** A fair draw gives each eligible person the same chance, and nobody can plan the result. Athens still voted for some officials, like its generals.
**Stuck example:** A club draws names for who feeds the fish. Can anyone make sure it's them?

### Set D. Order and evidence

**D1 · Sprout · order the cards** *(A true thing from history)*
**Prompt:** Put these in order. Oldest first.
**Cards:** The first Olympic Games, traditional date 776 BCE · Athens changes how it is run, about 508 BCE · The first ostracism we know of, about 487 BCE
**Answer:** 776 BCE, then 508 BCE, then 487 BCE.
**Misconception:** *with BCE, a bigger number is later*. Feedback if reversed: With BCE, years count down. A bigger number is longer ago.
**Hint:** BCE years count down toward year 1. Which number is biggest?
**Explanation:** BCE means before the Common Era. 776 BCE is longer ago than 508 BCE.
**Stuck example:** 10 BCE and 5 BCE. Counting down: 10, 9, 8, 7, 6, 5. So 10 BCE came first.

**D2 · Seedling · number**
**Prompt:** How many years from 508 BCE to 487 BCE?
**Answer:** 21.
**Steps:** Both are BCE, so subtract: 508 − 487 = 21.
**Hint:** Both dates are BCE. Take the smaller from the bigger.
**Explanation:** About 21 years after the new tribes, the first ostracism we know of took place.
**Stuck example:** From 10 BCE to 4 BCE: 10 − 4 = 6 years.

**D3 · Seedling · sort: evidence or interpretation**

**D4 · Seedling · true, or not from this source**

**Prompt:** Does the card say this? Tap **The card says it** or **Not from this card**.

**D5 · Seedling · 3-choice**
**Prompt:** 191 sherds in one well name Themistokles, in a few handwritings. What does that show?
- **Themistokles cheated.** · misconception: *evidence proves a motive*. Feedback: The sherds name him. They don't say he did anything.
- **A few people wrote many of them.** ✓ Feedback: Yes. That's what the handwriting suggests. Why they did it is a further question.
- **Nothing. They're just broken pots.** · misconception (underclaim): *objects can't tell us anything*. Feedback: Broken pots can tell us a lot. Look at the handwriting.
**Hint:** Stick to what you could see if you looked at them.
**Explanation:** Few hands, many sherds. Why? That's a question of interpretation, and the sherds alone can't answer it.
**Stuck example:** Ten birthday cards all in one person's handwriting. Who probably wrote them?

**D6 · Sapling · 3-choice**
**Prompt:** Who wrote the *Constitution of the Athenians*?
- **Aristotle or one of his students.** ✓ Feedback: Yes. Historians still argue about which.
- **Someone who watched Cleisthenes' changes.** · misconception: *a later writer was an eyewitness*. Feedback: It was written well over 100 years after Cleisthenes.
- **An Athenian woman who voted.** · misconception: *women voted in Athens*. Feedback: Women couldn't vote in Athens. The writer was Aristotle or a student.
**Hint:** Look at the source card from D4.
**Explanation:** It was written in the 320s BCE. The main copy we have is on papyrus, found in Egypt and first printed in 1891.

**D7 · Sapling · 3-choice** *(A story people told, and a true thing from history)*
**Prompt:** Archaeologists found a city at Hisarlik. What does that show?
- **Every part of the *Iliad* happened.** · misconception: *a real place proves the whole story*. Feedback: A real city doesn't prove the poem's war, or its gods.
- **The *Iliad* has nothing to do with real places.** · misconception (underclaim). Feedback: The poem may remember a real place. Most experts think Hisarlik is Troy.
- **A real city stood there, rebuilt many times.** ✓ Feedback: Yes. That's what the digging shows. The war is still argued about.
**Hint:** What did the digging find? A city, or a war?
**Explanation:** Archaeology found a city with many layers. Whether a war like the poem's happened is a separate question.

### Readings

**R1 · Sprout · 3-choice** *(Theater)*
**Prompt:** Who acted in plays in old Athens?
- **Men, wearing masks.** ✓ Feedback: Yes. All the actors were men, and they wore masks.
- **The gods themselves.** · misconception: *myth and history mixed*. Feedback: Plays were for the god Dionysus. People acted them.
- **Anyone who wanted to.** · misconception: *everyone could take part*. Feedback: Only men acted on the stage.
**Hint:** Look at the reading again. Who wore the masks?
**Explanation:** All actors were men. Masks helped one actor play several parts.

**R2 · Seedling · 3-choice** *(Olympics)*
**Prompt:** Why do we call 776 BCE a traditional date?
- **A ticket from the first Games shows it.** · misconception: *every date has a document*. Feedback: No such ticket survives. The date was handed down.
- **It's a handed-down date nobody can check.** ✓ Feedback: Yes. It's the date Greeks passed down. Historians can't prove it.
- **It means nothing at all.** · misconception (underclaim). Feedback: It's useful. It's the date Greeks handed down. It's just hard to check.
**Hint:** What does the reading say about checking it?
**Explanation:** "Traditional" means handed down. The Games probably began in the 700s BCE or a little earlier.

### Keeper's Desk prompts (human-read, never graded)

*At the desk, Wren Halloway sets out three cards. A story from Willowbank; the questions are about real history.*

**K1. Whose voices are missing?**
> Pick one source from the drawer.
> Whose voice is missing from it?
> How could we ever find out what they thought?
*For the grown-up:* look for a named group (women, enslaved people, metics, poorer men who couldn't attend) and an honest limit: we often can't know. Gently redirect any invented "quotation" from a real person to "what we'd want to find out".

**K2. Was Athens a democracy?**
> Some call Athens the birthplace of democracy.
> Make the best case for that.
> Now make the best case against.
*For the grown-up:* a good answer uses evidence on both sides (citizens decided directly; most residents were excluded) and may land anywhere.

**K3. Two writers, one rule**
> Plutarch and Philochorus disagree about the 6,000 rule.
> Why might two writers disagree?
> What evidence could settle it?
*For the grown-up:* reasons might include copying mistakes, different times, or different rules for different votes. Settling evidence might be a record of a full tally, if one were ever found.

## Key terms

| Word | What it means here |
| --- | --- |
| **democracy** | Rule by the people. From Greek words for "people" and "power". |
| **assembly** | The big meeting where Athenian citizens spoke and voted. In Greek, *ekklesia*. |
| **Pnyx** | The hill in Athens where the assembly met. |
| **citizen** | Someone who belongs to a city, with its rights. In Athens, only men citizens could vote. |
| **council of 500** | 500 citizen men, chosen by lot each year, who prepared the assembly's business. |
| **by lot** | Chosen by a fair draw, like names from a hat. |
| **election** | Choosing someone by voting for them. |
| **kleroterion** | A stone machine for choosing by lot. |
| **ostracism** | A vote to send one man away from Athens for 10 years. |
| **ostrakon** (plural **ostraka**) | A broken piece of pottery used as a voting slip. |
| **metic** | A free person from somewhere else who lived in Athens. |
| **jury** | A group of citizens who decided a court case. |
| **majority** | More than half. |
| **tie** | The same number on each side. |
| **quorum** | The smallest number needed for a vote to count. |
| **direct democracy** | The people vote on decisions themselves. |
| **representative democracy** | The people elect others to decide for them. |
| **evidence** | Things that survive from the past: objects, writing, places. |
| **interpretation** | What we think the evidence means. |
| **myth** | A story people told, often about gods or heroes. |
| **BCE** | Before the Common Era. BCE years count down toward year 1. |
| **estimate** | A careful guess using evidence. |
| **range** | From the smallest likely number to the biggest. |

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** how the Athenian assembly, council, jury courts, lot and ostracism worked; who was excluded (women, enslaved people, metics); the difference between surviving evidence and interpretation; that two ancient writers can disagree (the 6,000 quorum); that rules about who votes change outcomes; how to work with estimated ranges; and that myths are stories people told, different from archaeological evidence. Short readings touch Greek theater and the Olympic Games.

**What it does not claim:** an exact population of Athens or an exact share who could vote; that Athens was the first democracy anywhere; that Athens was a model or a villain; that the Trojan War happened or did not; details of the kleroterion that sources dispute. It does not teach Sparta, Rome, or the daily lives of enslaved people, which need their own reviewed sources. Thistle Ford and its numbers are made up, and contain no enslaved people on purpose.

**Sources in brief:** Encyclopaedia Britannica (ecclesia, boule, Cleisthenes, ostracism, theater, Olympic Games, Troy); the American School of Classical Studies at Athens (Agora excavations, ostraka, kleroterion); the Kerameikos ostraka publication; the International Olympic Committee; UNESCO World Heritage (Troy); scholarly reviews of the population and quorum debates. Full list in the source audit.

**Talk about it:** Willowbank's meeting lets everyone who lives there speak. Which rule from Thistle Ford is closest? Is there anyone it still leaves out?

**Off-screen (optional, records nothing):** With a grown-up and anyone nearby, hold a show-of-hands vote on something small, like which story to read. Then try a different rule: only people wearing socks vote. Did the result change? Talk about which rule felt fair.
