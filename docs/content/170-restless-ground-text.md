## 2. Story scenes

All scenes are **A story from Willowbank.**

### Scene 1. The shivers column *(Act I, chapter 3)*

**Hook:** Wren's storm-damp town ledger has a column in an old, unknown hand: *Ground shivers*.

**Short:**
> Wren Halloway reads the column aloud. "Cups rattled at the café. Felt at the mill, not at the ford." She looks up. "How do you measure a shake?"

**Longer:**
> Wren's old town ledger is drying at the mill. Most of it is flood heights. But down one edge runs a narrow column in someone's careful old hand: *Ground shivers*.
>
> Wren Halloway reads it out. "Spring. Cups rattled on the café shelf. Felt at the mill, not at the ford. Autumn: nothing felt, but the bell swung once."
>
> "The ground shivered and nobody fell over?" asks Pip. "Then it was a very polite earthquake."
>
> "Most of them are," says Wren. "Small, and soon over. Whoever kept this wrote down what people *felt*. But feelings are different at the mill and the ford."
>
> {guide} taps the column. "So how would you measure the shake itself? The same way, everywhere?"
>
> Wren smiles. "There is a weight on a spring at the Watch Table. Ask it."

**Keeper's note:** The old ledger recorded *intensity*: how strongly a place felt the shaking. Scientists also measure *magnitude*: one number for the size of the earthquake itself. You need an instrument for that, and it does not care where you stand.

### Scene 2. Nuria's market hall *(Act III, chapter 4)*

**Hook:** Nuria's letter has a drawing in it: a tall frame with crossed timbers, like a giant letter X.

**Short:**
> "We are building a market hall," Nuria writes. "Here the ground sometimes shakes, so there are rules for building. Why all these crosses?"

**Longer:**
> Dot hands over a fat letter from Vadoalto. Inside is a drawing of a wooden frame, every wall crossed with timbers.
>
> "*Dear Willowbank,*" reads {guide}. "*We are building a new market hall. The builders follow the rules for places where the ground can shake. They say the crosses matter most. Can you find out why? Your friend, Nuria.*"
>
> Bramble's magpie lands on the drawing. "Easy," says Bramble. "Build it so strong that no earthquake can touch it. Done."
>
> "Nobody can stop an earthquake," says {guide}. "But maybe you can help a building stand up better when one comes."
>
> Pip studies the X's. "They look like the crust on the baker's pies. Is that a clue?"
>
> "It might be," says {guide}. "Let's try it on the Shake Table."

**Keeper's note:** Engineers cannot make a building that nothing can harm. They make harm smaller: frames that keep their shape, feet that soak up the shaking, and plans for people. Every choice costs something.

### Scene 3. Ember's fire mountain *(Act V, the Gazette page)*

**Hook:** The librarian wants a *Gazette* page called "How We Watch the Ground." Ember wants it to be about fire.

**Short:**
> "A volcano is a mountain full of fire," says Ember. "Like a dragon's belly." The baker sets out a jug of honey and a jug of water.

**Longer:**
> The librarian spreads blank pages across the café table. "This week's *Gazette*: how people watch the ground. Who has an idea?"
>
> "Volcanoes!" says Ember, puffing a tiny smoke ring. "A volcano is a mountain full of fire. Like a dragon's belly, only bigger."
>
> The baker comes out with two jugs. He pours honey onto one plate and water onto another. The water runs to the edge at once. The honey piles up slowly in a heap.
>
> "Some melted rock is runny, like the water," he says. "Some is thick and sticky, like the honey. It isn't fire in either."
>
> Ember looks at the heap of honey. "So what makes a volcano go *boom*?"
>
> "Let's find out at the Watch Table," says {guide}. "Then we'll write it up properly."

**Keeper's note:** Lava glows because it is very hot. It is melted rock, not fire. How runny the magma is, and how much gas is trapped in it, shape how a volcano erupts and what it looks like afterwards.

## 3. Teach cards

Each card is one idea (30 words or fewer), then a worked example revealed one step per tap. All are **A true thing from science**.

**Recap card. From the Rock Bench** *(optional)*
> The Earth's outer shell is cracked into huge plates that move a few centimeters a year. When rock under strain suddenly slips along a crack, the ground shakes.

**Card 1. Inside a volcano**
> Melted rock underground is magma. When it comes out, we call it lava. Same rock, new name. It rises from the magma chamber, up the vent, out the crater.

Worked example: *Follow one blob.*
1. Deep down, in the magma chamber: it is magma.
2. It pushes up the vent: still magma.
3. It spills out of the crater: now it is lava. Same rock.

**Card 2. Runny or sticky**
> Runny magma lets gas out gently, so lava flows far. Sticky magma traps gas until it bursts out, throwing ash. The volcano's shape is a clue to which.

Worked example: *Why is Mauna Loa wide and low?*
1. Its lava is runny.
2. Runny lava flows a long way before it cools.
3. Layer after layer, it builds a wide, gentle shield.

**Card 3. Plate edges**
> Plates move apart, push together or slide past. Apart makes new crust. Together makes mountains or volcanoes. Sliding makes earthquakes but usually no volcanoes.

Worked example: *How did the Pyrenees rise?*
1. Two plates moved toward each other: Iberia and Europe.
2. Continental crust is too light to sink far, so it crumpled.
3. The crumpled crust rose into mountains. That is "together."

**Card 4. The seismometer**
> A seismometer has a heavy weight that stays nearly still while the ground shakes. The difference is drawn as a wiggly line called a seismogram.

Worked example: *Why does the weight stay still?*
1. The frame is fixed to the ground, so it shakes.
2. The heavy weight hangs loose and is slow to move.
3. The pen draws how far the ground moved past it.

**Card 5. Magnitude and intensity**
> Magnitude is one number for an earthquake's size. Each step up means ten times taller wiggles. Intensity is how strongly one place feels it.

Worked example: *Magnitude 4 against magnitude 3.*
1. One step up.
2. One step means wiggles ten times taller on the record.
3. So a 4 is not "a bit more." It is much bigger.

**Card 6. Watching a volcano**
> Rising magma makes small earthquakes, swells the ground and lets out gas. Scientists watch all three. One sign alone is not proof. Several together raise concern.

Worked example: *The ground rose. Is an eruption coming?*
1. Check the rain gauge: rain can tilt the ground too.
2. Check the quakes and the gas.
3. Only one sign? Watch closely. All three? Raise the alert.

**Card 7. Tsunamis**
> A tsunami is a series of long waves pushed up when the sea floor suddenly moves. Out at sea it is low and fast. Near shore it grows.

Worked example: *Why don't ships notice it?*
1. In deep water the wave is only a little higher than normal.
2. But it is very long and very fast.
3. Near shore it slows down, and the water piles up.

**Card 8. Helping buildings stand**
> Triangles keep their shape, so cross braces stop a frame leaning over. Rubber feet let less shaking reach the building. A wide base is harder to tip.

Worked example: *Push a square frame, then a braced one.*
1. The square leans into a squashed shape.
2. Add a cross brace: now it is two triangles.
3. Triangles can't squash, so the braced frame stays square.

**Card 9. No stop button**
> Nobody can stop an earthquake, eruption or tsunami. People make the harm smaller: strong buildings, warning sensors, practice and plans, and careful choices about where to build.

Worked example: *Two ideas for Vadoalto's hall.*
1. Stronger walls: protects the hall, costs a lot.
2. Practice and plans: costs little, helps people, not the walls.
3. Which is best? It depends what matters most.

## 4. True-thing cards

All **A true thing from science**.

**Mauna Loa and Vesuvius.** Mauna Loa in Hawai'i is a shield volcano built by runny lava, one of Earth's largest active volcanoes. Vesuvius in Italy is a steep stratovolcano of ash and lava layers. *(Pompeii: see the Travelers' Archive.)*

**The oldest volcano watchers.** The Vesuvius Observatory was founded in 1841. It is the oldest volcano observatory in the world. Today its scientists watch Vesuvius and other Italian volcanoes day and night.

**Garrotxa is watched too.** Near Olot, Catalonia, are about forty small volcanic cones. They sit off any plate edge, where stretched crust cracked. A seismometer has listened since 2021, hearing little so far.

**A brand-new island.** In 1963 an undersea eruption off Iceland began building an island. Surtsey grew until 1967. The first plants were found in 1965. Scientists still study how life arrives.

**A sensor on the sea floor.** DART tsunami stations put a pressure sensor on the deep sea floor. It notices one centimeter of extra water and sends word by buoy and satellite.

**A giant swinging ball.** The tall tower Taipei 101 has a steel ball of 660 tonnes hanging inside, near the top. It swings against the sway, mostly from wind.

### Set A. Inside and Out

**A1** · Sprout · 3-choice · *guided*
**Prompt:** What do we call melted rock under the ground?
**Steps shown:** 1. Find the chamber. 2. Is it under or above the ground? 3. Under means magma.
1. Magma **(right)**
2. Lava *(misconception: magma and lava are named the same way)*
3. Hot fire *(misconception: volcanoes are full of fire)*
**Feedback 1:** Yes. Under the ground, melted rock is magma.
**Feedback 2:** Close. It becomes lava once it is out in the air.
**Feedback 3:** It glows because it is hot, but it is melted rock.
**Hint:** Is the rock under the ground or out in the air?
**Explanation:** Melted rock is called magma while it is underground.

**A2** · Sprout · 3-choice
**Prompt:** The blob spills out of the crater. What is it now?
1. Still magma, because it is the same rock *(misconception: the name never changes)*
2. Fire, now that it meets air *(misconception: lava is fire)*
3. Lava, the same rock out in the air **(right)**
**Feedback 1:** Same rock, yes. But outside, its name changes to lava.
**Feedback 2:** No flames here. It is melted rock glowing with heat.
**Feedback 3:** Yes. Same rock, new name, because of where it is.
**Hint:** What name do we use once it is outside?
**Explanation:** Magma and lava are the same melted rock. Only the place changes the name.

**A3** · Seedling · order the cards
**Prompt:** Put the rock's journey in order.
**Cards:** Out of the crater · Up the vent · In the magma chamber · Cooling on the slope
**Answer:** In the magma chamber → Up the vent → Out of the crater → Cooling on the slope
**Misconception tested:** the crater is the start.
**Hint:** Where does the magma wait first?
**Explanation:** Magma gathers in a chamber, rises up the vent, leaves at the crater and cools on the slope.

**A4** · Seedling · 3-choice
**Prompt:** Runny magma, little gas. What happens?
1. A huge blast of ash and rock *(misconception: all eruptions explode)*
2. Lava pours out and spreads far **(right)**
3. Nothing, because runny magma can't erupt *(misconception: only thick magma erupts)*
**Feedback 1:** Blasts come from trapped gas. Runny magma lets gas out gently.
**Feedback 2:** Yes. Like the water on the baker's plate.
**Feedback 3:** It erupts, just gently. Think of the water jug.
**Hint:** Think of the water on the baker's plate.
**Explanation:** Runny magma lets gas escape easily, so lava flows out quietly and far.

**A5** · Seedling · 3-choice
**Prompt:** Why do some volcanoes throw out ash?
1. Sticky magma traps gas, then it bursts **(right)**
2. They hold far more lava than other volcanoes *(misconception: explosive means more lava)*
3. Their fire burns much hotter *(misconception: volcanoes hold fire)*
**Feedback 1:** Yes. Trapped gas escapes all at once, breaking rock into ash.
**Feedback 2:** It isn't the amount. It is gas trapped in sticky magma.
**Feedback 3:** There is no fire. Trapped gas does the blasting.
**Hint:** What happens to bubbles in honey?
**Explanation:** Gas can't escape sticky magma easily, so pressure builds until it bursts out.

**A6** · Sapling · match
**Prompt:** Match each volcano shape to how it erupted.
- Wide, low shield ↔ Runny lava that flowed far
- Steep layered cone ↔ Sticky magma, ash and lava in layers
- Small cinder cone ↔ Runny magma fountaining, bits falling near the vent
**Misconception tested:** shape is random.
**Hint:** Runny lava travels. What does sticky lava do?
**Explanation:** Shape follows eruption style, so a volcano's shape is a clue to its past.

**A7** · Sapling · 3-choice
**Prompt:** Surtsey rose from the sea in 1963. What does it show?
1. Eruptions only ever destroy things *(misconception: eruptions only destroy)*
2. Nothing can grow on new volcanic rock *(misconception: life never returns)*
3. Eruptions can build brand-new land **(right)**
**Feedback 1:** Surtsey is new land. Eruptions build as well as change.
**Feedback 2:** Plants were found there in 1965, just two years later.
**Feedback 3:** Yes. A whole island, and life arrived soon after.
**Hint:** Was Surtsey there before 1963?
**Explanation:** Eruptions can make new land. Surtsey grew until 1967, and plants arrived from 1965.

**A8** · Keeper's Desk · 3-choice
**Prompt:** Some steep cones are made of runny lava. What follows?
1. Shape always tells you exactly what magma it was *(overclaim: shape is a rule)*
2. Shape is a good clue, but you check the rock too **(right)**
3. Shape tells you nothing at all about a volcano's past *(underclaim: shape is still evidence)*
**Feedback 1:** These cones break that rule. Shape is a clue, not a rule.
**Feedback 2:** Yes. Geologists use shape and then test the rock itself.

**B7** · Keeper's Desk · number with steps *(A made-up model)*
**Prompt:** The slow waves came 5 seconds after the fast ones. How far away?

**B8** · Keeper's Desk · number with steps
**Prompt:** Speed is about √(9.8 × depth). Find it at 4,000 m.
**Answer:** about 198 m/s (about 713 km/h)
**Steps:** 1. 9.8 × 4,000 = 39,200. 2. √39,200 ≈ 198 m/s. 3. × 3.6 ≈ 713 km/h.
**Follow-up:** At 40 m deep, how many times slower? **10 times** (√(9.8 × 40) ≈ 19.8 m/s; depth 100 times less, speed 10 times less).
**Misconception tested:** a tsunami keeps its speed all the way to shore.
**Hint:** Multiply first, then take the square root.
**Explanation:** Deep water lets a tsunami race; shallow water slows it, and the water piles up.

**Explanation:** There is no single best pair. The best choice depends on what matters most, and every plan has a cost.

**Answer:** 12 mm

## 7. "I'm stuck" worked examples

**Set A (easier case: water and honey):** *Pour water and honey. Which spreads further?* 1. Water runs to the edge. 2. Honey piles up. 3. So runny lava spreads; sticky lava piles. **How to start yours:** decide if the magma is runny or sticky first.

**Set B (easier case: magnitude 2 against 3):** *One step up.* 1. One step. 2. Ten times taller wiggles. 3. So 3 is ten times 2. **How to start yours:** count the steps, then write one "× 10" for each.

**Set C (easier case: one change):** *Does a wide base help?* 1. Test the plain tower in a strong shake. 2. Add only the wide base. 3. Same shake again, then compare. **How to start yours:** name the one thing you will change.

## 8. Key terms

- **Magma:** melted rock underground.
- **Lava:** the same melted rock once it comes out.
- **Magma chamber:** the space under a volcano where magma gathers.
- **Vent:** the pathway magma rises through.
- **Crater:** the opening at the top where it comes out.
- **Shield volcano:** a wide, low volcano built by runny lava.
- **Stratovolcano:** a steep volcano built of layers of ash and lava.
- **Plate boundary:** where two plates meet.
- **Fault:** a crack in rock where the sides can slip.
- **Seismometer:** an instrument that records ground shaking.
- **Seismogram:** the wiggly line a seismometer draws.
- **Magnitude:** one number for an earthquake's size.
- **Intensity:** how strongly one place feels an earthquake.
- **Alert level:** a color or word showing how concerned volcano watchers are.
- **Tsunami:** a series of long sea waves made when the sea floor suddenly moves.
- **Base isolation:** flexible feet that let less shaking reach a building.
- **Trade-off:** getting more of one good thing by giving up some of another.
