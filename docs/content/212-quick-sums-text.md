# Quick Sums: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 212](../specs/212-quick-sums.md). Use as written, after independent spec review and a math check. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion and is never gendered; `{courier}` is the explorer's courier. Pip is he/him, Dot she/her, Bramble she/her (with her magpie), Nuria she/her, Hob Pennick he/him, Wren Halloway she/her, the miller she/her. Real-world facts are in the [source audit](../source-audits/212-quick-sums.md); items still marked `[check]` stay off screen until cleared. Nothing here is scored or timed.

## Labels used on every card

- **A story from Willowbank**: the town, its bridge, its counts and every character are made up.
- **A true thing about math**: checked math, explained simply.
- **A true thing from history**: something people really did or wrote.
- **A made-up model**: a simple game rule, shown on the card, that is not real measurement.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. One pebble each (Act I, chapter 4: mend the village)

**Hook:** The footbridge opens again. Pip wants to know how many people cross on the first day.

**Short:**
> The bridge is open! Pip drops a pebble in the pot for every crossing. Ten pebbles fill a bag. By supper, how many?

**Longer:**
> The last plank goes in at breakfast. Pip tests every one, twice. Then he sets a clay pot by the handrail.
>
> "One pebble for every crossing," he says. "Ten pebbles go in a bag. Bags are easier to count."
>
> By noon there are three bags and four loose pebbles. Dot crosses with the post. The miller crosses with flour. The miller's cat crosses, uninvited, and Pip counts her anyway.
>
> In the afternoon, two more full bags. "Thirty-four and twenty," says {guide}. "Only the bags changed."
>
> "Fifty-four," says Pip.
>
> At supper, eight more people cross to see the lanterns. Pip drops six pebbles and ties a new bag. Two pebbles are left loose.
>
> "Sixty," he says. "And two. Sixty-two crossings on the very first day."

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> Fifty-four and eight: jump six to sixty, then two more. Splitting eight into six and two makes the jump land on a ten. Real bridges count with clickers or sensors; this pot is made up.

### Scene 2. Twice as many? (Act II, chapter 1: the weekly market)

**Hook:** Market day brings crowds across the bridge. Bramble is sure it doubled the crossings.

**Short:**
> Ordinary Tuesday: 148 crossings. Market Saturday: 375. "Twice as many," says Bramble. Dot checks the ledger.

**Longer:**
> Saturday is market day, and the bridge is busy from dawn. Pip gives up on pebbles by ten o'clock and lets Dot write tallies in the ledger instead.
>
> At closing, Dot adds the page. "Three hundred and seventy-five."
>
> "Tuesday was a hundred and forty-eight," says Pip. "How many more is that?"
>
> "Count up," says {guide}. "From 148, two to 150. Fifty to 200. Then 175 to 375."
>
> "Two hundred and twenty-seven more," says Dot.
>
> "So it's twice as many," says Bramble. "I said so."
>
> "Double 148," says {guide}. "Double 150 is 300. Take away the four you added: 296."
>
> Bramble looks at 375, then at 296. "So it's more than twice." She sounds pleased anyway. Her magpie steals a pebble from the pot.

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> 148 is near 150, so double it as 150 + 150, then take away 2 and 2. Near doubles turn hard sums into easy ones. The counts are made up.

**Short:**

## Teach cards

Each card is under 30 words. Worked steps appear one tap at a time. T1 and the first step of T10 are recaps of Bundle Burrow (specs 53 and 78), marked "You've seen this before".

**T1. Tens go with tens** (A true thing about math; recap)
> A bag holds ten. Add bags, and only the bags change. The loose ones stay put.
Worked example: 1. 34 is 3 bags, 4 loose. 2. Add 2 bags: 5 bags, 4 loose. 3. 34 + 20 = 54.

**T2. Take tens away** (A true thing about math)
> Taking away tens is a small fact said in tens. The ones stay the same.
Worked example: 1. 76 − 30: 7 bags take 3 bags is 4 bags. 2. Six loose stay: 46. 3. 80 − 50: 8 tens take 5 tens is 3 tens: 30.

**T3. Jump to the ten** (A true thing about math)
> Split the number you add so the first jump lands on a ten. Then add the rest.
Worked example: 1. 58 + 6. 2. 58 needs 2 to reach 60. 3. Six is 2 and 4. 4. 60 + 4 = 64.

**T4. Big jumps on the rail** (A true thing about math)
> Add the tens first in one jump. Then jump to the next ten. Then the rest.
Worked example: 1. 47 + 36. 2. Jump 30: 77. 3. Jump 3: 80. 4. Jump 3: 83.

**T5. Count up to take away** (A true thing about math)
> When two numbers are close, count up from the smaller one. The jumps add up to the answer.
Worked example: 1. 82 − 79. 2. 79 to 80: 1. 3. 80 to 82: 2. 4. 1 + 2 = 3.

**T6. Jump past, step back** (A true thing about math)
> Adding 39? Add 40, then take 1 away. This is called compensation.
Worked example: 1. 54 + 39. 2. 54 + 40 = 94. 3. 94 − 1 = 93.

**T7. Near doubles** (A true thing about math)
> Two numbers almost the same? Double one, then fix the little difference.
Worked example: 1. 35 + 36. 2. Double 35 is 70. 3. 36 is one more: 71.

**T8. Which digit changes?** (A true thing about math)
> Adding tens changes the tens. If the tens fill past nine, a hundred is made, so the hundreds change too.
Worked example: 1. 263 + 30 = 293: tens only. 2. 283 + 40 = 323: hundreds and tens. 3. 263 + 400 = 663: hundreds only. 4. 263 + 8 = 271.

**T9. Columns that carry** (A true thing about math)
> Line up ones under ones. When a column makes ten or more, ten of them move left as one.
Worked example: 1. 348 + 175. 2. Ones: 8 + 5 = 13. Write 3, one ten moves left. 3. Tens: 4 + 7 + 1 = 12. Write 2, one hundred moves left. 4. Hundreds: 3 + 1 + 1 = 5. Answer: 523.

**T10. Open a bigger one** (A true thing about math)
> Not enough on top? Open one from the next column left. Ten of them come in. Across a zero, open twice.
Worked example: 1. 503 − 248. You've seen opening a ten before. 2. No tens, so open a hundred: 4 hundreds, 10 tens. 3. Open a ten: 9 tens, 13 ones. 4. 13 − 8 = 5, 9 − 4 = 5, 4 − 2 = 2. Answer: 255.

**T11. Longer numbers, and points** (A true thing about math)
> Same rule, more columns. Start lining up from the ones. With decimals, line up the points.
Worked example: 1. 2,647 + 1,585 = 4,232. 2. 3.45 + 2.80: points in a line. 3. Answer: 6.25.

**T12. In your head, or columns?** (A true thing about math)
> Look at the numbers first. Close numbers: count up. Messy numbers with carrying: columns.
Worked example: 1. 300 − 296: count up 4. 2. 467 + 285: carrying twice. 3. Columns: 752.

**T13. A box for the missing number** (A true thing about math)
> The equals sign means "is the same as". Is the box a part or the whole? Then check by putting it back.
Worked example: 1. 186 + □ = 250. 2. The box is a part. Count up: 4, 10, 50. 3. 64. 4. 186 + 64 = 250. It checks.

**T14. Guess first** (A true thing about math)
> Before you work it out, round and estimate. A silly answer will stand out.
Worked example: 1. 395 + 412. 2. About 400 + 400 = 800. 3. An answer of 87 is silly. So is 1,507. 807 is sensible.

**T15. Check by adding back** (A true thing about math)
> To check a take-away, add the answer back on. Doing the same sum again can repeat a slip.
Worked example: 1. 634 − 287 = 347. 2. Add back: 347 + 287. 3. 634. It checks.

**T16. Plan the steps** (A true thing about math)
> Draw the story as bars. Name the middle number. Then answer the question asked.
Worked example: 1. 415 apples. 168 sold. 230 come in. 2. Step 1: 415 − 168 = 247. That's the middle number. 3. Step 2: 247 + 230 = 477 apples.

**T17. Find the tidy pairs** (A true thing about math)
> Adding a list? Look for pairs that make tens. You can add in any order.
Worked example: 1. 26 + 17 + 14 + 23. 2. 26 + 14 = 40. 3. 17 + 23 = 40. 4. 40 + 40 = 80.

**T18. Mental moves for times, too** (A true thing about math; Keeper's Desk)
> Split a number, or use a near one and fix it. Build from facts you know. Multiply before you add.
Worked example: 1. 48 × 6 = 40 × 6 + 8 × 6 = 240 + 48 = 288. 2. 6 × 4 = 24, so 6.5 × 4 = 24 + 2 = 26.

## Real-world and history cards

**H1. The first printed plus and minus** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> In 1489, a book printed in Leipzig, Germany, used the signs + and −. It was an arithmetic book for merchants, by Johannes Widmann.
Keeper's note:
> Widmann did not use + and − for adding and taking away yet. He used them to mark goods that were over or under a weight: a little too much, or a little too little. Later writers turned them into the signs we use for sums.

**H2. The bead frame** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> In China, people added on a bead frame called a suanpan. Each rod is a place. A top bead counts five; a bottom bead counts one.
Keeper's note:
> When a rod is full, you clear it and push up one bead on the next rod to the left. That is a carry, done by hand, just like the ledger. A famous Chinese book from 1592, by Cheng Dawei, taught how to work the suanpan.

Feedback A: That's about twice the estimate. Something slipped.
Feedback B: Yes. It's sensible. Estimates can't prove exact, so check it.
Feedback C: That's far less than 800. A digit went missing.

**D6 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Pip found 634 − 287 = 347. Which check is best?
- A. Do 634 − 287 again the same way. *(tests: checking by repeating)*
- B. Add 347 + 287. Is it 634? *(right)*
- C. Check that 347 is less than 634. *(tests: a check too weak to help)*
Hint: Which check works the sum a different way?
Explanation: Adding back is a new way. The same way could repeat the same slip.
Feedback A: The same way can make the same slip again.
Feedback B: Yes. 347 + 287 = 634. It checks.
Feedback C: True, but lots of slips also give less than 634.

### Set E. Stories in steps (Seedling, Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**E1 (Seedling, number)**
Prompt: A rope is 64 cm. Dot cuts off 27 cm. How long is it now?
Answer: 37 cm.
Worked steps: 1. Draw the 64 cm bar. 2. Mark 27 cm cut off. 3. 64 − 27: count up 3 to 30, 34 to 64: 37 cm.
Tests: 91 (adding); 43 (smaller digit from bigger).
Hint: Draw the rope. Which part is left?
Explanation: The rope is the whole. Cut-off and left are its parts.

**E2 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: The new plank is 72 cm, 18 cm longer than the old. Old plank?
- A. 90 cm *(tests: "longer" always means add)*
- B. 54 cm *(right)*
- C. 18 cm *(tests: giving the difference as the length)*
Hint: Which plank is longer? Draw both bars.
Explanation: The old plank is shorter. 72 − 18 = 54 cm.
Feedback A: That would make the old one longer. Draw both.
Feedback B: Yes. The new one is 18 cm longer than 54.
Feedback C: 18 cm is the extra bit, not the old plank.

**E3 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: 326 crossings by noon, 148 after. How many more to reach 600?
Answer: 126.

Tests: 474 (stopping at the middle number).

Explanation: 474 is the middle number. The question asks how many more.

**E4 (Sapling, 3-choice)**

- C. 693 crossings *(right: 415 − 137 = 278, then 415 + 278)*

### Set F. Wren's year and the old signs (Keeper's Desk)

**F1 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: A coast city aims for 300,000 crossings. So far 146,259. How many more?

Explanation: Same opening rule along a row of zeros. About 150,000, so 153,741 fits.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **tens and ones**: a bag of ten is a ten; loose pebbles are ones.
- **bridging**: jumping past a ten or a hundred, by landing on it first.
- **counting up**: finding a difference by jumping from the smaller number.
- **compensation**: adding a near-ten, then fixing the little difference.
- **near doubles**: doubling one number, then adding the small extra.
- **column**: a place in a written sum: ones, tens, hundreds and more.
- **carry**: ten in one column moving left as one of the next.
- **opening (exchanging)**: one from the next column becoming ten here.
- **decimal point**: the dot between ones and tenths. Line the points up.
- **equals sign**: means "is the same as".
- **missing number**: the box in a number sentence.
- **estimate**: a rounded guess made first, to spot silly answers.
- **inverse check**: checking a take-away by adding back, or the other way.
- **middle number**: what step one finds, before the question's answer.
- **suanpan**: a Chinese bead frame for working sums.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** adding and taking away tens, hundreds and ones in your head; bridging through ten; counting up, compensation and near doubles; adding lists by pairing; column addition and subtraction with every carry and exchange explained in place value, up to six digits and decimals; missing numbers in any position; estimating first and checking by adding back; two-step and multi-step stories, including lengths.

**What it does not claim:** the counts, routes and city are made up. A typed digit does not show handwriting or how a child would set out columns on paper; try a few sums on squared paper together.
