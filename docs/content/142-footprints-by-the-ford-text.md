### Scene 1. The slab by the ford (Act VI, after the long rain)

**Hook (30 max):** The long rain has washed the bank by the ford. Hob Pennick has found something pale in the mud.

**Short (30 max):**
> Hob Pennick points at a slab by the ford. It has three-toed hollows in a line. "A dragon!" says Pip. Ember looks very offended.

**Longer (150 max):**
> The long rain has gone, and the brook is back between its banks. Hob Pennick walks down to check his fence by the ford. Where the bank used to be grass, there is now a pale, flat slab of stone.
>
> In the slab is a line of hollows, each with three long toes.
>
> Pip gets there first. "A dragon walked here," he says. "A big one. Probably hungry."
>
> Ember, perched on Hob's gate, puffs a small, offended cloud. "Dragons do not leave three toes. And we wipe our feet."
>
> "We could measure them," says {guide}, "before we decide who made them."
>
> Hob nods slowly. "My grandad said this bank was full of old stones. Never said they had feet in."
>
> Pip measures one hollow with his tail. It is longer than his tail. He goes very quiet, then asks if anyone brought lunch.

**Keeper's note (80 max):**
> This slab is part of the story. In the real world, footprints in rock are trace fossils: marks an animal made, later buried and turned to stone. Real tracksites exist in the Pyrenees, at places like Fumanya in Catalonia. Scientists measure prints and strides before saying what made them, and they say "probably" a lot.

**Real-or-story card after Scene 1:**
> In Willowbank, Ember is a pocket-dragon in a story. In the real world, dragons are stories people tell. Dinosaurs were real animals. We know because we find their fossils.

### Scene 2. A letter from Vadoalto (Nuria)

**Hook:** The courier brings a letter from Nuria, upstream.

**Short:**
> Nuria writes that real dinosaur footprints are in Spain, in the Pyrenees. Some are on a huge rock at Fumanya.

**Longer (letter, 150 max):**
> *¡Hola!* (Hello!)
>

**Keeper's note:**

## Teach cards (30 words max each; steps revealed one tap at a time)

**Teach 1. Real, and long ago** (A true thing from science)
> Dinosaurs were real animals. They lived long, long before any people. We know about them from fossils: bones, teeth and footprints turned to stone.

Worked example (time ribbon):
1. Put "now" at one end.
2. The pyramids are a tiny step back.
3. The first people are a little further.
4. The dinosaurs are far, far away. Look at the empty space.

**Teach 2. A footprint is a trace**
> A body fossil is part of the animal, like a tooth. A trace fossil is something it did, like a footprint. A footprint is never the foot.

Worked example (pace and stride):
1. A pace goes from one footprint to the next one.
2. A stride goes from one footprint to the next print of the same foot.
3. So one stride is about two paces.

**Teach 3. Stride for its size**
> A long stride can mean a big animal, or a fast one. Compare the stride with the footprint size to tell.

Worked example:
1. Trackway A: footprint 40 cm, stride 280 cm.
2. 280 ÷ 40 = 7 footprints in one stride.
3. Trackway B: footprint 10 cm, stride 160 cm. That is 16 footprints in one stride.
4. B's stride is short, but long for its size. B was probably running.

**Teach 4. Two rules of thumb** (Sapling and Keeper's Desk)
> Hip height is about 4 times footprint length. Relative stride is stride ÷ hip height. Under 2 suggests walking. Over 2.9 suggests running.

Worked example (trackway A):
1. Footprint 40 cm. Hip height about 4 × 40 = 160 cm.
2. Stride 280 cm. Relative stride 280 ÷ 160 = 1.75.
3. 1.75 is under 2. That suggests walking.
4. Try 5 × 40 = 200 cm. 280 ÷ 200 = 1.4. Still walking.

**Rule credit (Details):**
> A true thing from science: in 1976 R. McNeill Alexander, who studied how animals move, worked out these estimates from living animals. They are rules of thumb. Real dinosaurs varied.
>
> Alexander put the walk-to-trot change near 2. Later, Thulborn and Wade added the run line near 2.9.

**Teach 5. How a fossil forms**
> Most animals leave no fossil. A fossil needs quick burial in mud or sand. Then, over a very long time, minerals turn the hard parts to stone.

Worked example (fossil path):
1. An animal dies near a river.
2. Mud covers it quickly.
3. Soft parts rot. Bones and teeth stay.
4. Minerals soak in. The mud slowly turns to rock.

**Teach 6. Teeth tell diet**
> Sharp, pointed teeth cut meat. Blunt or flat teeth strip and grind plants. Size does not tell you. The biggest dinosaurs ate plants.

**Teach 7. Birds are dinosaurs** (Sapling and up)
> Birds are living dinosaurs. They share a wishbone, hollow bones and three-toed feet with a group of dinosaurs called theropods.

**Teach 8. Ideas can change**
> Old pictures showed scaly dinosaurs dragging their tails. New fossils showed feathers, and tracks with no tail marks. So scientists changed the pictures.

## Mary Anning card (A true thing from science history)

> Mary Anning (1799 to 1847) hunted fossils on the cliffs at Lyme Regis, England. She was poor and mostly taught herself.
>
> With her brother Joseph, she found an ichthyosaur, a sea reptile. Later she found a plesiosaur and a flying pterosaur. None of these were dinosaurs.
>
> Her finds helped show that animals could die out completely. She was often left out of the scientists' meetings, and others wrote about her finds without her name.

**Keeper's note:**
> The ichthyosaur skull was found in 1811 and the skeleton dug out in 1812. Her nearly complete plesiosaur came in 1823; her pterosaur, *Dimorphodon*, in 1828, the first found in England. The Geological Society did not invite her to the meeting that debated her plesiosaur.

## The Trackway Table

**Choose a trackway:** Trackway A · Trackway B · Trackway C · Trackway D · Trackways E

**Card lines:**
- A: Big three-toed prints, in one line.
- B: Small three-toed prints, far apart.
- C: Middle-sized three-toed prints.
- D: Big round prints, with smaller ones in front.
- E: Three lines of small prints, side by side.

**Buttons and prompts:**
- Tap a footprint, then Measure.
- Footprint: {n} cm long.
- Tap a print. Then tap the next print made by the same foot.
- Stride: {n} cm.
- **Pace instead of stride:** That's one pace: left foot to right foot. A stride goes to the same foot again.
- **Two strides:** That's two strides. Try the very next print of that foot.
- Footprints in one stride: about {n}.
- Estimate hip height (optional).
- Hip height: about {lo} to {hi} cm. (4 × and 5 × the footprint.)
- Relative stride: about {lo} to {hi}.
- What does the band suggest? · walking · in between · running · can't tell from this range

**Result card:**
- Footprint {fl} cm. Stride {s} cm.
- Hip height about {lo} to {hi} cm.
- Relative stride about {rlo} to {rhi}.
- Estimate from a rule of thumb. Real trackmakers varied.

**Band feedback (no right/wrong mark):**
- All walking: The whole band is under 2. That suggests walking, whichever rule you use.
- All running: The whole band is over 2.9. That suggests running, whichever rule you use.
- Crosses a line (C): The band crosses the line at 2. It might be walking, or in between. These rules can't decide.

**Silhouette label:** A guess at the shape. Footprints don't show skin, color or the whole animal.

**Keep this reading** · **Kept.** You can measure again any time.

**2.2 (Seedling)** Sharp, pointed teeth with jagged edges. What do they tell you?

- b) It hunted the biggest animals. `sharp-big-prey` · Feedback: Maybe not. Sharp teeth could catch fish or small animals too.

**2.3 (Seedling, order)** Put the fossil path in order.
- Cards: The animal dies · Mud covers it quickly · Soft parts rot away · Minerals turn bones to stone · Rain and rivers wear the rock away

- Misconception watched: `surface-fossil` (minerals before burial).
- Hint: What has to happen before it can rot or be eaten?
- Explanation: Quick burial comes first. Then soft parts rot, minerals harden the bones, and erosion later shows them.

**2.4 (Sapling)** Why do most animals never become fossils?

- b) Their bones are too soft to last. `soft-bone` · Feedback: Bones are hard parts. The problem is rotting before burial.
- c) Fossils only form deep in caves. `place-only` · Feedback: Most form under mud or sand, often in water.
- Hint: What happens to an animal left in the open?
- Explanation: Most remains rot or are eaten. Only a lucky few are buried fast enough.

**2.5 (Sapling, match: body or trace?)** Part of the animal, or something it did?

- Feedback, coprolite as body: It's a lump, but it records something the animal did: eating! Trace.
- Hint: Ask: part of the animal, or something it did?
- Explanation: Body fossils are parts of an animal. Trace fossils record actions. Coprolites can show what an animal ate.

**2.6 (Sapling, true or not-true from this card)** From the Mary Anning card: "She found dinosaur skeletons."

### Set 3. Read the trackway (Seedling, Sapling)

- Watched: `count-prints` (counting footprints instead of trackways).

**3.3 (Seedling)** Trackway A's stride is longer than B's. Which animal was probably running?
- a) A, because its stride is longer than B's. `raw-stride` · Feedback: A is big. For its size, its stride is short.
- b) A, because its prints are pressed in deeper. `depth-speed` · Feedback: Depth can mean soft mud or a heavy animal.
- c) ✓ B, because its stride is long for its size. · Feedback: Yes. 16 footprints in one stride is a lot.
- Hint: How many footprints fit in each stride?
- Explanation: A fits 7 footprints in a stride; B fits 16. B's stride is long for its size.

**3.4 (Seedling)** Three trackways go side by side, the same way. What might it mean?
- a) It proves they were a family. `herd-proven` · Feedback: Prints can't prove family. It's one possible idea.
- b) It proves they were racing. `race-proven` · Feedback: Racing needs long strides. These strides suggest walking.
- c) ✓ They may have walked together, or not. · Feedback: Yes. They may have walked together, or used the same path.

- Explanation: Side-by-side tracks can mean a group. They might also be a path used at different times.

**3.5 (Sapling, number)** A footprint is 25 cm long. Use the 4 times rule. Hip height?
- Answer: about 100 cm.
- Steps: 1) Hip height ≈ 4 × footprint. 2) 4 × 25 = 100. 3) About 100 cm, an estimate.
- Watched: `add-not-times` (25 + 4 = 29).
- Hint: Times, not plus. Four lots of 25.
- Explanation: The rule says about 4 times. 4 × 25 = 100 cm. It's a rule of thumb, so it's approximate.

**3.6 (Sapling, number)** Footprint 30 cm, stride 360 cm. Use the 4 times rule. Walking or running?
- Answer: running. Hip height 120 cm; relative stride 3.0, over 2.9.
- Steps: 1) 4 × 30 = 120. 2) 360 ÷ 120 = 3. 3) 3 is over 2.9: running.
- Watched: `ratio-backwards` (120 ÷ 360 ≈ 0.33).
- Hint: Find hip height first. Then stride ÷ hip height.
- Explanation: Relative stride 3.0 is just over 2.9. With the 5 times rule it is 2.4, in between. Estimates have ranges.

**3.7 (Sapling, number, reverse)** Hip height about 100 cm, relative stride 2. How long is the stride?
- Answer: about 200 cm.
- Steps: 1) Relative stride = stride ÷ hip height. 2) 2 = stride ÷ 100. 3) Stride = 2 × 100 = 200.
- Watched: `ratio-backwards` (100 ÷ 2 = 50).
- Hint: Which number divided by 100 makes 2?
- Explanation: Stride ÷ 100 = 2, so the stride is 200 cm.

**3.8 (Sapling, number)** Paces are 70 cm each. How long is one stride?
- Answer: about 140 cm.
- Steps: 1) A stride is two paces. 2) 70 + 70 = 140.
- Watched: `pace-stride` (answers 70).
- Hint: Left to right is one pace. Left to left is a stride.
- Explanation: One stride goes from one foot back to the same foot: two paces, about 140 cm.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Dinosaur:** a real animal from long ago. The big ones are all gone. Birds are dinosaurs that live today.
- **Extinct:** none left alive anywhere.
- **Fossil:** remains or traces of past life, kept in rock.
- **Body fossil:** part of an animal, like a bone, tooth or shell, kept in rock.
- **Trace fossil:** a sign of something an animal did, like a footprint, burrow or fossil poo.
- **Coprolite:** fossil poo. It can show what an animal ate.
- **Sediment:** mud, sand and tiny bits of rock that settle in layers.
- **Minerals:** natural materials in water and rock. They can slowly turn bones to stone.
- **Trackway:** a line of footprints made by one animal.
- **Pace:** from one footprint to the next.
- **Stride:** from one footprint to the next print of the same foot.
- **Hip height:** how high the hip is above the ground.
- **Relative stride:** stride divided by hip height. It helps compare big and small animals.
- **Estimate:** a careful guess, with a range.
- **Rule of thumb:** a handy rule that is roughly right, most of the time.
- **Mesozoic:** the time of the dinosaurs, about 252 to 66 million years ago.
- **Theropods:** a group of mostly two-legged dinosaurs with three main toes. Birds belong here.
- **Pterosaur:** a flying reptile. A neighbor of dinosaurs, not a dinosaur.
- **Plesiosaur:** a sea reptile. A neighbor of dinosaurs, not a dinosaur.
- **Paleontologist:** a scientist who studies fossils.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** that dinosaurs were real animals known from fossils; how to tell them from story creatures and from flying and sea reptiles; reading diet from teeth; how fossils form and why most living things leave none; reading a trackway; estimating hip height and gait with stated rules of thumb and ranges; the order and length of the Mesozoic; that birds are living dinosaurs; Mary Anning; and how new fossils changed old ideas.

**What it does not claim:** the trackways and the slab by the ford are made up. The bench's rules (hip height about 4 to 5 times footprint length; relative stride under about 2 walking, over about 2.9 running) are rules of thumb used by scientists, with real limits: dinosaurs of different groups had different proportions, and soft mud changes prints. The bench never calculates a speed. Story characters (including Ember the dragon) are fiction. The extinction section is brief and gentle.
