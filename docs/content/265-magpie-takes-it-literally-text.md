# The Magpie Takes It Literally: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 265](../specs/265-magpie-takes-it-literally.md). Use as written, after review. `{guide}` is never gendered. Pronouns: Pip and the baker he/him; Dot, Bramble and the miller she/her; the librarian and Hollis they/them; the magpie "it" (as in spec 273). Ember takes no pronoun here. Sources: [audit](../source-audits/265-magpie-takes-it-literally.md). Nothing is scored, timed or locked.

**Languages:** every Spanish line is marked **[ES]** and every French line **[FR]**. Each sits beside an English line. All of them need a fluent reader before build.

**Hollis rule:** Hollis is named only in scene 3 and in the Act IV version of bench (f). Both open after page 4 comes free. Everything else says nothing about Hollis or the pages.

**Labels used throughout:**
- **A story from Willowbank** marks the town, its people and the magpie (fiction).
- **A true thing about language** marks real facts about words, sayings and languages.
- **A story people told** marks myths, fables and old tales behind allusions.
- **A true thing from history** marks real writers, books and dates.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. A horse on the menu (Act II ch. 3, Ember's birthday)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** Bramble comes to Ember's birthday with ribbons and her magpie. The magpie hears everything. It understands all of it wrong.

**Short:**
> At Ember's party, Pip says he could eat a horse. The magpie flies off to warn the horses. Pip only meant he was very hungry.

**Longer:**
> The café is warm and loud. Candles crackle on the cake.
>
> Bramble comes in with ribbons over one arm and the magpie on her hat. Nobody says anything. Dot tells a joke to break the ice.
>
> The magpie looks at the floor. "What ice?"
>
> "I'm so hungry I could eat a horse," says Pip.
>
> The magpie shoots out of the window. A minute later it is back. "I've warned every horse in the valley. They're hiding."
>
> "He means he's very hungry," says Bramble. "It's a saying."
>
> The magpie thinks about this. "Then why didn't he say that?"
>
> "Sayings are more fun," says {guide}.
>
> Pip takes a second slice of cake. The horses stay hidden until supper.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> A group of words with its own meaning is called an idiom. You can't work out an idiom from its single words. Many are hundreds of years old. Nobody knows for sure where "raining cats and dogs" came from, and the stories told about it are guesses.

### Scene 2. Ember's birthday verse (Act II ch. 3)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** Dot writes Ember a birthday verse and reads it standing on a chair. The magpie listens closely. Much too closely.

**Short:**
> Dot's verse calls the cake a golden hill. The magpie goes to look for the hill. Dot meant the cake was big and golden.

**Longer:**
> Dot climbs onto a chair and unfolds her paper.
>
> *Ember, small as a teacup,*
> *glows like a lantern at dusk.*
> *Candles crackle, cinnamon drifts,*
> *and the cake is a golden hill.*
>
> Ember glows, pleased.
>
> The magpie frowns. "Ember isn't a teacup. Ember is a dragon."
>
> "It means Ember is small," says Dot.
>
> "And there is no hill. I checked the whole café."
>
> "The cake is big and golden, like a hill," says Dot. "I just said it shorter."
>
> "Read it again," says Bramble. "The crackly part."

### Scene 3. Water under the bridge (Act IV ch. 4, before the meeting)

Opens only after page 4 comes free. The meeting's own speeches belong to Listening at the Meeting (275); this is Bramble practicing first.

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** Bramble practices her speech for the Wall at the ribbon gate. Every point is a picture. The magpie checks each one.

**Short:**
> Bramble practices her speech for the Wall. The magpie takes each picture literally. Plain words show what she means, and what still needs reasons.

**Longer:**
> Bramble stands at the ribbon gate with her notes. The magpie is the audience.
>
> "A wall is the town's umbrella," she says.
>
> "Umbrellas have handles," says the magpie. "Where is the handle?"
>
> "I mean a wall would keep the water off our houses."
>
> "Then say that."
>
> "The orchard is the town's heart."
>
> "Hearts don't grow apples."
>
> "I mean the orchard families matter. They've farmed there for three generations." She turns a page. "And draining the Sponge is water under the bridge."
>
> The magpie looks over the gate. "There is a lot of water under that bridge. That's the problem."
>
> Bramble laughs in spite of herself.
>
> "Your plain words are real reasons," says {guide}. "The pictures make them easy to see."
>
> "Hollis's map calls the marsh the Sponge," says the magpie. "Is that a picture too?"
>
> "Yes," says Bramble slowly. "It is."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> A figure of speech can make a reason easy to remember. It can't show the reason is true. Every plan at the meeting has its pictures. The Wall is an umbrella. The marsh is a sponge. Turn each picture into plain words, then ask what would show it is right.

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer; worked-example steps appear one tap at a time. Every card is **A true thing about language**.

**Card 1. Sense words (Sprout)**
> Some words help you see, hear, smell, taste or touch what happens. They are called sense words.

Worked example: *"Candles crackle, cinnamon drifts."*
1. Crackle is a sound: hear.
2. Cinnamon drifts through the air: smell.
3. Two senses in four words.

**Card 2. Story words (Sprout)**
> Many stories open and close with the same words. "Once upon a time" opens a story. "Happily ever after" closes one.

Worked example: *Three stories on the library shelf.*
1. All three start "Once upon a time."
2. Two end "happily ever after."
3. These words tell you: a story is starting, or ending.

**Card 3. Literal or figurative (Seedling)**
> Literal words mean just what they say. Figurative words mean something else. "On fire" can mean burning. It can also mean doing really well.

Worked example: *Two sentences.*
1. "The log was on fire." Flames. Literal.
2. "Dot was on fire at the quiz." No flames. She got every answer.
3. Figurative.

**Card 4. Idioms (Seedling)**
> An idiom is a group of words with its own meaning. The single words don't tell you. "A piece of cake" means something easy.

Worked example: *"That riddle was a piece of cake."*
1. Is there cake? No.
2. What would a piece of cake be like to eat? Easy.
3. The riddle was easy.

**Card 5. Use the clues around it (Seedling)**
> Stuck on a saying? Read the sentences around it. They often show what it means. Then check in a dictionary.

Worked example: *"Nobody spoke. Dot told a joke to break the ice. Soon everyone was laughing."*
1. Before: nobody spoke.
2. After: everyone laughed.
3. Break the ice: help people start talking.

**Card 6. Proverbs (Sapling)**
> A proverb is an old saying that gives advice. "The early bird catches the worm" means starting early can help.

Worked example: *"Don't judge a book by its cover."*
1. Is it about books? Not really.
2. What advice does it give? Don't decide too fast from how something looks.
3. You could say it about a person, a plan, or a cake.

**Card 7. Other languages, other pictures (Sapling)**
> Other languages have sayings too. Sometimes the picture is the same. Often the picture is different, but the meaning matches.

Worked example: *Heavy rain.*
1. English: raining cats and dogs.
2. **[FR]** *Il pleut des cordes.* Word for word: it's raining ropes.
3. Different pictures. Same meaning: very heavy rain.

**Card 8. Similes (Seedling)**
> A simile compares two different kinds of things, usually with "like" or "as." Comparing two of the same kind, like two hats, is usually called a plain comparison.

Worked example: *"Ember glows like a lantern."*
1. A dragon and a lantern: different kinds of things.
2. It uses "like."
3. A simile. "Dot's hat is like Bramble's hat" compares two hats: a plain comparison.

**Card 9. Metaphors (Seedling)**
> A metaphor says one thing is another. Ask two questions: what two things are compared? What do they share?

Worked example: *"The cake is a golden hill."*
1. Two things: the cake and a hill.
2. Shared: big, rounded, golden.
3. Not shared: grass, sheep. A metaphor borrows only some things.

**Card 10. Like a person (Sapling)**
> Personification gives a thing a person's feelings or actions. "The trees bow down their heads" makes trees seem like people.

Worked example: *"The wind howled down the chimney."*
1. Who howls? Wolves, dogs, upset people.
2. The wind can't feel upset.
3. "Howled" makes it sound wild and loud.

**Card 11. Allusions (Sapling)**
> An allusion points to a famous story. "A Herculean task" points to Hercules, a hero in old stories. It means a huge, hard job.

Worked example: *"Clearing the storm mud was a Herculean task."*
1. Who was Hercules? A hero who had twelve huge jobs.
2. What is the mud job like? Huge and hard.
3. The story gives the meaning.

**Card 12. What the words do (Sapling)**
> Writers choose words to make you feel something. Sense words and figures can make a scene feel calm, cozy, wild or scary.

Worked example: *"It rained" and "Rain drummed on the roofs."*
1. Same rain.
2. "Drummed" adds a loud, steady sound.
3. The second makes the storm feel bigger.

**Card 13. A picture is not a reason (Sapling)**
> A figure of speech makes a point easy to see. It doesn't prove the point. Turn it into a plain claim. Then ask for reasons.

Worked example: *"The market is the town's heart."*
1. Plain claim: the market matters a lot to the town.
2. Does the picture prove that? No.
3. Reasons might: who meets there, what is sold.

**Card 14. Saying the opposite (Keeper's Desk)**
> Irony is saying the opposite of what you mean, or an event turning out opposite to what you'd expect. "Lovely weather!" in a storm is irony.

Worked example: *Two rainy moments.*
1. Bramble says "Lovely weather" in a downpour: words opposite to meaning. Irony.
2. The umbrella seller is the only one soaked: an event opposite to what you'd expect. Irony.
3. Rain on market day is just bad luck.

**Card 15. Puns, oxymorons, long metaphors (Keeper's Desk)**
> A pun plays with two meanings. An oxymoron joins two opposites, like "sweet sorrow." An extended metaphor keeps one comparison going for many lines.

Worked example: *The baker's sign: "Fresh bread. We knead you."*
1. Knead: press dough with your hands.
2. Need: want very much.
3. One sound, two meanings: a pun.

**Card 16. Same meaning, different feeling (Keeper's Desk)**
> Words can share a meaning but carry different feelings. "Thrifty" praises careful saving. "Stingy" blames it. That feeling is called connotation.

Worked example: *Three words for a thin cat.*
1. Slender: sounds graceful.
2. Thin: sounds plain.
3. Scrawny: sounds unkind. Same cat, three feelings.

### (c) Sayings from Three Towns (Sapling)

**Header:** One idea, three languages. Match each saying to what it means.

**Saying cards (A true thing about language):**
1. Raining cats and dogs. · **[ES]** *Llueve a cántaros.* Word for word: it rains by the pitcherful. · **[FR]** *Il pleut des cordes.* Word for word: it's raining ropes. · **Meaning:** It's raining very hard.
2. When pigs fly. · **[ES]** *Cuando las ranas críen pelo.* Word for word: when frogs grow hair. · **[FR]** *Quand les poules auront des dents.* Word for word: when hens have teeth. · **Meaning:** Never.
3. Head in the clouds. · **[ES]** *Estar en las nubes.* Word for word: to be in the clouds. · **[FR]** *Être dans la lune.* Word for word: to be in the moon. · **Meaning:** Daydreaming, not paying attention.
4. Rome wasn't built in a day. · **[ES]** *No se ganó Zamora en una hora.* Word for word: Zamora wasn't won in an hour. · **[FR]** *Petit à petit, l'oiseau fait son nid.* Word for word: little by little, the bird builds its nest. · **Meaning:** Big things take time.
5. A frog in my throat. · **[FR]** *Un chat dans la gorge.* Word for word: a cat in the throat. · **Meaning:** A croaky voice.

**Same picture or different?** Tap each pair. (Clouds and clouds: same. Pigs and frogs: different.)

**The magpie:** "Frogs with hair. Hens with teeth. Flying pigs. Your languages are very strange." Bramble: "So is a magpie that talks."

**Proverb cards (A true thing about language):** The early bird catches the worm. · Don't judge a book by its cover. · Actions speak louder than words.

**When would you say it?** Match each moment to a proverb.
- Dot gets to the market first and finds the best apples. → The early bird catches the worm.
- The plainest cake at the party tastes the best. → Don't judge a book by its cover.
- Pip says he'll help tidy up. Dot just picks up a broom. → Actions speak louder than words.

**When would you say it? feedback (`wrong-pair`):** Read the moment again. Which advice does it show?

**Note on Zamora (A true thing from history):** Zamora is a city in Spain. The saying remembers a long fight over the city, hundreds of years ago.

**Talking to whom? (links to To the Council, To a Friend, `#post/register`):** Sayings suit friendly talk. In a letter to the town council, plain words often work better.

### (d) Like, or Is? (Seedling, Sapling)

**Header:** Sort each line: a simile, a metaphor, or a plain comparison.

**Lines:** small as a teacup (simile) · glows like a lantern (simile) · the cake is a golden hill (metaphor) · The brook was a silver ribbon (metaphor) · Dot's hat is like Bramble's hat (plain comparison) · This cake is as big as last year's cake (plain comparison)

**Plain comparison feedback (`like-means-simile`):** Two hats, or two cakes, are the same kind of thing. Most people call that a plain comparison.

**Two things and what they share:** Pick the two things compared. Then pick what they share.
- The brook / a ribbon / long, thin and shiny
- The cake / a hill / big, rounded and golden
- Ember / a lantern / glowing in the dark

**Too far?** The magpie asks: "The fog was a gray blanket. So is the fog warm?" Choose: warm, or covering everything.

**Feedback:** Blankets are warm, but the fog borrows only "covering." A metaphor doesn't borrow everything.

### (e) Old Stories in New Words (Sapling)

**Header:** Some words point to an old story. Open the story to find the meaning.

**Cards. Each shows a sentence, the story behind it, and the meaning:**
- **Herculean.** "Clearing the storm mud was a Herculean task." *A story people told:* Hercules was a hero who had to do twelve huge, hard jobs. **Meaning:** very big and hard. (Hercules is his Roman name. The Greeks called him Heracles. More old stories: Myth, Ash and Empire, `#archive/myths`, once it is built.)
- **Achilles' heel.** "Pip's Achilles' heel is cake." *A story people told:* the hero Achilles could be hurt nowhere except on one heel. **Meaning:** one weak spot.
- **Pandora's box.** "Pip's question opened a Pandora's box." *A story people told:* Pandora opened a jar, and troubles flew out into the world. **Meaning:** something that lets out many troubles at once.
- **The Midas touch.** "The baker has the Midas touch with pies." *A story people told:* King Midas wished that all he touched would turn to gold. Then his supper did too. **Meaning:** a knack for making things go well.
- **Gargantuan.** "The baker made a gargantuan loaf." *A true thing from history:* Gargantua is a giant with a huge appetite in books by the French writer François Rabelais, from the 1530s. **Meaning:** enormous.
- **Tilting at windmills.** "Pip charged at the scarecrow, sure it was a pie thief. He was tilting at windmills." *A true thing from history:* in *Don Quixote* (1605), by the Spanish writer Miguel de Cervantes, a knight charges at windmills, sure they are giants. **Meaning:** fighting enemies that aren't really there.
- **Sour grapes.** "Saying the prize was silly was just sour grapes." *A story people told:* in a fable, a fox can't reach some grapes, so he says they must be sour. **Meaning:** pretending you never wanted what you couldn't get. (The whole fable is on the Fable Shelf, `#loft/fables`, once it is built.)

**Keeper's note on Pandora (A true thing from history):** In the oldest Greek telling, Pandora's container was a big storage jar. "Box" came much later. It is usually traced to the scholar Erasmus, who retold the story in Latin in 1508.

**Keeper's note on Gargantua (A true thing about language):** The giant's name may come from *garganta* **[ES]**, a Spanish word for throat. Nobody is sure.

**The magpie:** "A jar full of troubles. I'll leave that one closed."

### (f) The Wall Is an Umbrella (Sapling, Keeper's Desk)

**Header:** Turn each picture into plain words. Then ask what the picture leaves out.

**Three steps (shown on every card):** 1. What is the picture? 2. Say it plain. 3. What does it leave out?

**Market version (standalone; A story from Willowbank):** The town is deciding whether the Saturday market moves to the meadow for summer.
- The miller: "The market is the town's heart." **Plain:** the market is where the town meets and trades. **Leaves out:** whether the meadow would work just as well.
- The baker: "Moving it is a recipe for trouble." **Plain:** moving might cause problems. **Leaves out:** which problems.
- Dot: "The meadow is a breath of fresh air." **Plain:** the meadow is pleasant and new. **Leaves out:** how far it is to carry the stalls.

**Act IV version (opens after page 4; A story from Willowbank):**
- Bramble: "A wall is the town's umbrella." **Plain:** a higher wall would keep floodwater off the houses and orchard behind it. **Leaves out:** where the water would go instead.
- Bramble: "The orchard is the town's heart." **Plain:** the orchard families matter a lot to the town. **Leaves out:** which plan protects them best.
- Bramble: "Draining the Sponge is water under the bridge." **Plain:** the draining is done, so plan for now. **Leaves out:** whether some fields could be wet again.
- Hollis's old map: "the Sponge." **Plain:** a marsh can hold water and slow it down. **Leaves out:** how much water, and whose fields.
- The miller: "Don't put all your eggs in one basket." **Plain:** two smaller fixes might be safer than one big one. **Leaves out:** whether two smaller fixes are enough.

**Closing line (both versions):** Every plan has its pictures. Pictures make points easy to see. Reasons and evidence decide.

**Two Storms (A story from Willowbank):**
> **Storm A.** It rained all night. The wind blew. In the morning the brook was high.
>
> **Storm B.** All night the rain drummed on the roofs. The wind howled down the chimney like a lost dog. By morning the brook was high, a growling beast.

**Two Storms questions:** Are the facts the same? Which words change the feeling?

**One thing, many names (Keeper's Desk).** Tap the feeling each name carries: kind, plain, or unkind.
- Standalone: the miller's cat is *slender* (kind) · *thin* (plain) · *scrawny* (unkind).
- Act IV version: the old marsh is *the Sponge* (kind) · *the marsh* (plain) · *a soggy swamp* (unkind). Both sides can use loaded names.

### (g) Saying the Opposite (Keeper's Desk)

**Header:** Words that say the opposite, play with sound, or keep a picture going.

**Irony cards (A story from Willowbank):**
- Rain drums on the library roof. Bramble, dripping, grumbles, "Lovely weather for a meeting about floods." *Words opposite to the meaning: verbal irony.*
- The baker sells umbrellas at the Fair. On the wettest day, he is the only one soaked. *Events opposite to what you'd expect: situational irony.*
- It rains on market day. *Just bad luck. Not irony.*
- When the reader knows something a character doesn't, that's dramatic irony. Meet it at Two Stories of One Flood (`#archive/accounts`).

**Pun card (A story from Willowbank):** The baker's sign: "Fresh bread. We knead you." The magpie: "Need, or knead? Both, I suppose."

**Oxymoron card (A true thing from history):** In *Romeo and Juliet* (first printed 1597), William Shakespeare's Juliet says parting is "such sweet sorrow." Sweet and sorrow are opposites, joined.

**Extended metaphor cards (A true thing from history):**
- Emily Dickinson's poem "Hope," as first printed in 1891, begins: *Hope is the thing with feathers / That perches in the soul,* Tap the bird words that keep the picture going.
- Robert Louis Stevenson's "Windy Nights" (*A Child's Garden of Verses*, 1885) begins: *Whenever the moon and stars are set, / Whenever the wind is high, / All night long in the dark and wet, / A man goes riding by.* The poem never says who the rider is. Many readers hear the wind, galloping all night. What do you think? Tell a grown-up.

**The magpie, on Dickinson:** "I have feathers. I perch. Am I hope?" Bramble: "Today, a bit."

## Problem sets

Every item has the "I'm stuck" ladder. Choices appear in the order written; ✔ marks the right one. Tags in backticks name the misconception a wrong choice tests.

### Set A. Sense words and story words (Sprout, Seedling)

**A1 (Sprout, guided).** Steps shown first: 1. Read "candles crackle." 2. Is crackle a look, a sound or a smell? 3. Pick the sense.
- Prompt: Which sense does "crackle" wake up?
- Choices: Hearing ✔ · Tasting (`sense-mixup`) · Smelling (`sense-mixup`)
- Feedback: Tasting: You can't taste a crackle. Try listening. · Smelling: Cinnamon is the smell. Crackle is a sound.
- Hint: Say "crackle" out loud. What do you hear?
- Explanation: Crackle is a sound word. It helps you hear the candles.

**A2 (Sprout, sort).** Put each word in its sense jar.
- Words: golden (see) · ding (hear) · cinnamon (smell) · sour (taste) · sticky (touch)
- Feedback for sticky in see (`sense-mixup`): You can't see sticky. You feel it on your paws.
- Hint: Ask: would I see it, hear it, smell it, taste it or feel it?
- Explanation: Each sense word wakes up one sense.

**A3 (Seedling).**
- Prompt: Which words usually open a story?
- Choices: The end (`opening-closing`) · Once upon a time ✔ · Happily ever after (`opening-closing`)
- Feedback: The end: Those words close a story. · Happily ever after: That's how many stories close.
- Hint: What do you hear first when a story starts?
- Explanation: "Once upon a time" opens many stories. **[FR]** *Il était une fois* does too, in French.

### Set B. What the magpie heard (Seedling)

**B1 (Seedling, guided).** Steps shown first: 1. Picture it exactly as said. 2. Could Pip eat a horse? 3. What feeling is he showing?
- Prompt: Pip says, "I could eat a horse." What does he mean?
- Choices: He wants a horse for supper (`literal-reading`) · He likes horses a lot (`word-association`) · He is very, very hungry ✔
- Feedback: Horse for supper: That's the magpie's picture. No horse is on the menu. · Likes horses: He's talking about eating, not liking.
- Hint: What is Pip always thinking about at parties?
- Explanation: It's an idiom. "I could eat a horse" means very hungry.

**B2 (Seedling, two trays).** Put each line in a tray: means just what it says, or means something else.
- Cards: The log was on fire (says) · Dot was on fire at the quiz (else) · Pip took a step forward (says) · The town took steps to mend the bridge (else)
- Feedback for "Dot was on fire" in says (`literal-reading`): Was Dot burning? No. She did really well.
- Hint: Picture each one exactly. Could it really happen?
- Explanation: The same words can be literal in one line and figurative in another.

**B3 (Seedling).**
- Prompt: Ember says, "I've got butterflies in my stomach." What does Ember mean?
- Choices: Ember swallowed some butterflies (`literal-reading`) · Ember feels nervous and fluttery ✔ · Ember wants butterflies at the party (`word-association`)

**Step 1, hint:** the item's own hint.

**Step 2, an easier case and how to start:**
- Sense items: "Try this one first: *the bell went ding.* Ding is a sound: hear. Now say your word aloud."
- Literal or figurative items: "Try this one first: *Dot was on fire.* No flames, so it means something else. Now picture yours exactly."

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Sense words:** words that help you see, hear, smell, taste or touch.
- **Literal:** meaning exactly what the words say.
- **Figurative:** meaning something other than the exact words.
- **Figure of speech:** a way of saying something that isn't meant literally, like a simile or an idiom.
- **Idiom:** a group of words with its own meaning, like "a piece of cake."
- **Proverb:** an old saying that gives advice.
- **Simile:** a comparison of two different kinds of things, usually with "like" or "as."
- **Metaphor:** saying one thing is another, to show what they share.
- **Personification:** giving a thing a person's feelings or actions.
- **Allusion:** a word or phrase that points to a famous story.
- **Plain claim:** what a figure of speech means, said in plain words.
- **Irony:** saying the opposite of what you mean, or events turning out opposite to what you'd expect.
- **Pun:** a joke that plays with two meanings of one sound or word.
- **Oxymoron:** two opposite words joined, like "sweet sorrow."
- **Extended metaphor:** a metaphor that keeps going for many lines.
- **Connotation:** the feeling a word carries, beyond its meaning.
- **Fable:** a short story, often with animals, that ends in a lesson.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** sense words and recurring story language; literal against figurative meaning; idioms and proverbs, with Spanish and French sayings beside them; similes, metaphors and personification; allusions to myths, fables and famous books; how word choices change a reader's feeling; turning a figure of speech into a plain claim; and, at Keeper's Desk, irony, puns, oxymorons, extended metaphors and connotation.

**Why the magpie:** taking words literally is a normal step in learning figurative language. The magpie does it so no child has to. Its mistakes are the joke; the child's never are.

**What it does not claim:** stories about where idioms came from are often guesses, so the pack gives none, except where a writer or book is known. Myths and fables are labeled "A story people told." Spanish and French sayings are common ones, chosen for their pictures; they need a fluent reader's check, and other regions may say them differently. No line asks how your child feels; the feelings belong to Ember and the town.

**About Act IV:** Bramble's pictures make fair points. So do the other side's. The pack shows how to turn every picture into a plain claim. It never says which plan is right.

**About magpies:** people say magpies steal shiny things. A 2014 study found wild magpies were wary of new objects, shiny or not. Willowbank's magpie is a made-up character.
