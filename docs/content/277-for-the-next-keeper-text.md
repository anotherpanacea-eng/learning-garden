# For the Next Keeper: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 277](../specs/277-for-the-next-keeper.md). Use as written, after review. `{guide}` is never gendered. Pronouns: the librarian they/them; Pip he/him; Dot, Bramble, Nuria and the miller she/her; Hob, Léon and the baker he/him; Bramble's magpie "it" (as in packs 265, 272 and 273); Hollis they/them, named only in scene 1 (Act V). The explorer is "you". Sources: [audit](../source-audits/277-for-the-next-keeper.md). Nothing is scored, timed or locked.

**Writing rule:** the child's own writing is spoken or done on paper and read by a person. Nothing the child writes is typed, stored or graded. On screen, the child works only with supplied drafts.

**Labels used throughout:**
- **A story from Willowbank** marks the town, its people and every supplied draft (fiction, newly written for this pack).
- **A true thing about language** marks how writing works.
- **A true thing about living things** marks the real frog facts on the research bench.
- **A story people told** marks the fable lines, quoted exactly from the Fable Shelf's retellings (pack 261).
- **Book cards** are real public-domain text from *The Wind in the Willows* (Kenneth Grahame, 1908), quoted exactly as in pack 272, with a source line.

**Reading lengths.** Scenes have Short, Longer and a Keeper's note. Every bench task and every problem-set item shows its own draft or card, so it can be answered at any reading length. Read-aloud is offered on every card.

## Arriving

**Place title:** The Writing Table. (After page 5 comes free in Act V, a subtitle may add *For the next Keeper*. Before that, no screen at this table shows page 5's heading or names Hollis.)

**First visit** (A story from Willowbank):
> Beside the Keeper's Desk stands a long writing table. It holds paper strips, a pot of glue and a red pencil. Drafts from all over town wait in a tray.
>
> "Writing is mostly rewriting," says the librarian. "Everyone at this table gets a second go."

**What you already know (recall card, links to `#post/letters`):** You helped letters say what a reader needed. Here the drafts are longer, with paragraphs.

**Every bench screen (always shown):** These drafts were written for this table. Your own writing stays on paper, or you say it aloud.

**Grown-up panel (always available):** This page practices how writing is built and improved: paragraphs, headings, linking words, revising for a reader, short research, quoting a story, choosing a form and using a technique on purpose. The screen only ever works with drafts we wrote. Your child's own writing happens on paper or aloud, and you are its reader. Nothing your child writes is typed, kept or marked. When you read it, try asking "Who is this for, and what do they need?" before you mention spelling. Many writing choices are matters of taste; the page keys only what a stated reader or purpose decides, and says "either works" for the rest.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. Blank pages (Act V, after page 5)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** The fifth page lifts off the baker's windowsill. It is blank, except for its heading: *For the next Keeper*.

**Short:**
> Hollis left blank pages for the next Keeper. The librarian offers one page of the town paper. What will you write, and who will read it?

**Longer:**
> The fifth page comes free on the baker's windowsill. Then another under it. All blank. Each has the same heading: *For the next Keeper*.
>
> "Hollis kept the flood post," says the librarian. "And they wrote things down. Now it's someone else's turn."
>
> "Write about lunch," says Pip.
>
> "Write about me," says Bramble.
>
> "Write for somebody," says Dot. "That's the trick. Know who's reading."
>
> The librarian taps the *Gazette*. "There's room for one page. A report, a story, a script, a letter. Your choice."
>
> {guide} pulls out a chair at the long writing table. "First version first," says {guide}. "Then we make it better."
>
> The magpie steals the red pencil and has to give it back.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> Most writers plan, draft, revise and edit, and then do some of it again. A first version is supposed to be rough. Your page for the next Keeper keeps a plan, a first version, one change you made for your reader, and one honest limit.

### Scene 2. Pip's wall of words (Act V, the newspaper path)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** Pip has written a whole piece about the bakery. It is one enormous paragraph. Dot wants to know when it opens.

**Short:**
> Pip's piece is one long block. Dot can't find the opening times. Paragraphs and headings help a reader find what they need.

**Longer:**
> Pip holds up his page. It is covered from edge to edge. No gaps at all.
>
> "It's about the bakery," he says. "Everything about the bakery."
>
> Dot reads. Her eyes go down, and down. "When does it open?"
>
> "It says. Somewhere in the middle. After the cherry buns."
>
> "Let's cut it into strips," says {guide}. "One pile for what's for sale. One for opening times. One for how to find it."
>
> The piles grow. Each one becomes a paragraph. The librarian writes a heading over each.
>
> Dot reads it again. "Opens at seven," she says, finding it at once. "Good."
>
> "It's the same words," says Pip.
>
> "Same words," says the librarian. "Better rooms."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> A paragraph is a group of sentences about one thing. In a report, a heading names what a section is about, so a reader can jump straight to it. In a story, a new paragraph often starts when the time, the place or the speaker changes.

### Scene 3. Meanwhile, at the mill (Act V, the newspaper path)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** Dot writes up market day for the paper. Two things happened at once, in two places. How do you tell a reader that?

**Short:**
> Dot's report jumps from the square to the mill. The reader gets lost. One opening phrase, "Meanwhile, at the mill," fixes it.

**Longer:**
> Dot's report starts at the market. The baker is selling buns. Pip is counting coins.
>
> The next paragraph says: *The miller's cat stole a fish.*
>
> "Wait," says Pip. "Our cat? At the market?"
>
> "At the mill," says Dot. "At the same time."
>
> "Then tell the reader," says the librarian. "Start the paragraph with a signpost."
>
> Dot tries *Later,*. "No. It wasn't later."
>
> She tries *Meanwhile, at the mill,*. She reads it aloud. The reader jumps across town, and knows the clock hasn't moved.
>
> "That's two jobs in four words," says {guide}.
>
> "And one comma," says Dot.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> Words at the start of a paragraph can tell the reader when (later that day, meanwhile), where (nearby, beyond the bridge) or which point comes next (secondly, finally). In British schools these are called adverbials.

### Scene 4. Bramble's red pencil (Act V, the newspaper path)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** Bramble offers to edit Pip's notice for visitors. She cuts his favorite sentence. Pip is not pleased.

**Short:**
> Bramble cuts Pip's line about his lunch. Visitors don't need it. She also swaps one nice word for another. That part is just taste.

**Longer:**
> Bramble reads Pip's notice with the red pencil held high.
>
> *Visitors are welcome at the bakery. I had two buns for lunch today.*
>
> Slash. The second sentence is gone.
>
> "That was my best one!" says Pip.
>
> "Who's this notice for?" says Bramble.
>
> Pip thinks. "Visitors."
>
> "Do visitors need your lunch?"
>
> Pip says nothing, which means no.
>
> Then Bramble changes *lovely bread* to *delicious bread*.
>
> "Why?" says Pip.
>
> "I like it better."
>
> {guide} looks at both. "Either works. The cut helped the reader. That swap is just Bramble."
>
> Bramble considers this. "The cut was right, though."
>
> "The cut was right," says Pip, sadly.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> Revising changes what writing says or how it is built, so it works for its reader. Editing fixes spelling, punctuation and grammar. Some changes are needed for a reader. Others are only taste. Both kinds are allowed; it helps to know which is which.

### Scene 5. Show me where (Act V, the newspaper path)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** The paper has a story corner. Pip writes that the goat in the well should have looked first. The librarian asks, "Which line shows it?"

**Short:**
> Pip has a point about a fable. The librarian wants the exact words from the story that show it. Point, quote, then explain.

**Longer:**
> Pip's story-corner piece is one line long. *The goat should have looked first.*
>
> "Good point," says the librarian. "Can a reader check it?"
>
> "They could read the fable."
>
> "Or you could show them. Find the line."
>
> Pip runs a paw down the fable. He stops. "Here. 'The goat jumps without a second thought.'"
>
> "Put that in, inside quotation marks," says the librarian. "Then tell the reader what it shows."
>
> Pip writes. *This shows she didn't think about getting out.*
>
> Dot reads all three sentences. "Now I believe you."
>
> "You believed me before," says Pip.
>
> "Now I can see why," says Dot.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> When you write about a story, a point is stronger with evidence. Quote the exact words, inside quotation marks, and say where they come from. Then explain how the words show your point. Readers can check it for themselves.

### Scene 6. Who wants the best seat? (Act V, the newspaper path, Keeper's Desk)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** Bramble writes an advert for the paper: the show is going up to Vadoalto. Every line is a question. Pip wants to answer them all.

**Short:**
> Bramble's advert asks three questions that start the same way. Nobody is meant to answer. They are there to make readers want to come.

**Longer:**
> Bramble pins her advert to the writing table.
>
> *Who wants a dragon that juggles? Who wants a fox that sings? Who wants the best seat?*
>
> "Me," says Pip. "Me. And me."
>
> "You're not supposed to answer," says Bramble.
>
> "Then why did you ask?"
>
> "Three questions," says {guide}. "Each starts with *Who wants*. They aren't really questions. They're a drumroll."
>
> Dot counts. "A question, a repeat, and three of them."
>
> "Three tricks in one advert," says Bramble.
>
> "One trick would do," says Pip.
>
> The librarian shrugs. "Either works. Bramble likes a drumroll."
>
> Ember reads the advert and buys a ticket, which settles nothing.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> A rhetorical question is asked for effect, not for an answer. Starting several lines with the same words is called anaphora. A group of three is called a tricolon, or the rule of three. Writers choose them on purpose, for a reader, and a plain sentence is sometimes better.

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer; worked-example steps appear one tap at a time. Every card is **A true thing about language**; drafts in the worked examples are **A story from Willowbank**.

**Card 1. One paragraph, one main thing**
> A paragraph is a group of sentences about one main thing. Writers usually start a new paragraph when the topic, the time, the place or the speaker changes.

Worked example: *Dot's market day.*
1. "At dawn, Dot opened the post office. She sorted the letters."
2. "By noon, the square was full of stalls."
3. The time and the place both changed. New paragraph.

**Card 2. Headings help readers find things**
> In a report, a heading names what the next paragraph is about. A reader can jump straight to the part they need.

Worked example: *Pip's bakery report.*
1. Group the sentences: what's for sale, opening times, finding it.
2. Each group becomes a paragraph.
3. Write a heading over each one.

**Card 3. Signposts between paragraphs**
> Words at the start of a paragraph can tell the reader when, where, or which point is next: later that day, beyond the bridge, secondly.

Worked example: *Three kinds of signpost.*
1. When: Later that day, ... · Meanwhile, ...
2. Where: Nearby, ... · Beyond the bridge, ...
3. Which point: Firstly, ... · Secondly, ... · Finally, ...

**Card 4. Meanwhile or later?**
> "Meanwhile" can mean at the same time. "Later" means after. Check when the new paragraph happens before you choose.

Worked example: *Dot's report.*
1. Paragraph 1: the market, in the morning.
2. Paragraph 2: the mill, the same morning.
3. Same time, new place: "Meanwhile, at the mill, ..."

**Card 5. Holding writing together**
> Writing holds together when the reader can tell how the parts connect. Repeat a key word, or use a word that means the same thing.

Worked example: *The parcel.*
1. "Pip found a parcel by the bridge."
2. "The package was wet."
3. "The package" means the same parcel. "The" tells us it's the one we know.

**Card 6. Who is "it"?**
> Words like it, she and they point back to someone or something. If two things could fit, the reader gets lost. Name the thing instead.

Worked example: *The bun and the plate.*
1. "Pip set the bun on the plate. Then it slid off the table."
2. "It" could be the bun or the plate.
3. Fix: "Then the bun slid off the table."

**Card 7. Joining paragraphs by meaning**
> Some openers show how two paragraphs connect: "On the other hand" for a contrast, "As a consequence" for a result, "In addition" for one more point.

Worked example: *The market roof.*
1. Paragraph 1: the new roof keeps the stalls dry.
2. Paragraph 2: it cost more than the town planned.
3. A downside after a good point: "On the other hand, ..."

**Card 8. Leaving words out**
> If the reader can fill in the missing words, you can leave them out. This is called ellipsis. It saves repeating yourself.

Worked example: *Pip and Dot.*
1. "Pip wanted to see the show, and Dot wanted to see the show too."
2. The reader can fill in "wanted to see the show."
3. "Pip wanted to see the show, and Dot did too."

**Card 9. Revising and editing**
> Revising changes what writing says or how it's built, for its reader. Editing fixes spelling, punctuation and grammar. Writers do both.

Worked example: *Pip's notice.*
1. Moving the opening times to the top: revising.
2. Adding "the blue door" so visitors can find it: revising.
3. Changing "bred" to "bread": editing.

**Card 10. Who is reading?**
> Before you change anything, ask: who is reading, and what do they need? A sentence that doesn't help this reader can go. Some changes are just taste.

Worked example: *Bramble's red pencil.*
1. The notice is for visitors who want to find the bakery.
2. "I had two buns for lunch" doesn't help them. Cut it.
3. "Lovely" or "delicious"? Either works.

**Card 11. A short research project**
> Choose a question your sources can answer. Read the sources. Take short notes, a few words each. Sort the notes under headings. Then write.

Worked example: *Frogs in the millpond.*
1. Question: when does spawn appear in the millpond?
2. Note: "spawn: late winter or spring."
3. Sort it under the heading "When spawn appears."

**Card 12. Point, quote, explain**
> Say your point. Give the exact words from the text, inside quotation marks. Then explain how those words show your point.

Worked example: *The goat in the well.*
1. Point: the goat doesn't think ahead.
2. Quote: she "jumps without a second thought."
3. Explain: she never asks how she'll get out.

**Card 13. The form fits the job**
> Choose a form for its job. A script gives actors their lines. Notes for a talk are short reminders. A report uses headings. A letter usually speaks to one reader.

Worked example: *Changing a story line into a script line.*
1. Story: Pip yawned and said, "Is it supper yet?"
2. The speaker's name comes first: PIP:
3. The action often goes in brackets: PIP: (yawns) Is it supper yet?

**Card 14. Techniques for effect**
> A rhetorical question, a group of three, a repeated opening and a contrast are techniques. Use one on purpose, for a reader. Sometimes plain is better.

Worked example: *Bramble's advert.*
1. "Who wants a dragon that juggles?" A question nobody answers.
2. "Who wants ..." three times: a repeated opening.
3. Three questions in a row: a group of three.

## Benches

### (a) Paragraph Sort (Seedling)

**Header:** Sort the sentences into paragraphs. Each paragraph keeps to one main thing.

**Three trays: Pip's report, "Visit the Bakery"** (A story from Willowbank). Reader card: *For visitors to Willowbank.*
- Heading **What's for sale:** The baker makes seed bread and soft rolls. · The cherry buns are sticky and sweet. · The crusts are dark and crunchy.
- Heading **Opening times:** The bakery opens at seven. · It closes when the bread runs out. · On Sundays it stays shut.
- Heading **Finding the bakery:** The bakery is on the square, next to the post office. · Look for the blue door. · If you smell bread, you are close.

The nine strips appear mixed. Feedback when a strip sits in the wrong tray: Read the heading. Is this sentence about that?

**Order the paragraphs: Dot's market day** (A story from Willowbank). Three paragraphs, shown mixed:
- By noon, the square was full of stalls. The baker sold out of buns, and Pip helped him count the coins.
- At dawn, Dot opened the post office. She sorted the letters into neat piles.
- That evening, the stalls packed up. Dot walked home under the lanterns.

Header: Put Dot's day in order. Look at the first words of each paragraph.
Done line: Dawn, noon, evening. The first words told you the order.

**Mark the break:** Tap where a new paragraph should start. Each block needs one break.
- Block 1: (1) At dawn, Dot opened the post office. (2) She sorted the morning letters. (3) Pip came in for stamps. (4) By noon, the square was full of stalls. (5) The baker sold out of buns. Break: before 4. *The time and the place change.*
- Block 2: "Can I have a bun?" asked Pip. "Not until noon," said the baker. Break: before "Not until noon". *A new person speaks.*
- Block 3: (1) The mill has a big wooden wheel. (2) Water from the stream turns it. (3) The flood post stands beside the stream. (4) It shows how high the water has risen. Break: before 3. *The topic changes from the wheel to the flood post.*

Feedback for a break in the wrong place: Read on. What stays the same here, and where does it change?

### (b) The Linking Word (Sapling)

**Header:** Pick the opener that tells the reader what changes.

Each join shows the end of one paragraph, a "What changes" line, and three openers. Joins 1 and 2 use the openers of B1 and B2, in the same order.

- **Join 1.** End of paragraph 1: *Dot sorted letters all morning.* What changes: the next paragraph is at the mill, the same morning.
- **Join 2.** End of paragraph 1: *Pip ran onto Willow Bridge.* What changes: the next paragraph is on the far side, the same afternoon.
- **Join 3.** End of paragraph 1: *The first reason to visit the market is the bread.* What changes: the next paragraph gives the second reason. Openers: Nearby, · Secondly, ✔ · Meanwhile,
- **Join 4.** End of paragraph 1: *The third reason is the music.* What changes: the next paragraph gives the last reason. Openers: Finally, ✔ · Firstly, · Later that day,

**Feedback, any opener that doesn't fit:** Read the "What changes" line. Does your opener say that?

### (c) Holding It Together (Sapling, Keeper's Desk)

**Header:** Make the writing hold together, so the reader can follow.

**Same thing, new words (Sapling).** Tap every word that means the parcel.
> Pip found a parcel by the bridge. The package was wet. He carried it to the bakery, and the baker dried the bundle on the windowsill.

Answer: parcel, package, it, bundle. Done line: Four words, one parcel. The reader never loses it.

**Who is "it"? (Sapling).** Tap the word the reader could mix up, then choose a fix.
> Pip set the bun on the plate. Then it slid off the table.

Fixes: Then it slid off the edge of the table. · Then that one slid off the table. · Then the bun slid off the table. ✔
Feedback for the other fixes: The reader still can't tell which one slid.

**Join by meaning (Keeper's Desk).** Pick the opener for paragraph 2. The roof and the rain are C2 and C3, with their choices. Then:
- Paragraph 1: the show has a juggling dragon. Paragraph 2 gives one more thing to enjoy: a singing fox. Openers: As a consequence, · On the other hand, · In addition, ✔

**Leave it out (Keeper's Desk).** Which shorter version keeps the meaning? The first pair is C4, with its choices. Then:
- "Where are you going?" "I'm going to the mill." Versions: "To the mill." ✔ · "I'm going there." · "Going."

Feedback for a version that cuts too much: The reader can't fill that gap. Put a word back.

### (g) Form and Effect (Keeper's Desk)

**Header:** Pick a form for the job. Then pick a technique, on purpose.

**Which form? (match).** Match each job to a form.
- Actors need lines and actions to perform the story. → a script
- The librarian will speak at the meeting and needs short reminders. → notes for a talk
- Visitors want facts they can find fast. → a report with headings
- You want to thank Nuria for the reed seeds. → a letter

Feedback for a wrong match: What does this reader need to do with it?

**Story to script.** Change a story line into a script line (G2). The speaker's name comes first; actions go in brackets.

**Spot the technique.** Bramble's advert (A story from Willowbank):
> Who wants a dragon that juggles? Who wants a fox that sings? Who wants the best seat?

Tap: the repeated opening (each "Who wants"); a question nobody is meant to answer (any line); the group of three (all three lines together).

**Try one on paper (off-screen).** Take one plain sentence from Pip's bakery report. Rewrite it with one technique. Read both to a grown-up. Which suits a visitor better? Either answer can be right.

**Either works card:** Once the job is done, choosing a technique is often taste. A plain sentence can be the strongest.

## Your Page (Act V; Sapling, Keeper's Desk)

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Paragraph:** a group of sentences about one main thing.
- **Heading:** a title over a part of a report that says what it is about.
- **Topic:** what a piece of writing, or a part of it, is about.
- **Signpost word (linking adverbial):** words at the start of a paragraph that tell the reader when, where, or which point.
- **Cohesion:** how a piece of writing holds together, so the reader can follow from part to part.
- **Pronoun:** a word like he, she, it or they that points back to someone or something already named.
- **Ellipsis:** leaving out words the reader can fill in. (The same word also names the dots ..., but here it means the missing words.)
- **Draft:** a version of a piece of writing that you will improve.
- **Revise:** change what writing says or how it is built, so it works for its reader.
- **Edit:** fix spelling, punctuation and grammar.
- **Reader card:** a card that says who will read a draft and what they need.
- **Source:** where information comes from: a book, a person, a record.
- **Note:** a few words that keep one fact.
- **Research question:** a question you try to answer from sources.
- **Quotation marks:** the marks " " around someone's exact words.
- **Evidence:** words from a text that show your point is true.
- **Form:** the kind of writing: a report, a story, a script, a letter.
- **Script:** writing for actors, with each speaker's name and their lines.
- **Stage direction:** an action in a script, often written in brackets.
- **Rhetorical question:** a question asked for effect, not for an answer.
- **Group of three (tricolon):** three words, phrases or sentences in a row.
- **Repeated opening (anaphora):** starting several lines or sentences with the same words.
- **Contrast:** setting one thing against its opposite.
- **Either works:** both versions are fine; choosing is taste.

## Grown-up panel: UK and US terms

**UK and US terms:** this page uses US terms. In British schools, "signpost words" are taught as adverbials, a period is a full stop, and quotation marks are inverted commas. This page calls ( ) "brackets"; many US classrooms say parentheses. Scripts set stage directions in brackets or italics; this page uses brackets. Starting a new paragraph for each new speaker is a widely taught custom for stories, not a grammar rule.
