# The Keeper of the Ways: story text

Companion to [spec 120](../specs/120-keeper-of-the-ways.md). Child-facing text for the build to use as written, after the same independent review as the spec. `{guide}` is the chosen companion's name. `{courier}` is the explorer's courier. Pip is a fox (he/him); Dot is a ladybug (she/her); Bramble is a girl witch (she/her); Hollis is a grey heron (they/them); Nuria is an otter (she/her). Willowbank, Willow Brook, the Sponge, Hollis and Vadoalto are fiction. Nothing here is scored.

Spanish and French text is a first draft for review by fluent readers. The Spanish uses *ustedes* throughout. Léon (kingfisher, he/him) keeps the ferry at Les Saules, the French town downstream.

## 1. The parcel

Shown once, on the garden home, after the storm film on a first visit.

> The rain has stopped. Everything drips.
>
> Pip is tugging at something wedged against the flowerpot. "It's a parcel," says Dot. "A very wet parcel."
>
> The label is smudged, but you can still read it: **To the Keeper of the Ways, Willowbank, by Willow Brook.**
>
> "Who's the Keeper of the Ways?" asks Pip.
>
> {guide} turns the parcel over. "I don't know. Somebody sent this a long way to find them."
>
> Inside, the pages are stuck together in one damp lump. "We dry them," says Dot. "Slowly. Nobody is peeling anything."

**Button:** Keep it safe
**After:** Pip puts the parcel in his satchel. He will check on it every few minutes.

**For an explorer who began before the parcel existed** (shown once, next visit to the garden home):

> Pip has been keeping something for you. "I found it after the storm," he says, "and I didn't open it. Mostly."

Then the parcel text above from "The label is smudged."

## 3. The Keeper's Pages

The almanac gains a section. **Heading:** The Keeper's Pages. **Under it:** *Drying on the bakery windowsill.* A page that hasn't come free shows its stuck edge and one word you can read, given below.

Each page has three versions: **Short** (with read-aloud), **Longer**, and **Hollis's page**, the original at the Keeper's Desk. In Hollis's page, `[smudged]` marks a word the water took. The desk invites the reader to guess the word from the rest of the page and to mark how sure they are.

### Page 1: The old map

**Edge word before it comes free:** *Sponge*

**When it comes free** (during the welcome-back supper):
> While everyone is eating, Dot slips out to check the windowsill. She comes back holding one page by its corners. "It came free," she says. "It's a map."

**Short:** An old map of our valley. The brook wanders through a big green patch called *the Sponge*. The orchard is there now.

**Longer:** It is a map of the whole valley, drawn by hand. Willow Brook doesn't run straight the way it does now. It wanders in loops through a wide green patch with tiny reeds drawn all over it. Someone has written *the Sponge* across the middle. Pip puts his paw on the spot. "That's where the orchard is," he says. "There's no Sponge there now." Dot looks at the map for a long time. "Then where did it go?"

**Hollis's page:**
> The valley, drawn in my second year of keeping. Willow Brook from the high ford to the mill. The Sponge holds the spring water and lets it go [smudged], so the mill boards are seldom needed. Reeds, frogs, a great many herons, one of them me. The bridge road runs on the dry side. Scale: one finger to a [smudged] walk.

**Guide line after reading:** "{guide}: A map is a picture of what was there when someone drew it. I wonder what changed."

### Page 2: The flood ledger

**Edge word:** *mark*

**When it comes free** (at the Act II celebration):
> The second page peels away on its own, the way a leaf comes off a puddle. It's a table of numbers.

**Short:** A list of floods. Each line says a year and how high the brook came up the mill's flood post. At the bottom: *Ask upstream.*

**Longer:** The page is a table. Each line has a year and a height, measured on the flood post at the mill, where the brook comes over its banks. The first floods are small and far apart. The later ones are higher, and closer together. At the very bottom, in tiny writing, Hollis has added two words: *Ask upstream.* "Upstream of what?" says Pip. "Upstream of us," says Dot. "Where the brook comes from."

**Hollis's page** (heights are above the brook's ordinary level):

| Year of my keeping | Height at the flood post |
| --- | --- |
| 4 | 40 cm |
| 15 | 60 cm |
| 27 | 120 cm |
| 31 | 80 cm |
| 34 | 120 cm |
| 36 | 140 cm |
| 38 | 100 cm |

> Years not written had no flood worth the ink. [smudged] the gaps grow shorter. Ask upstream.

**After the table, a new line the town adds:** The miller has scratched a fresh mark on the post, higher than any of Hollis's. *The storm: 160 cm.* The almanac offers: **Add the storm to the ledger.**

### Page 3: The Ways

**Edge word:** *ways*

**When it comes free** (at the end of Act III, while Nuria is visiting):
> Nuria is reading the drying pages over your shoulder when the third one comes free. She goes very still. "I know this writing," she says.

**Short:** A letter about *the Ways*: the paths between the river towns, and the paths the water takes. Nuria knows who wrote it.

**Longer:** The page is a letter. It says there are three Ways between Willowbank and the towns upstream: the river path, the bridge road and the high ford. Then it says there are other ways too: "the water's ways, which are where it goes when there is too much of it." Nuria puts her paw on the signature, a long thin H. "That's Hollis," she says. "Hollis taught me to read our flood post. Then they went to live by the sea."

**Hollis's page:**
> There are three Ways between Willowbank and Vadoalto: the river path, the bridge road and the high ford. Keep them open, even in winter, even when nobody seems to need them. Someone always does. There are also the water's ways, which are where it goes when there is too much of it. Those need keeping too. A town that closes them [smudged] finds out where the water goes instead. H.

### Page 4: The year of the fine harvest

**Edge word:** *harvest*

**When it comes free** (opening Act IV):
> The fourth page is short. Pip reads it out loud, then reads it again, slower.

**Short:** In the year the Sponge was drained, the orchard grew its best apples ever. Five years later came the first big flood.

**Longer:** Hollis wrote that in their twenty-second year of keeping, the town drained the Sponge to make new orchard fields, and straightened the brook so it would run past them quickly. That autumn the orchard had the best harvest anyone remembered. Five years later, the brook came 120 centimeters up the flood post. "So draining the Sponge caused the floods," says Pip. Bramble, who has been listening from the gate, snorts. "My hat and my boots both got wet that year too. Doesn't mean my hat did it."

**Hollis's page:**
> Year 22. The Sponge is drained and the brook cut straight past the new fields. The orchard keepers are very pleased and so, I admit, am I: the apples this autumn are [smudged]. Year 27. The brook to 120 at the post, the highest in my keeping. Wet years, or our doing? I have only my own post to go by. Vadoalto keeps a rain gauge. [smudged] someone should ask them.

### Page 5: For the next Keeper

**Edge word:** *next*

**When it comes free** (opening Act V):
> The rest of the pages come apart all at once. They're blank. Across the top of the first one, Hollis has written a heading and nothing else.

**Short and Longer:** *For the next Keeper.* The pages are empty. They're waiting for someone to fill them.

**Guide line:** "{guide}: Blank pages are the best kind of invitation."

### Page 6: Hollis's letter

Not in the parcel. Hollis brings it home at the end of Act VI and gives it to the explorer at supper. One version, with read-aloud.

> To whoever is keeping the record,
>
> I addressed this parcel to the Keeper of the Ways because I didn't know your name. I still don't, and that is all right.
>
> For forty years I read the flood post and walked the Ways. I wrote down what the water did, and I kept the paths open between our towns, because the brook doesn't stop at the edge of Willowbank and neither should we.
>
> My legs prefer the seaside now. So I wrapped up my pages and sent them home for someone to carry on. Then the storm came, and I thought they were lost.
>
> They found you anyway. Perhaps you mended a bridge, or wrote in the almanac, or carried a letter upstream. Perhaps you argued about the wall. Then you had already started.
>
> Anyone can be the Keeper of the Ways. The job belongs to whoever keeps the record and keeps the ways open. It's yours for as long as you want it. When someone new arrives, show them the post.
>
> Hollis

## 10. Archive pack outline: Valencia, 1957

Joshua chose this as the next archive pack (2026-10-23). This is an outline for the pack's own spec and source review. The review checked a bounded subset directly against official pages (below); the remaining historical claims and all archive tasks still need source/content review before child text is final. Dates marked *(check)* remain unresolved.

**Title:** *The River That Moved* / *El río que se mudó*

**Why it belongs:** Valencia had a terrible flood, then made two big choices. The city moved its river, and its people turned the empty riverbed into a garden. Act IV's argument asks what a town should do about its river; here is a real city that answered, in two parts, with people disagreeing along the way.

**Timeline for the pack:**

| When | What happened |
| --- | --- |
| 14 October 1957 | The Turia flooded Valencia, in two waves on the same day. Much of the city was under water. Many people died. |
| July 1958 | The national government chose the *Plan Sur*: dig a new channel for the river around the south of the city. |
| 23 December 1961 | A national law (Ley 81/1961, published in the BOE) made the plan official. This is a public-domain Spanish primary source. |
| 22 December 1969 | The new channel officially opened; work went on until about 1973. It is about 12 km long; sources give its width as 175, 200 or 250 m *(check)*. |
| 1966 | A city plan proposed building a road along the dry old riverbed. |
| 1970s | Neighbors campaigned for a park instead. The city cultural service records the Valencian slogan *El riu es nostre i el volem verd*: "The river is ours, and we want it green." Other variants need their own source if quoted. |
| 1978 or 1979 *(sources disagree)* | The city council decided the whole old riverbed would be a garden. The Keeper's Desk case turns this disagreement into a task. |
| 1981 | The architect Ricardo Bofill was asked to plan the park. |
| 1986 | The regional tourism authority dates the gardens' inauguration to 1986 and describes a 9 km route. This is an attributed account; it does not settle which individual sections opened when. |

**Short text (English):** In 1957, the river in Valencia flooded the city. Afterward, the city dug a new path for the river, around the outside. The old riverbed was left dry. Some people wanted a road there. Neighbors wanted a garden. Today it is a long, green park.

**Short text (Spanish):** En 1957, el río de Valencia inundó la ciudad. Después, la ciudad excavó un camino nuevo para el río, por fuera. El cauce viejo quedó seco. Algunas personas querían una carretera allí. Los vecinos querían un jardín. Hoy es un parque largo y verde.

**The slogan, for older learners:** The neighbors wrote it in Valencian, the language many people in Valencia speak alongside Spanish. In Spanish it would be *El cauce del Turia es nuestro y lo queremos verde.* Comparing the two is a small lesson in how close the languages are.

**Jobs, one per depth:**

| Depth | Job |
| --- | --- |
| Sprout | Two pictures of the same place: a river full of water, and a garden with paths and trees. "What changed?" |
| Seedling | Put four events in order: the flood, the new channel, the neighbors' campaign, the park opening. |
| Sapling | "The city did two things: it moved the river, and it made a garden. Which one protects the city from floods? Which one did the neighbors ask for?" (keeps evidence and purpose separate) |
| Keeper's Desk | Read short sources from both sides of the road-or-garden argument. Who wanted what, and why? Then: Willowbank's plans are the Wall, the Sponge and Some of Each. Valencia did something none of them do: it moved the river. Why might Willowbank be unable to do that? |

**Care with the hard part.** The flood killed people; official and unofficial counts differ widely. Child text says "many people died" once, plainly, with no number in the short version. The desk version may give the sourced figures with their disagreement, which is itself a lesson in sources. A grown-up note says that Valencia flooded again in 2024, that this pack is about 1957, and that it does not cover the recent flood; per spec 120, the pack does not use it.

**Keeper's Desk depth:** written in full as spec 118's Valencia case, `docs/content/118-valencia-1957-case.md` (PR #126). In it, Nuria's grandmother remembers the story.

**Bounded official-source check, 2026-10-23:** [Cultural València](https://cultural.valencia.es/es/conoce-el-jardin-del-turia/) directly supports the road proposal, campaign and quoted slogan. [Turisme Comunitat Valenciana](https://www.comunitatvalenciana.com/es/valencia/valencia/espacios-naturales/jardines-del-rio-turia) gives 1986 and the 9 km route. These two checks do not clear the full flood history or its bilingual teaching text. Facts are paraphrased; no images are reused.
