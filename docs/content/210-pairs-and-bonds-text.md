Companion to [spec 210](../specs/210-pairs-and-bonds.md). Use as written, after independent spec review and a math check. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion and is never gendered. Pip is he/him, Dot she/her, Bramble she/her (with her magpie), the baker he/him, the miller she/her. Real-world facts are in the [source audit](../source-audits/210-pairs-and-bonds.md); items still marked `[check]` stay off screen until cleared. Nothing here is scored or timed. ## Labels used on every card

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. The ducks come back (Act I, chapter 3: investigate and plant)

**Hook:** The storm blew the ducks off the pond. Now they are back, in odd places. Bramble says her line of ducks is bigger.

**Short:**
> Bramble's ducks stand in a long line. Pip's ducks huddle in a bunch. "Mine has more," says Bramble. Dot gets a rope.

**Longer:**
> The storm blew every duck off the pond. This morning they came back and stood about, looking offended.
>
> Bramble has lined some up along the bank. Pip has some in a huddle by the reeds.
>
> "My line goes all the way to the footbridge," says Bramble. "So I have more."
>
> "Mine are squashed together," says Pip. "Squashed things count double."
>
> "They don't," says Dot. She ties a loop of rope from one of Bramble's ducks to one of Pip's. Then another. Then another.
>
> At the end, every duck has a partner. Nobody is left over.
>
> "Oh," says Bramble. "The same."
>
> "Spreading out doesn't add a duck," says {guide}. "It just adds space."
>
> Bramble's magpie walks along the line, counting with its beak. It agrees with the rope.

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> Matching works before counting does. Pair one with one. If one side has some left over, it has more. If nothing is left over, the groups are the same, however they are spread out.

### Scene 2. The stones that make ten (Act I, chapter 4: mend the village)

**Hook:** The storm knocked the stepping stones out of line. The old stones have dots carved on top, and they came in pairs.

**Short:**
> Pip lifts a stone with seven dots. "Its partner had three," says Dot. "Seven and three. That's how the old stones went."

**Longer:**
> The stepping stones by the footbridge lie tumbled in the mud. Each has dots carved on top.
>
> "They went in pairs," says the miller, leaning on her shovel. "Every pair made ten. My grandmother said you could count your way across."
>
> Pip heaves up a stone with seven dots. "Where's the other half of ten?"
>
> Dot draws ten nests in the mud. She puts a pebble in seven of them. "Three empty. Find the stone with three."
>
> Pip finds a stone with four dots. "Close enough?"
>
> "Then you'd have eleven," says Dot. "One pebble with no nest."
>
> He finds the three. The two stones sit side by side, seven and three, ten dots in all.
>
> "Ten," says Pip. "Next!"
>
> There are a lot of stones.

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> The carved stones are made up. The idea is real: every number from 1 to 9 has one partner that makes ten. 7 and 3, 6 and 4, 9 and 1. Knowing partners of ten makes bigger sums easier.

### Scene 3. Crusts for the ducks (Act I, chapter 1: find and feed)

**Hook:** The baker brings a basket of crusts. Pip thinks the sum on Dot's slate is written backwards.

**Short:**
> Dot writes 8 = 5 + 3 on her slate. "Backwards!" says Pip. "The answer goes at the end." Dot points at the ducks.

**Longer:**
> The baker brings a basket of crusts. "Not for you," he tells Pip. "For the ducks."
>
> Eight crusts. Five ducks paddle up, then three more.
>
> Dot writes on her slate: **8 = 5 + 3**.
>
> "That's backwards," says Pip. "The answer goes at the end."
>
> "This sign means *is the same as*," says Dot. "Eight crusts is the same as five ducks and three ducks."
>
> She hands out crusts, one for each duck. None left. No duck without.
>
> "So 5 + 3 = 8 is true," says Pip, "and 8 = 5 + 3 is true too?"
>
> "Both. Same amount on each side."
>
> Pip eats a crumb that fell off. Nobody counts that one.

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> The equals sign says two things are the same amount. It does not mean "the answer comes next". 8 = 5 + 3, 5 + 3 = 8 and 5 + 3 = 4 + 4 are all true.

## Teach cards

Each card is under 30 words. Worked steps appear one tap at a time.

**T1. Match to compare** (A true thing about math)
> Tie a rope from each duck to one crust. Any ducks left over? Then there are more ducks. Nothing left over? The same.
Worked example: 1. Rope each duck to a crust. 2. Two crusts have no duck. 3. More crusts, fewer ducks.

**T2. Spread out is not more** (A true thing about math)
> Spreading a line out makes it longer. It does not add a duck. Match or count to be sure.
Worked example: 1. Six ducks in a line, six in a bunch. 2. Rope them in pairs. 3. None left over: the same.

**T3. Count each one once** (A true thing about math)
> Tap a duck and move it to a nest as you say its number. Moved ducks are done. The last number you say tells how many.
Worked example: 1. Tap, move: one, two, three. 2. All ducks moved. 3. Last number: three ducks.

**T4. Ten and some more** (A true thing about math)
> Fill a house of ten nests first. Then count the extras. Fourteen is ten and four. We write the ten first: 14.
Worked example: 1. Ten nests full. 2. Four ducks outside. 3. Ten and four: 14.

**T5. Number words to twenty** (A true thing about math)
> Fourteen is "four" and "teen". We say the four first but write the ten first: 14. Eleven and twelve are special words to learn.
Worked example: 1. six + teen = sixteen. 2. Write the ten, then the six. 3. 16.

**T6. Count back and count in threes** (A true thing about math)
> Count back on the stones: 20, 19, 18. A duck can hop every third stone: 0, 3, 6, 9.
Worked example: 1. Start at 0. 2. Hop three stones: 3. 3. Hop again: 6, then 9, 12.

**T7. Tens from any lily pad** (A true thing about math)
> On the Lily Square, a straight jump down is ten more. The ones stay the same. The tens go up one.
Worked example: 1. Start on 7. 2. Jump down: 17. 3. Down again: 27. The 7 stays.

**T8. One number, many pairs** (A true thing about math)
> Six ducks on two lily pads can split many ways. Move one duck across. One pad loses one, one gains one. Still six.
Worked example: 1. 0 and 6. 2. 1 and 5. 3. 2 and 4. 4. 3 and 3.

**T9. Partners of ten** (A true thing about math)
> A duck house has ten nests. Count the ducks, then the empty nests. Together they make ten.
Worked example: 1. 7 ducks. 2. 3 empty nests. 3. 7 + 3 = 10, and 10 − 7 = 3.

**T10. One family, four facts** (A true thing about math)
> Three numbers can make four facts. Know one, and you know the others.
Worked example: 1. 8 and 5 make 13. 2. 8 + 5 = 13 and 5 + 8 = 13. 3. 13 − 5 = 8 and 13 − 8 = 5.

**T11. Plus, minus, equals** (A true thing about math)
> + means join. − means take away. = means "is the same as". So 8 = 5 + 3 is true.
Worked example: 1. Read 8 = 5 + 3 aloud. 2. "8 is the same as 5 plus 3." 3. Rope them: none left over.

**T12. Swap to add, never to take away** (A true thing about math)
> Swap the lily pads: 3 + 5 and 5 + 3 are both 8. Take-away cannot swap: 5 − 3 is 2, but from 3 you run out.
Worked example: 1. 3 + 5 = 8. 2. Swap: 5 + 3 = 8. 3. 5 − 3 = 2. Try 3 − 5: not enough ducks.

**T13. Find the missing number** (A true thing about math)
> 8 + ? = 13. Count up from 8 to 13. Then check by taking away: 13 − 5 = 8.
Worked example: 1. 8 to 10: two hops. 2. 10 to 13: three hops. 3. 2 + 3 = 5.

**T14. Three pads: look for a ten** (A true thing about math)
> Adding three numbers? Look for two that make ten. Add those first. Then add the last one.
Worked example: 1. 4 + 7 + 6. 2. 4 + 6 = 10. 3. 10 + 7 = 17.

**T15. Draw the story** (A true thing about math)
> Lay the story out as a duck strip: the whole on top, the parts below. Which part is missing?
Worked example: 1. 12 ducks in all. 2. 8 stayed. 3. Missing part: 12 − 8 = 4 flew off.

**T16. Jump to the next ten** (A true thing about math)
> 26 + 7: jump to 30 first. That uses 4 of the 7. Then 3 more.
Worked example: 1. 26 + 4 = 30. 2. 7 is 4 and 3. 3. 30 + 3 = 33.

**T17. Use a fact you know** (A true thing about math)
> Know 7 + 7 = 14? Then 7 + 8 is one more: 15. Know 6 + 4 = 10? Then 6 tens and 4 tens make 10 tens: 100.
Worked example: 1. 7 + 7 = 14. 2. 8 is one more than 7. 3. 7 + 8 = 15.

## History cards

**H1. Numbers on your fingers** (A true thing from history; Seedling and up)
> About 1,300 years ago, a monk in England named Bede wrote a book about time and calendars. It begins by showing how to make numbers with finger shapes.
Keeper's note:
> Bede finished the book, *The Reckoning of Time*, in the year 725. Its first chapter shows finger shapes for numbers. Most accounts say two hands could show any number up to 9,999. He did not invent finger numbers; people already used them before him.

**H2. Partners of ten on the soroban** (A true thing from history; Sapling and Keeper's Desk)
> In Japan, people still calculate on a bead frame called a soroban. A top bead is worth 5. Each bottom bead is worth 1.
Keeper's note:
> Soroban users learn partners of ten. To add 9 when the beads are full, they add 10 on the next rod and take 1 away, because 9 is 10 − 1. The soroban came from the Chinese abacus, the suanpan. Today's soroban has one top bead and four bottom beads on each rod.

## Problem sets

Twenty-one three-choice items put the right answer at A seven times, B seven times and C seven times.

### Set A. More, fewer, the same (Sprout and Seedling)

**A1 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Every duck has a rope to a crust. Two crusts have none. Which is true?
- A. There are more crusts than ducks. *(right)*
- B. There are more ducks, because ducks are bigger. *(tests: bigger things mean more)*
- C. They are the same. Every duck has a crust. *(tests: ignoring the leftovers)*
Hint: Look for crusts with no rope.
Explanation: Two crusts are left over, so there are more crusts.
Feedback A: Yes. The leftovers tell you which has more.
Feedback B: Size doesn't change how many. Look at the leftovers.
Feedback C: Every duck has one. But two crusts have no duck.

**A2 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Bramble's ducks make a long line. Pip's huddle. All are paired. Which has more?
- A. Bramble's, because her line is longer. *(tests: longer means more)*
- B. Pip's, because his look crowded. *(tests: crowded means more)*
- C. The same. Every duck has a partner. *(right)*
Hint: Is any duck left without a partner?
Explanation: Nobody is left over, so the groups are the same.
Feedback A: Longer adds space, not ducks. Look at the ropes.
Feedback B: Crowded ducks take less space. Are any left over?
Feedback C: Yes. No leftovers means the same.

**A3 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Ducks are scattered everywhere. How can you count without missing one?
- A. Look at them all and say a number. *(tests: guessing for counting)*
- B. Move each duck to a nest as you count it. *(right)*
- C. Count very fast before they swim off. *(tests: speed over care)*
Hint: How can you tell which ducks are done?
Explanation: Moved ducks are done. You count each one once.
Feedback A: A look can be close. Moving each duck makes it sure.
Feedback B: Yes. Moved means counted.
Feedback C: Fast counting skips ducks. Slow and sure is fine.

**A4 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: One island duck has no partner. How can you make the groups the same?
- A. Add one duck to the raft. *(right)*
- B. Add one duck to the island. *(tests: adding to the bigger group)*
- C. Spread out the raft ducks. *(tests: longer means more)*
Hint: Which side needs one more?
Explanation: The raft needs one more duck to match.
Feedback A: Yes. Now every duck has a partner.
Feedback B: Then two island ducks have no partner.
Feedback C: Spreading adds space, not ducks.

**A5 (Seedling, number)**
Prompt: 9 ducks, 6 crusts. How many more ducks than crusts?
Answer: 3.
Worked steps: 1. Rope 6 ducks to 6 crusts. 2. Count the ducks left over: 3. 3. 9 is 3 more than 6.
Tests: 15 (adding both groups); 9 (giving the bigger group).
Hint: Rope them. How many ducks are left over?
Explanation: The leftovers show how many more.

**A6 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: 8 ducks, 5 crusts. Which sentence is true?
- A. 5 is more than 8. *(tests: reversing more and fewer)*
- B. 8 is fewer than 5. *(tests: mixing up the words)*
- C. 8 is more than 5. *(right)*
Hint: Rope them. Which side has some left over?
Explanation: Three ducks are left over, so 8 is more than 5.
Feedback A: 5 comes before 8 when you count. It is fewer.
Feedback B: Fewer means less. Which side ran out first?
Feedback C: Yes. 8 is more than 5.

### Set D. Plus, minus and "the same as" (Seedling and Sapling)

**D1 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Pip says 8 = 5 + 3 is backwards. Is it?
- A. Yes. The answer must come last. *(tests: = means "answer next")*
- B. No. 8 is the same as 5 + 3. *(right)*
- C. Yes. 8 is bigger than 5. *(tests: comparing one part with the whole)*
Hint: Read = as "is the same as".
Explanation: 8 is the same amount as 5 and 3 together. It's true.
Feedback A: = means "is the same as". Either side can come first.
Feedback B: Yes. Same amount on each side.
Feedback C: 8 matches 5 and 3 together, not just 5.

**D2 (Seedling, true or not true)**
Prompt: Rope each side. Is each sentence true?
- 5 + 3 = 4 + 4 *(true: 8 and 8)*
- 4 + 1 = 3 + 3 *(not true: 5 and 6)*
- 7 = 7 *(true)*
- 6 = 2 + 3 *(not true: 6 and 5)*
- 5 + 0 = 5 *(true: adding nothing keeps 5)*
Tests: = means "answer next"; anything with + and = looks true; adding zero must change the number.
Hint: Work out each side. Are they the same?
Explanation: True when both sides are the same amount.

**D3 (Seedling, build a sentence)**
Prompt: Six ducks. Two fly away. Build the sentence.
Answer: 6 − 2 = 4.
Tests: 6 + 2 = 8 (joining instead of taking away); 2 − 6 (take-away in the wrong order).
Hint: Did ducks join or leave?
Explanation: Two left, so take away: 6 − 2 = 4.

**D4 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: 3 + 5 = 5 + 3. Is 5 − 3 the same as 3 − 5?
- A. Yes. You can always swap. *(tests: swapping take-away)*
- B. No. 3 − 5 is 8. *(tests: adding when the order changes)*
- C. No. 5 − 3 is 2. From 3 you run out. *(right)*
Hint: Try taking 5 ducks from 3 ducks.
Explanation: Adding can swap. Taking away can't.
Feedback A: Swapping works for adding. Try 3 − 5 with ducks.
Feedback B: 8 is 3 + 5. Taking away 5 from 3 runs out.
Feedback C: Yes. Take-away can't swap.

**D5 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: 8 + ? = 13. What is the missing number?
Answer: 5.
Worked steps: 1. Count up from 8 to 10: 2. 2. Then 10 to 13: 3. 3. 2 + 3 = 5. Check: 13 − 5 = 8.
Tests: 21 (adding 8 and 13); 13 (writing the whole).
Hint: Count up from 8 to 13.
Explanation: 8 + 5 = 13, so 5 is missing.

**D6 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Start on stone 15. Hop forward 7, then back 7. Where are you?
- A. 15 *(right)*
- B. 22 *(tests: forgetting the back hop)*
- C. 29 *(tests: treating back as forward)*
Hint: What does the back hop do to the forward hop?
Explanation: Back 7 undoes forward 7. You land where you started.
Feedback A: Yes. Back the same undoes it.
Feedback B: That's after the forward hop. Now hop back.
Feedback C: Back hops go down the numbers.

### Set E. Pond stories (Seedling and Sapling)

**E1 (Seedling, number)**
Prompt: 7 ducks swim. 5 more land. How many now?
Answer: 12.
Worked steps: 1. 7 + 3 = 10. 2. 5 is 3 and 2. 3. 10 + 2 = 12.
Tests: 2 (taking away); 11 (slipping by one past ten).
Hint: Make ten from 7 first.
Explanation: Ducks joined, so the group grew: 7 + 5 = 12.

**E2 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: 12 ducks. Some fly off. 8 are left. How many flew?
- A. 20 *(tests: adding the two numbers seen)*
- B. 4 *(right)*
- C. 8 *(tests: giving the number left)*
Hint: Lay out the duck strip. Which part is missing?
Explanation: 12 is 8 and 4. So 4 flew off.
Feedback A: Ducks left, so there are fewer than 12.
Feedback B: Yes. 8 + 4 = 12.
Feedback C: 8 stayed. How many went?

**E3 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: 15 ducks. 9 are brown. The rest are white. How many white?
- A. 6 *(right)*
- B. 24 *(tests: adding the two numbers seen)*
- C. 7 *(tests: counting the start number as a hop)*
Hint: Count up from 9 to 15.
Explanation: 9 + 6 = 15, so 6 are white.
Feedback A: Yes. 9 brown and 6 white make 15.
Feedback B: All the ducks together make 15, not more.
Feedback C: Check: 9 + 7 is 16. Count the hops again.

**E4 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Pip counts 13 stones. Dot counts 8 ducks. How many more stones?
- A. 21 *(tests: "more" means add)*
- B. 13 *(tests: giving the bigger number)*

Explanation: 8 matched, 5 left over. 13 is 5 more than 8.
Feedback A: "More" here asks for the difference. Rope them.
Feedback B: 13 is all the stones. How many have no duck?

**F1 (Sapling, number)**

## Key terms (glossary)

- **more**: the group that still has some left when you match.
- **fewer**: the group that runs out first when you match.
- **the same**: nothing is left over when you match.
- **count**: say one number for each thing, once each.
- **teen**: means "and ten", as in four-teen: ten and four.
- **pair**: two numbers that make a whole.
- **partners of ten**: two numbers that make 10, like 7 and 3.
- **whole**: all of them together.
- **part**: some of the whole.
- **plus (+)**: join.
- **minus (−)**: take away.
- **equals (=)**: is the same as.
- **fact family**: three numbers and the four facts they make.
- **missing number**: the one you have to find, shown as ?.
- **number line**: numbers in order, one step apart, like the stepping stones.

## Off-screen invitation (optional, grown-up-led)

**Ten nests in an egg box** (Sprout and Seedling): use a ten-cup egg box or draw ten circles on paper. Put dried beans or buttons in some. Ask how many are in and how many are empty. Swap roles. *Grown-up note: use large buttons or beans with young children (small parts), and stay close by.*

**Write the numbers** (Seedling): write 0 to 20 together on paper, slowly, saying each one. Teen numbers start with the 1. Mirror-writing a digit is normal at this stage; just show the right way again. *Grown-up note: the screen uses tiles and never checks handwriting.*

**Finger numbers** (Seedling and up): after card H1, show each other numbers on your fingers in your own way, then try showing 7 with as few fingers moving as you can.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** comparing groups by matching (more, fewer, the same), including when a line is spread out; counting up to 20 objects so each counts once, sending an exact number, and counting back, in threes and in tens from any number; reading numerals and number words to 20; splitting numbers to 10 into pairs and partners of ten, then fact families to 20; +, − and = read as "is the same as"; showing addition and subtraction with ducks, strips, stones and sentences; word problems with the missing number in different places; adding in any order (not taking away); grouping three numbers to make a ten; finding a missing number by counting up and checking with the inverse; quick sums within 20 by using facts already known, and jumps to the next ten.

**What it does not claim:** the ducks, stones and stories are made up. It never times answers or counts how fast. It does not check handwriting. Ten Pairs is a cooperative memory game, not a test. Repeated play is practice; no level, star or "mastered" label comes from it.

**Which parts revisit other places:** teen numbers as ten and some ones are taught at the Bundle Exchange; counting a small group by pointing is part of the gathering activity; = as "the same mass" is the Balance Stall; making ten for 8 + 5 and 17 − 8 is in the Number Garden's Picnic Clearing; Shutters and Ribbon Rows use sums and take-away in games. This pond adds matching, partners of ten, = by pairing, fact families and stories.
