## Story scenes

### Scene 1. Ribbons for the chairs (Act II, chapter 3: Ember's birthday)

**Hook:** Bramble ties ribbon on every chair for Ember's birthday. She joins a blue length and a gold length.

**Short:**
> Bramble joins 3/8 of a meter of blue to 2/8 of gold. "Five sixteenths," says Pip. Bramble holds it up. "Shorter than the blue alone?"

**Longer:**
> The café chairs need bows, and Bramble has run short. She finds a blue scrap, three eighths of a meter, and a gold one, two eighths. She knots them end to end.
>
> "How long is it now?" asks Ember, who wants a very long bow.
>
> Pip counts on his paws. "Three and two is five. Eight and eight is sixteen. Five sixteenths."
>
> Bramble lays the joined ribbon beside the measuring board, then lays the blue piece alone beside it. "Five sixteenths is less than three eighths. But I just added gold."
>
> "So the eights don't add," says {guide}. "They tell you the size of each piece. Eighths joined to eighths are still eighths."
>
> "Five eighths," says Bramble. She ties the bow. Ember says it is the best bow so far, which is also what she said about the first one.

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> The bottom number names the size of the piece. The top number counts the pieces. Joining same-size pieces changes the count, never the size.

### Scene 2. Rope for the Vadoalto boats (Act III, chapter 1: the floating market)

**Hook:** The Vadoalto boats need a longer mooring rope. The mending crew splices two lengths at the Ropewalk.

**Short:**
> The Vadoalto baker needs a longer rope for her boat. The crew joins 2 3/5 meters and 1 4/5 meters. "Three and seven fifths?" says Pip.

**Longer:**
> The flat boats from Vadoalto bump against the landing all morning. "Our rope is too short," says the Vadoalto baker. "The boat drifts, and so does the bread."
>
> At the Ropewalk, the mending crew finds two good lengths: two and three fifths meters, and one and four fifths. They splice them.
>
> Pip adds it up. "Two and one is three. Three fifths and four fifths is seven fifths. Three and seven fifths meters."
>
> "Seven fifths is more than a whole," says {guide}. "Five fifths make one meter. So pull one whole out."
>
> Pip counts again. "Four meters, and two fifths left over. Four and two fifths."
>
> The baker ties her boat. It stops drifting. The bread, she says, is another matter.

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> Real splices use up some rope in the join. Here, joining uses no rope, so the lengths simply add.

### Scene 3. Ribbons where the old ones stop (Act III, chapter 3: the route by the high ford)

**Hook:** Halfway to Vadoalto, the faded ribbons stop. Bramble cuts new ribbons for two posts from one spool.

**Short:**
> Bramble has 2 1/2 meters of ribbon. She cuts 3/4 of a meter, then 2/3. "Is there enough for a third post?"

**Longer:**
> Where the new path cuts across the old one, nobody tied a ribbon. Bramble wants to mark it the way her grandmother would have. She brings one spool: two and a half meters.
>
> The first post gets three quarters of a meter of blue. The second gets two thirds of a meter of gold.
>
> "How much is left?" asks {courier}.
>
> "Three quarters and two thirds," says Bramble. "Quarters and thirds don't match. I can't count them together yet."
>
> "Cut them all into twelfths," says {guide}. "Every quarter is three twelfths. Every third is four."
>
> Bramble counts. "Nine twelfths and eight twelfths. Seventeen twelfths used. The spool was thirty twelfths." She smiles. "Thirteen twelfths left. Over a meter. Enough for another post."

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> To join different-size pieces, first cut both into one shared size. Twelfths fit quarters and thirds, because 12 is in the 4s and the 3s.

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer. Worked steps appear one tap at a time.

**T1. Split every part** (A true thing about math)
> Split every part of a ribbon into equal smaller parts. The colored stretch doesn't move. It just has a new name.
Worked example: 1. 1/3 is colored. 2. Split each third into 2. 3. Now 2 of 6 parts are colored. 4. 1/3 = 2/6.

**T2. Both numbers move together** (A true thing about math)
> Splitting each part into 2 doubles the colored parts and all the parts. Multiply top and bottom by the same number. Adding the same number doesn't work.
Worked example: 1. 1/3, split each part in 2. 2. Top: 1 × 2 = 2. Bottom: 3 × 2 = 6. 3. 1/3 = 2/6. 4. Adding 1 to each gives 2/4: a longer stretch.

**T3. Chains of names** (A true thing about math)
> Start from the same ribbon each time. Split each part into 2, 3 or 4. New names, same amount. Look for the times-table pattern.
Worked example: 1. Start at 1/4. 2. Split in 2: 2/8. 3. Start again at 1/4; split in 3: 3/12. 4. Tops go 1, 2, 3. Bottoms go 4, 8, 12.

**T4. Group the parts** (A true thing about math)
> Group the parts into equal bunches. Fewer, bigger parts, same colored stretch. Divide top and bottom by the bunch size.
Worked example: 1. 6/8 is colored. 2. Group the parts in pairs. 3. 6 ÷ 2 = 3; 8 ÷ 2 = 4. 4. 6/8 = 3/4.

**T5. Can you go further?** (A true thing about math)
> A bunch size must go into both numbers. When only 1 does, the fraction is in simplest form.
Worked example: 1. 18/24. Group in 2s: 9/12. 2. 9 and 12 both go in 3s. 3. 9/12 = 3/4. 4. Only 1 goes into 3 and 4. Done.

**T6. Count past one whole** (A true thing about math)
> Four quarters make one whole. Keep counting quarters past the whole. 11/4 is two wholes and 3 quarters more.
Worked example: 1. Count quarters: 1/4 ... 4/4 is one whole. 2. 8/4 is two wholes. 3. Three more quarters: 11/4. 4. 11/4 = 2 3/4.

**T7. Wholes into pieces** (A true thing about math)
> To write 3 2/5 as fifths, cut each whole into 5 fifths. Count them, then add the extra fifths.
Worked example: 1. One whole is 5 fifths. 2. Three wholes: 3 × 5 = 15 fifths. 3. Add 2 more: 17 fifths. 4. 3 2/5 = 17/5. Check: 17/5 is 3 wholes and 2/5.

**T8. Same-size pieces add** (A true thing about math)
> Two fifths and two fifths make four fifths, like two apples and two apples. The bottom number names the piece. It stays the same.
Worked example: 1. 2/5 + 2/5. 2. Count fifths: 2 + 2 = 4. 3. Still fifths. 4. 4/5.

**T9. Never add the bottoms** (A true thing about math)
> Adding the bottoms makes smaller pieces. Lay four tenths beside four fifths. They only cover two fifths. Too short!
Worked example: 1. 2/5 + 2/5 is not 4/10. 2. 4/10 = 2/5, equal to just one addend. 3. The answer is 4/5.

**T10. Many ways to split** (A true thing about math)
> One fraction can be split into same-size pieces in many ways. Every way adds back to the same amount.
Worked example: 1. 5/6. 2. 1/6 + 4/6. 3. 2/6 + 3/6. 4. 1/6 + 1/6 + 3/6. All make 5/6.

**T11. Make the pieces match** (A true thing about math)
> Thirds and sixths are different sizes. Split each third into 2 sixths. Now count sixths.
Worked example: 1. 1/3 + 1/6. 2. 1/3 = 2/6 (top and bottom × 2). 3. 2/6 + 1/6 = 3/6. 4. 3/6 = 1/2.

**T12. One cut for both** (A true thing about math)
> Thirds don't fit quarters. Find a number in both times tables. Twelfths fit both. Change both fractions, top and bottom.
Worked example: 1. 2/3 + 1/4. 2. 2/3 = 8/12. 3. 1/4 = 3/12. 4. 8/12 + 3/12 = 11/12.

**T13. Guess the zone first** (A true thing about math)
> Before working it out, compare each fraction with 0, 1/2 and 1. Guess where the answer lands. Then check.
Worked example: 1. 2/5 + 1/3. 2. Each is under 1/2, over 1/4. 3. Together: over 1/2, under 1. 4. Exact: 11/15. It fits.

**T14. Wholes, then pieces** (A true thing about math)
> Add the wholes. Add the pieces. If the pieces make more than a whole, pull a whole out.
Worked example: 1. 2 3/5 + 1 4/5. 2. Wholes: 2 + 1 = 3. 3. Fifths: 3/5 + 4/5 = 7/5. 4. 7/5 = 1 2/5, so 4 2/5.

**T15. Trade a whole** (A true thing about math)
> Not enough pieces to take away? Trade one whole for pieces. Or turn both into improper fractions first.
Worked example: 1. 4 2/6 − 1 5/6. 2. 2/6 is less than 5/6. 3. Trade: 4 2/6 = 3 8/6. 4. 3 8/6 − 1 5/6 = 2 3/6 = 2 1/2.

**T16. Story first** (A true thing about math)
> Draw the story as a bar before writing numbers. Are lengths joining, or is some taken away? Then guess, work and check.
Worked example: 1. A pot has 3/4 liter; 2/4 is poured out. 2. Taken away. 3. 3/4 − 2/4 = 1/4 liter left.

## Real-world and history cards

**H1. The fraction bar** (A true thing from history; Seedling and up)
> More than 800 years ago, a writer called al-Hassar told readers: put the bottom number under a line. It is the oldest known writing of a fraction bar.
Keeper's note:
> Al-Hassar lived in the western Islamic world, in North Africa or Spain. Historians think teachers there were already using the bar. In 1202, Leonardo of Pisa (Fibonacci) used it in a book that spread it in Europe.

**H2. The Nine Chapters' rule for simpler fractions** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> About 2,000 years ago, a Chinese book, the Nine Chapters, gave a rule. Halve both numbers if you can. Otherwise take the smaller from the bigger, again and again.
Keeper's note:
> Stop when the two numbers are equal. The book calls that the "equal number". Divide top and bottom by it. For 49/91: 91 − 49 = 42, 49 − 42 = 7, then keep taking 7 from 42 until both are 7. So 49/91 = 7/13. The same book adds 1/3 and 2/5 to get 11/15.

**H3. Fibonacci's mixed numbers** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> In his 1202 book, Fibonacci wrote the fraction first and the whole number after it. Where we write 2 1/3, he wrote the 1/3, then the 2.
Keeper's note:
> His book, *Liber Abaci*, taught Hindu-Arabic numerals to European traders. He also stacked several fractions on one bar, read from right to left. Same amounts, different writing: notation is a choice people agree on.

## Bench (a): The Split-and-Group Ribbon

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> One whole ribbon, colored from the left. Splitting or grouping changes the marks, never the colored stretch.

**Opening:** Change the cuts. Watch the colored end stay put.
**Buttons:** Split every part into 2 · into 3 · into 4 · Group in 2s · in 3s · in 4s · in 5s · in 6s · Undo · Add to the chain
**Readout pattern:** "[colored] of [all] equal parts are colored: [colored]/[all]."
**When a group size doesn't fit:** Some parts are left over. That bunch size doesn't go into both numbers.
**Simplest form note** (shown only when asked): Only 1 goes into both numbers now. This is simplest form.
**Contrast button: Add 1 to each.** Shows a second ribbon at the new fraction. "Adding 1 to each moved the end. Splitting never does."

**Guided item:** Split 1/2 so it has sixths.
- Step 1: Split every half into 3. *(Each half becomes 3 sixths.)*
- Step 2: Count the colored parts. *(3.)*
- Step 3: 1/2 = 3/6. Did the colored end move? *(No.)*

**Second guided item (grouping):** Group 4/6 in 2s.
- Step 1: Do 2s go into 4 and into 6? *(Yes.)*
- Step 2: 4 ÷ 2 = 2; 6 ÷ 2 = 3. *(2/3.)*

**Wren's desk card** (Keeper's Desk; A made-up model):
> Wren keeps a ledger of ribbon orders. Use the Nine Chapters' rule to simplify 12/18. Halve first, then take away.
Steps: 1. Halve: 6/9. 2. 9 − 6 = 3. 3. 6 − 3 = 3. Equal number: 3. 4. 6/9 = 2/3.

## Bench (b): The Spool Rack

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> Each piece is a fraction of one meter. When a spool holds a whole meter of pieces, it closes.

**Opening:** Stamp pieces one at a time. Watch wholes form.
**Buttons:** Stamp one piece · Close full spools · Break a spool into pieces · Write it both ways
**Readout pattern:** "[n] [pieces] = [wholes] whole meters and [left]/[size] of a meter."
**Same-point line:** The improper fraction and the mixed number light up at one point on a 0-to-4 line.

**Guided item:** 7 thirds of a meter. How many whole meters?
- Step 1: Stamp 3 thirds. Close the spool. *(1 whole.)*
- Step 2: Stamp 3 more. Close it. *(2 wholes.)*
- Step 3: One third is left. *(7/3 = 2 1/3.)*

## Bench (c): The Joining Table

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> One meter bar, already cut into one size of piece. Pieces join or leave; they never change size.

**Opening:** Join pieces. Take pieces away. Name what you have.
**Buttons:** Join a piece · Take a piece away · Split it another way · Lay the bottoms-added answer beside it
**Running readout:** "[count] [pieces] of a meter: [count]/[size]."
**When pieces pass one meter:** The next meter bar appears, the same size. "4/4 is one whole meter."
**Bottoms-added check:** "Here is 4/10 beside your 4/5. It only reaches 2/5."

**Guided item:** Join 1/4 and 2/4.
- Step 1: What size are the pieces? *(Quarters.)*
- Step 2: Count them: 1 + 2. *(3 quarters.)*
- Step 3: 1/4 + 2/4 = 3/4.

**Split-it-another-way prompt:** Find another way to make 5/6. Then another.

## Bench (d): The Common Cut

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> Two ribbons, each one meter long, cut different ways. A common cut splits both into one shared size.

**Opening:** These pieces don't match. Find a cut that fits both.
**Buttons:** Place on the 0, 1/2, 1 line · Try a common cut: [offered sizes] · Join · Take away · Simplify
**When the cut doesn't fit one ribbon:** That size doesn't split [thirds] evenly. Try a number in both tables.
**Zone line:** "Your guess: [between 1/2 and 1]. Exact answer: [11/15]. They agree."

**Guided item:** 1/2 + 1/4.
- Step 1: Do quarters fit halves? *(Yes. Each half is 2 quarters.)*
- Step 2: 1/2 = 2/4.
- Step 3: 2/4 + 1/4 = 3/4.

## Bench (e): The Rope Walk

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> Rope comes in whole-meter coils and short pieces. A coil can be untied into same-size pieces, and pieces can be coiled.

**Opening:** Join ropes. Cut ropes. Trade a coil when you need to.
**Buttons:** Join · Cut off · Untie a coil into pieces · Coil a whole meter · Write as improper fractions
**When fraction parts pass a whole:** "These pieces make more than a meter. Coil a whole one?"
**When there aren't enough pieces to cut:** "Not enough pieces. Untie a coil, or write both as improper fractions."

**Guided item:** Join 1 3/4 m and 1 2/4 m.
- Step 1: Coils: 1 + 1 = 2.
- Step 2: Pieces: 3/4 + 2/4 = 5/4.
- Step 3: 5/4 is a coil and 1/4. *(3 1/4 m.)*

## Bench (f): The Order Board

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> Made-up orders for ribbon, rope and dye. Every order uses one same-size whole: one meter, one liter or one pie.

**Opening:** Read the order. Draw it. Then work it out.
**Steps on every card:** Draw the bar · Join or take away? · Guess the zone · Work it out · Answer the order
**Answer pattern:** "[amount] [unit] [left / in all]."

**Guided item:** Bramble needs 1/2 m of red and 1/8 m of gold. How much ribbon?
- Step 1: Draw two lengths joined. *(Join.)*
- Step 2: Guess: a bit over 1/2.
- Step 3: 1/2 = 4/8. 4/8 + 1/8 = 5/8 m.

## Problem sets

Eighteen three-choice items put the right answer at A six times, B six times and C six times. Number items accept any equal form (3/6 or 1/2; 3 7/5 or 4 2/5), then offer the tidy form as a next step, never as a correction.

### Set A. Same amount, new names (Sprout and Seedling)

**A1 (Sprout, 3-choice, picture)**
Prompt: Bramble splits every piece in two. Is more ribbon colored now?
- A. Yes. There are more colored pieces. *(tests: more pieces means more)*
- B. No. Same colored stretch, smaller pieces. *(right)*
- C. Less. Each piece is smaller now. *(tests: smaller pieces means less)*
Hint: Look at where the color ends.
Explanation: The color ends at the same place. Only the cuts changed.
Feedback A: More pieces, yes. But look where the color ends.
Feedback B: Yes. New cuts, same stretch.
Feedback C: Smaller pieces, but more of them. The color ends in the same place.

**A2 (Sprout, 3-choice, picture)**
Prompt: Which ribbon has the same colored stretch as Bramble's?
- A. The one with more cuts, colored to the same mark. *(right)*
- B. The one with more colored pieces, past the mark. *(tests: counting pieces, not length)*
- C. The one with the same number of colored pieces, but tiny pieces. *(tests: same count means same amount)*
Hint: Line the ribbons up. Where does each color end?
Explanation: Same mark, same amount, however many cuts.
Feedback A: Yes. Its color ends at the same mark.
Feedback B: Its color goes past the mark. That's more ribbon.
Feedback C: Same count, but tiny pieces. Its color stops short.

**A3 (Sprout, 3-choice, picture)**
Prompt: Bramble groups the pieces in pairs. Did the colored stretch change?
- A. Yes. Fewer pieces means less ribbon. *(tests: fewer pieces means less)*
- B. Yes. Grouping takes some ribbon away. *(tests: grouping as removing)*
- C. No. Same stretch, bigger pieces. *(right)*
Hint: Did any ribbon leave the table?
Explanation: Grouping only takes away cut marks. No ribbon goes anywhere.
Feedback A: Fewer pieces, but bigger ones. Look at the colored end.
Feedback B: Only marks went away. The ribbon is all still there.
Feedback C: Yes. Bigger pieces, same colored end.

**A4 (Seedling, number)**
Prompt: 1/3 = ?/6. Split each third to find out.
Answer: 2.
Worked steps: 1. Each third splits into 2 sixths. 2. Bottom: 3 × 2 = 6. 3. Top: 1 × 2 = 2.
Tests: 4 (adding 3 to the top because 3 was added to the bottom); 1 (changing only the bottom).
Hint: Into how many pieces does each third split?
Explanation: Both numbers are multiplied by 2, so the stretch stays the same.

**A5 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Pip says 2/3 = 3/4. He added 1 to each. Is he right?
- A. Yes. He did the same thing to both numbers. *(tests: additive equivalence)*
- B. No. 3/4 is a bit longer. Adding moves the end. *(right)*
- C. No. 3/4 is shorter, because 4 is bigger than 3. *(tests: bigger bottom always means less)*
Hint: Put 2/3 and 3/4 on two ribbons. Compare the ends.
Explanation: Multiplying both keeps the stretch. Adding to both moves it.
Feedback A: Same to both only works for multiplying. Line up the two ribbons.
Feedback B: Yes. 3/4 reaches a little past 2/3.
Feedback C: Quarters are smaller, but there are three of them. Check the ribbons.

**A6 (Seedling, number)**
Prompt: Finish the chain: 1/4 = 2/8 = ?/12.
Answer: 3.
Worked steps: 1. 12 = 4 × 3. 2. So the top is 1 × 3. 3. 3/12.
Tests: 6 (adding 4 because the bottom went up by 4); 4 (doubling the 2).
Hint: What times 4 makes 12?
Explanation: Tops go 1, 2, 3 as bottoms go 4, 8, 12.

### Set B. Joining same-size pieces (Sprout and Seedling)

**B1 (Sprout, 3-choice, picture)**
Prompt: You join two blue pieces and one more, all the same size. What now?
- A. Three pieces, the same size as before. *(right)*
- B. Three pieces, but each one is smaller. *(tests: joining shrinks the pieces)*
- C. One piece, three times as small. *(tests: joining changes the piece size)*
Hint: Did any piece change size?
Explanation: Joining changes how many pieces. It never changes their size.
Feedback A: Yes. Three pieces, same size.
Feedback B: Nothing cut them. Each piece is the size it was.
Feedback C: Joined pieces get longer together, not smaller.

**B2 (Sprout, 3-choice, picture)**
Prompt: Pip takes one piece away from three same-size pieces. What's left?
- A. Two pieces, but each one is bigger. *(tests: taking away resizes pieces)*
- B. Two pieces, the same size as before. *(right)*
- C. Three smaller pieces. *(tests: taking away means cutting)*
Hint: Count what's left. Did they change size?
Explanation: One piece left the table. The others stayed just as they were.
Feedback A: Nothing stretched them. They're the same size.
Feedback B: Yes. Two pieces, same size.
Feedback C: Pip didn't cut anything. He took one away.

**B3 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: 2/5 + 2/5 = ?
- A. 4/10 *(tests: adding the bottoms)*
- B. 4 *(tests: dropping the piece size)*
- C. 4/5 *(right)*
Hint: What size are the pieces? Count them.
Explanation: Two fifths and two fifths make four fifths. The bottom names the piece.
Feedback A: 4/10 is the same as 2/5. Joining made it longer than that.
Feedback B: Four what? Four fifths, not four whole meters.
Feedback C: Yes. Four fifths.

**B4 (Seedling, number)**
Prompt: Bramble has 6/8 m of ribbon. She cuts off 3/8 m. What's left?
Answer: 3/8 m.
Worked steps: 1. Pieces are eighths. 2. 6 − 3 = 3. 3. Still eighths: 3/8.
Tests: 3 (dropping the eighths, as if 3 whole meters); 9/8 (adding instead of taking away).
Hint: What size are the pieces? How many go?
Explanation: Taking away eighths leaves eighths. Three eighths are left.

**B5 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Pip says 5/6 = 2/3 + 3/3. Is he right?
- A. Yes. 2 + 3 = 5 and 3 + 3 = 6. *(tests: adding tops and bottoms in reverse)*
- B. No. 5/6 can only be five 1/6 pieces. *(tests: only one way to split)*
- C. No. 2/3 + 3/3 makes 5/3, not 5/6. *(right)*
Hint: Add 2/3 and 3/3. Do you get 5/6?
Explanation: 2/3 + 3/3 is 5/3, much more. Split 5/6 into sixths: 2/6 + 3/6.
Feedback A: 3/3 is a whole meter alone. That's more than 5/6.
Feedback B: There are many ways, like 2/6 + 3/6 or 1/6 + 4/6.
Feedback C: Yes. In sixths, 2/6 + 3/6 works. So does 1/6 + 4/6.

**B6 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: 3/4 m + 2/4 m = ?
- A. 5/4 m: one meter and a quarter. *(right)*
- B. 5/8 m *(tests: adding the bottoms)*
- C. 1 m. It can't go past one. *(tests: stopping at one whole)*
Hint: Count quarters. What happens at four?
Explanation: Five quarters. Four make a meter; one quarter more starts the next.
Feedback A: Yes. 5/4 is 1 1/4.
Feedback B: 5/8 is less than 3/4 alone. You joined more on.
Feedback C: Lengths can pass one meter. Count the fifth quarter.

### Set C. Wholes and pieces (Seedling and Sapling)

**C1 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: You have 11 quarter-meter pieces. How much ribbon is that?
- A. 2 whole meters and 3/4. *(right)*
- B. 3 whole meters and 3/4. *(tests: miscounting the wholes)*
- C. 2 whole meters and 7/4. *(tests: counting two wholes, then keeping 11 − 4 = 7 quarters)*
Hint: Four quarters make a meter. How many fours fit in 11?
Explanation: Two fours make 8 quarters. Three quarters are left: 2 3/4.
Feedback A: Yes. 11/4 = 2 3/4.
Feedback B: Three wholes would need 12 quarters. You have 11.
Feedback C: Two meters use 8 quarters. Only 3 quarters are left.

**C2 (Seedling, number)**
Prompt: Write 3 2/5 as fifths.
Answer: 17/5.
Worked steps: 1. One whole is 5 fifths. 2. Three wholes: 15 fifths. 3. Plus 2: 17 fifths.
Tests: 5/5 (adding 3 + 2); 15/5 (forgetting the 2 fifths); 32/5 (writing the digits side by side).
Hint: How many fifths are in one whole?

- **Grouping items:** "2/4 grouped in 2s is 1/2. Start: what bunch size goes into both numbers?"

## Key terms (glossary)

- **fraction**: equal parts of one whole, like 3/4.
- **numerator**: the top number. It counts the pieces.
- **denominator**: the bottom number. It names the size of the pieces.
- **equivalent fractions**: different names for the same amount, like 1/2 and 2/4.
- **simplest form**: when only 1 goes into both top and bottom.
- **common factor**: a number that goes into both numbers.
- **improper fraction**: a fraction at least as big as one whole, like 7/4.
- **mixed number**: wholes and a fraction together, like 1 3/4.
- **common denominator**: one piece size that fits both fractions.
- **benchmark**: an easy number to compare with, like 0, 1/2 or 1.
- **trade a whole**: swap one whole for its pieces, like 1 for 4/4.
- **fraction bar**: the line between the top and bottom numbers.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** equivalent fractions by splitting every part, simplifying by grouping parts with a common factor, mixed numbers and improper fractions both ways, adding and subtracting same-size pieces (and why the bottoms never add), more than one way to split a fraction, common denominators for unlike fractions, adding and subtracting mixed numbers with and without trading a whole, and fraction word problems with a benchmark guess first.

**What it does not claim:** the ribbons and ropes are made-up models; real knots and splices use up length.
