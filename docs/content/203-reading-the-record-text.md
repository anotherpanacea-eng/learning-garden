# Reading the Record: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 203](../specs/203-reading-the-record.md). Use as written, after independent spec review and a math check. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion and is never gendered; `{courier}` is the explorer's courier. Pip is he/him, Dot she/her, Bramble she/her (with her magpie), the miller she/her (with her cat), the librarian they/them, Wren Halloway she/her, Hob Pennick he/him. **Hollis rule:** the Act I scene and every bench, card and item say "the old Keeper"; only the Act IV and Act V scenes, after page 3, name Hollis (they/them). Real-world facts are in the [source audit](../source-audits/203-reading-the-record.md); items still marked `[check]` stay off screen until cleared. Nothing here is scored or timed.

## Labels used on every card

- **A story from Willowbank**: the town, its record room, its finds, boats, polls and every character are made up.
- **A true thing about math**: checked mathematics, explained simply.
- **A true thing from history**: something people really did or wrote.
- **A made-up model**: a simple game rule or a made-up set of numbers, shown on the card, that is not a real record.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. The tray of storm finds (Act I, chapter 4: mend the village)

**Hook:** The storm pushed mud and odd things under the record room door. The miller wants the floor back.

**Short:**
> The storm left shells, buttons and feathers on the record room floor. "Sort them," says the miller, "so we know what we've got." Pip finds a key.

**Longer:**
> The storm got into the record room. It left mud, a puddle and a heap of odd things on the floor: shells from the brook, buttons, feathers, a spoon and a little brass key. "The old Keeper would have had this sorted by lunch," says the miller, leaning on her broom. "Sort them, so we know what we've got."
>
> Dot lays out three baskets: shells, buttons, feathers. Pip holds up the key. "Where does this go? It's shiny like a shell." "It's a key, Pip," says Dot.
>
> {guide} sets down a fourth basket and writes *Other* on it. "Some things don't fit any group. That's allowed." Pip drops in the key and the spoon. Then he tries to drop in himself. The cat watches, unimpressed.

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> Fifteen finds: 6 shells, 4 buttons, 3 feathers and 2 in Other. Count every basket and the total should be 15. If it isn't, something got lost or counted twice.

### Scene 2. Before and after the draining (Act IV, chapter 1: the old map and Hollis's account)

**Hook:** The librarian wants Hollis's flood ledger on a chart. Bramble already knows what the chart will say.

**Short:**
> "Two floods before year 22. Five after," says Bramble. "Case closed." Wren Halloway taps the ledger. "How many years in each part?"

**Longer:**
> The librarian pins Hollis's flood ledger to the record room wall. Seven floods, seven years, seven heights. "The Sponge was drained in year 22," says Pip. "Count the floods before and after."
>
> Bramble counts. "Years 1 to 21: two floods. Years 22 to 40: five floods. Case closed."
>
> Wren Halloway, who keeps the ledgers at the Keeper's Desk, taps the page. "How many years in each part?" "Twenty-one before," says Dot. "Nineteen after." "So the after part is shorter," says Wren, "and it still has more floods. That's worth knowing. Does it tell us why?"
>
> Bramble looks at Nuria's letter about the big rains, pinned beside the ledger. "We need the rain on the same chart," she says. "Now you're keeping a record," says Wren.

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> By ten-year blocks, Hollis's floods go 1, 1, 1, 4. Nuria's big rains go 6, 5, 6, 8. Floods rose much more than big rains. That is a clue, not a proof of what caused it.

### Scene 3. Charts for the Gazette (Act V, path 2: the Willowbank Gazette)

**Hook:** The Gazette wants a chart of which plan people chose. Bramble has drawn one already.

**Short:**
> Bramble's chart makes the Sponge bar seven times taller than the Wall bar. "Fifteen isn't seven times nine," says Dot. Bramble's axis starts at 8.

**Longer:**
> The librarian asked 36 neighbors which plan they would keep. Nine chose the Wall, fifteen the Sponge, twelve Some of Each. Bramble brings a bar chart for her column. The Sponge bar towers. The Wall bar is a stub. "Seven times as many people chose the Sponge!" she says.
>
> Dot counts. "Fifteen isn't seven times nine. It isn't even twice." {guide} points at the bottom of the chart. "Your numbers start at 8. Each bar has lost its first 8." Bramble sighs. "Fine. Start at zero."
>
> "Or try a round chart," says the librarian. "The whole circle is all 36 people." Pip asks whether the Gazette could have one chart that is just a picture of him. The librarian says no, kindly. Hollis's old charts, pinned on the wall, all start at zero.

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> A bar chart's axis should start at zero, or say clearly that it doesn't. In a pie chart, each slice is a share of the whole: 9 of 36 is a quarter, 90 degrees.

## Teach cards

Each card is at most 30 words. Worked steps appear one tap at a time.

**T1. Sort by one rule** (A true thing about math)
> Pick one rule, like "what kind is it?" Each thing goes in one group. If it fits no group, put it in Other.
Worked example: 1. Rule: kind. 2. A shell goes with shells. 3. A spoon fits no group, so it goes in Other.

**T2. Count, write, order** (A true thing about math)
> Count each group. Write each name beside its count. Add the counts: the total should match everything you sorted. Then order from most to fewest.
Worked example: 1. Shells 6, buttons 4, feathers 3, other 2. 2. 6 + 4 + 3 + 2 = 15. 3. We had 15 finds. It matches.

**T3. How many more is a gap** (A true thing about math)
> "How many more?" asks for the difference. Line the two groups up, one by one. Count what is left over, or take away.
Worked example: 1. Shells 6, buttons 4. 2. Pair them up. 3. Two shells have no partner: 6 − 4 = 2 more.

**T4. Two rules make a grid** (A true thing about math)
> Sort by one rule across and another rule down. Each thing lands in one box, where its row and column meet.
Worked example: 1. Across: big or small. 2. Down: shell or button. 3. A small shell goes in the shell row, small column.

**T5. A line plot is a ruler with Xs** (A true thing about math)
> Measure from zero. Draw a line with every number, evenly spaced. Put one X for each thing, above its length. Stack Xs that match.
Worked example: 1. A feather measures 5 inches. 2. Find 5 on the line. 3. Put an X above it.

**T6. Eighths on the line** (A true thing about math)
> Split each inch into eight equal parts. Two eighths is a quarter. Four eighths is a half. To add lengths, add every X, once each.
Worked example: 1. Three Xs at 5/8. 2. 5/8 + 5/8 + 5/8. 3. 15/8 = 1 7/8 inches.

**T7. Where the row and column meet** (A true thing about math)
> Put one finger on the row. Put one finger on the column. Read only the box where they meet.
Worked example: 1. Row: Willowbank mill. 2. Column: middle boat. 3. They meet at 9:15.

**T8. How long? Count on** (A true thing about math)
> An hour has 60 minutes, not 100. Count on to the next o'clock, then past it. Add the jumps.
Worked example: 1. 8:45 to 9:00 is 15 minutes. 2. 9:00 to 10:00 is 60 minutes. 3. 10:00 to 10:20 is 20. 4. 95 minutes: 1 hour 35 minutes.

**T9. Read the scale, not the squares** (A true thing about math)
> Check what each square stands for. If each square is 5, a bar nine squares tall shows 45, not 9.
Worked example: 1. Each square is 5. 2. Orchard Way: 9 squares. 3. 9 × 5 = 45 finds.

**T10. One picture, many things** (A true thing about math)
> Read the key first. If one sack picture stands for 10 finds, half a sack stands for 5. Tally marks bundle in fives.
Worked example: 1. Key: one sack = 10. 2. Mill Lane: 3 and a half sacks. 3. 30 + 5 = 35 finds.

**T11. A line shows change over time** (A true thing about math)
> Time goes along the bottom. A point's height is the reading. The steepest part is the biggest change. Between points, the line is only an estimate.
Worked example: 1. Wednesday 12 cm, Thursday 26 cm. 2. Up 14 cm. 3. The steepest climb all week.

**T12. A pie chart is one whole** (A true thing about math)
> The whole circle is everyone, 360 degrees. Each slice is its share of the total. Angle = count ÷ total × 360.
Worked example: 1. 9 of 36 chose the Wall. 2. 9 ÷ 36 = one quarter. 3. A quarter of 360 is 90 degrees.

**T13. Typical and spread** (A true thing about math)
> To compare two sets, give a typical value and a spread. The median is the middle. The range is highest minus lowest. One odd value pulls the mean.
Worked example: 1. Ford: 31, 32, 33, 34, 36, 38, 76. 2. Middle value: 34. 3. Range: 76 − 31 = 45.

**T14. Dots for pairs** (A true thing about math)
> Each dot is one day: two measures. Draw one straight line with about as many dots above as below. Read from it inside the dots, not far outside.
Worked example: 1. 20 mm of rain, rise 17 cm. 2. Go across to 20, up to 17. 3. Put the dot there.

**T15. Goes with is not causes** (A true thing about math)
> Two things can rise together because something else moves both. A pattern is a clue. It is not, by itself, a reason.
Worked example: 1. More umbrellas, higher brook. 2. Umbrellas don't fill brooks. 3. Rain brings both.

## Real-world and history cards

**H1. A river written on clay** (A true thing from history; Seedling and up)
> In ancient Babylon, sky watchers wrote diaries on clay tablets. Each month they noted the weather, the price of barley and dates, and how high the Euphrates river stood.
Keeper's note:
> The diaries cover about 600 years, ending about 2,000 years ago. Most tablets are broken pieces. Like the old Keeper's tables, they are a long record kept by careful people, so later people could compare.

**H2. Playfair's charts** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> In 1786, William Playfair printed a book of charts about trade. Lines showed money going up and down over years. One chart used bars.
Keeper's note:
> Playfair drew his famous bar chart because he did not have numbers for many years, only one. So he drew Scotland's trade with 17 places, one year, as bars. In 1801 he printed what may be the first pie chart.

**H3. Florence Nightingale's wedges** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> In 1858, Florence Nightingale drew a chart of soldiers' deaths in a war. One wedge for each month. Blue showed sickness that better care could prevent.
Keeper's note:
> She sent it to Queen Victoria. The blue was far bigger than the red for wounds. Her charts helped her argue for better army hospitals. Each wedge's area, not its length, stood for the number.

## Problem sets

Twenty-four three-choice items put the right answer at A eight times, B eight times and C eight times.

### Set A. Sorting storm finds (Sprout and Seedling)

**A1 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: We sort by kind. Where does the brass key go?
- A. In the shells. It is shiny too. *(tests: forcing a thing into a group)*
- B. In the Other basket. *(right)*
- C. Nowhere. Leave it out of the sort. *(tests: dropping things that don't fit)*
Hint: Is a key a shell, a button or a feather?
Explanation: It fits no group, so it goes in Other. Every find is still counted.
Feedback A: Shiny isn't our rule. Our rule is what kind it is.
Feedback B: Yes. Other is for things that fit no group.
Feedback C: Then our sort misses a find. Other keeps it in.

**A2 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Shells and buttons are lined up, one under one. Which has more?
- A. Shells. Some shells have no button under them. *(right)*
- B. Buttons. The buttons are bigger. *(tests: size of the pictures as amount)*
- C. Neither. Both rows reach the end of the tray. *(tests: row length as amount)*
Hint: Look for shells with no partner.
Explanation: Matching one to one shows the extra shells.
Feedback A: Yes. The shells with no partner are the extra ones.
Feedback B: Big buttons are still just buttons. Match them one to one.
Feedback C: The buttons are spread out. Match them one to one.

**A3 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Sort by kind, then by size. Where does a small shell go?
- A. The big shells box. *(tests: using only the first rule)*
- B. The small buttons box. *(tests: using only the second rule)*
- C. The small shells box. *(right)*
Hint: Find the shell row. Then find the small column.
Explanation: Both rules count. Shell row, small column.
Feedback A: It is a shell. Is it big?
Feedback B: It is small. Is it a button?
Feedback C: Yes. Shell and small, both at once.

**A4 (Seedling, number)**
Prompt: Shells 6, buttons 4, feathers 3, other 2. How many finds altogether?
Answer: 15.
Worked steps: 1. 6 + 4 = 10. 2. 10 + 3 = 13. 3. 13 + 2 = 15.
Tests: 4 (counting the groups); 13 (leaving out Other).
Hint: Add every count, Other too.
Explanation: Fifteen, which matches the fifteen finds we sorted.

**A5 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: How many more shells than feathers?
- A. 6 *(tests: giving the bigger count)*
- B. 3 *(right)*
- C. 9 *(tests: adding instead of comparing)*
Hint: Line up 6 shells and 3 feathers. How many are left over?
Explanation: 6 − 3 = 3. "How many more" is the gap.
Feedback A: 6 is how many shells. How many more than feathers?
Feedback B: Yes. Three shells have no feather partner.
Feedback C: That's how many altogether. We want the gap.

**A6 (Seedling, order)**
Prompt: Two more feathers arrive. Order the kinds from most to fewest.
Answer: shells 6, feathers 5, buttons 4, other 2.
Tests: keeping the old order (feathers not updated); putting the newest group first.
Hint: Feathers are 3 + 2 now.
Explanation: Update the count first, then order. The total is now 17.

### Set B. Lengths on a line plot (Seedling and Sapling)

**B1 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: The tallest stack of Xs is over 5. What does that show?
- A. Three feathers are 5 inches long. *(right)*
- B. The longest feather is 5 inches. *(tests: stack height read as length)*
- C. One feather is 3 inches long. *(tests: each X read as one inch)*
Hint: Each X is one feather. Where does it sit?
Explanation: The stack tells how many. The place tells how long.
Feedback A: Yes. Three Xs, three feathers, each 5 inches.
Feedback B: Tall stack means many feathers. The longest sits farthest right.
Feedback C: Each X is a whole feather, not an inch.

**B2 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: No feather was 8 inches. What happens to 8 on the line?
- A. Leave 8 out and close the gap. *(tests: skipping empty values)*
- B. Keep 8 on the line, with no X. *(right)*
- C. Put one X there so it isn't empty. *(tests: inventing data)*
Hint: The line is a ruler. Does a ruler skip 8?
Explanation: Every number stays, evenly spaced. An empty spot shows nothing measured 8.
Feedback A: Then 9 slides too close to 7. A ruler never skips.
Feedback B: Yes. The empty 8 is information too.
Feedback C: An X means a real feather. None measured 8.

**B3 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: Shells: widest 1 1/8 inches, narrowest 3/8 inch. How much wider?
Answer: 6/8 inch, which is 3/4 inch.
Worked steps: 1. 1 1/8 = 9/8. 2. 9/8 − 3/8 = 6/8. 3. 6/8 = 3/4 inch.
Tests: 1 4/8 (adding instead of taking away); 6 (dropping "eighths").
Hint: Count the eighths between them on the line.
Explanation: Six eighth-steps apart. Six eighths is the same as three quarters.

**B4 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Three shells sit at 5/8 inch. Their widths together?
- A. 3 inches *(tests: counting Xs instead of adding lengths)*
- B. 15/24 inch *(tests: adding the bottoms too)*
- C. 15/8, or 1 7/8 inches *(right)*
Hint: Add 5/8 three times. Eighths stay eighths.
Explanation: 5/8 + 5/8 + 5/8 = 15/8 = 1 7/8 inches.
Feedback A: 3 is how many shells. How wide are they together?
Feedback B: The parts are still eighths. Only the top number grows.
Feedback C: Yes. Each X adds its own 5/8.

**B5 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: One shell is 1/2 inch. Another is 3/8. How much wider?
- A. 1/8 inch *(right)*
- B. 2/6 inch *(tests: taking tops from tops, bottoms from bottoms)*
- C. 1/4 inch *(tests: miscounting the steps)*
Hint: On the line, 1/2 sits at 4/8.
Explanation: 4/8 − 3/8 = 1/8. One eighth-step apart.
Feedback A: Yes. 1/2 is 4/8, one step past 3/8.
Feedback B: Make the parts match first: 1/2 is 4/8.
Feedback C: Count again. One step of 1/8 separates them.

Explanation: An end number is a total for its whole row.
Feedback A: Yes. 4 + 1 + 2 = 7 finds from the mill yard.
Feedback B: The shells box says 4. The 7 counts everything there.
Feedback C: The footbridge had 6, so only 1 more.

### Set D. Bars, pictures and tallies (Seedling, Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**D1 (Seedling, number)**
Prompt: Each square is 5. The Orchard Way bar is 9 squares. How many finds?
Answer: 45.
Worked steps: 1. Each square is 5. 2. 9 squares. 3. 9 × 5 = 45.
Tests: 9 (counting squares).
Hint: Count in fives up the bar.
Explanation: Read the scale, not the number of squares.

**D2 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: How many more finds from Orchard Way than Bridge Row?
- A. 5 *(tests: comparing squares, not finds)*
- B. 25 *(right)*
- C. 45 *(tests: giving the bigger bar's value)*
Hint: Orchard Way 45, Bridge Row 20. Find the gap.
Explanation: 45 − 20 = 25 more finds.
Feedback A: That's 5 squares. Each square is 5 finds.
Feedback B: Yes. 45 − 20 = 25.
Feedback C: 45 is Orchard Way alone. How many more than 20?

**D3 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: One sack picture means 10 finds. Mill Lane shows 3 and a half. How many?
- A. 3 and a half finds *(tests: one picture read as one thing)*
- B. 35 finds *(right)*
- C. 31 finds *(tests: half a picture read as 1)*
Hint: Read the key. What does half a sack stand for?
Explanation: 3 sacks are 30. Half a sack is 5. 35 in all.
Feedback A: Check the key. Each sack is 10 finds.
Feedback B: Yes. 30 + 5 = 35.
Feedback C: Half of 10 is 5, so half a sack is 5.

**D4 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: Market Square's tally shows three groups of five. How many finds?
Answer: 15.
Worked steps: 1. Each group is four strokes and one across: 5. 2. Three groups. 3. 5 + 5 + 5 = 15.
Tests: 12 (counting only the upright strokes); 3 (counting groups).
Hint: The stroke across is a mark too.
Explanation: Each bundle is five, so count by fives.

**D5 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Floods by decade: 1, 1, 1, 4. Big rains: 6, 5, 6, 8. Which fits?
- A. Draining the Sponge caused the floods. *(tests: reading a cause from a pattern)*
- B. Nothing. Floods and rains can't be compared. *(tests: refusing more than the evidence requires)*
- C. Floods rose much more than big rains did. *(right)*
Hint: Compare the first decade with the last, for each color.
Explanation: Floods went 1 to 4; big rains 6 to 8. Why needs more evidence.
Feedback A: The chart shows a change, not its cause.
Feedback B: Same ten-year blocks, same order. You can compare how each changed.
Feedback C: Yes. That's what these counts support.

**D6 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Where does 20 go: 10 ≤ h < 20 or 20 ≤ h < 30?
- A. In 20 ≤ h < 30. *(right)*
- B. In 10 ≤ h < 20. *(tests: putting a boundary value in the lower class)*
- C. In both classes. *(tests: overlapping classes)*
Hint: Read "h < 20" aloud. Is 20 less than 20?
Explanation: Each reading goes in exactly one class. 20 starts the next one.
Feedback A: Yes. 20 is at least 20 and under 30.
Feedback B: That class stops just before 20.
Feedback C: Then it is counted twice. Each value fits one class.

### Set E. Lines over time and round charts (Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**E1 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: On which day did the brook rise the most?
- A. Friday, the highest point. *(tests: height read as change)*
- B. Wednesday to Thursday, the steepest climb. *(right)*
- C. Friday to Saturday, the steepest part. *(tests: a steep fall read as a rise)*
Hint: Look for the steepest part that goes up.
Explanation: Wednesday 12, Thursday 26: up 14 cm, the most all week.
Feedback A: Friday is highest. It only rose 6 cm that day.
Feedback B: Yes. Steepest climb, biggest rise.
Feedback C: That part slopes down. The brook fell.

**E2 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: How much higher was the brook on Friday than Monday?
Answer: 28 cm.
Worked steps: 1. Friday: 32. 2. Monday: 4. 3. 32 − 4 = 28 cm.
Tests: 14 (counting squares, each worth 2 cm); 32 (Friday alone).
Hint: Read both points on the scale, then take away.
Explanation: Difference = Friday's reading minus Monday's.

**E3 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which of these should not be a line graph?
- A. The brook's height each morning. *(tests: rejecting a real time series)*
- B. How many finds of each kind. *(right)*
- C. A thermometer read every hour. *(tests: rejecting a real time series)*
Hint: A line joins readings in time order.
Explanation: Shells, buttons and feathers have no order in time. Use bars.
Feedback A: Mornings come in order, so a line fits.
Feedback B: Yes. Kinds aren't times. A line between them means nothing.
Feedback C: Hours come in order, so a line fits.

**E4 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: The line between Wednesday (12) and Thursday (26) passes 19. What is 19?
- A. A measured reading from Wednesday night. *(tests: an estimate read as a measurement)*
- B. Nothing useful. Only points were measured. *(tests: refusing any estimate)*
- C. An estimate, if the brook rose steadily. *(right)*
Hint: When were the readings taken?
Explanation: The line is a guess between two real readings.
Feedback A: Nobody read the post that night. The line fills a gap.
Feedback B: It can be useful, if you call it an estimate.
Feedback C: Yes. Useful, and honest about being a guess.

**E5 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: 15 of 36 neighbors chose the Sponge. How big is its slice?
Answer: 150 degrees.
Worked steps: 1. 15 ÷ 36 = 5/12. 2. 5/12 of 360. 3. 360 ÷ 12 = 30, and 30 × 5 = 150.
Tests: 24 (360 ÷ 15); 540 (15 × 36).
Hint: What fraction of everyone chose the Sponge?
Explanation: Then check: 90 + 150 + 120 = 360.

**E6 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: The Wall's slice is 90 degrees. How many people chose the Wall?
- A. 90 people. *(tests: reading an angle as a count)*
- B. 9 people. A quarter of the 36 asked. *(right)*
- C. 4 people. 360 ÷ 90. *(tests: dividing 360 by the angle)*
Hint: What share of the circle is 90 degrees? Who was asked?
Explanation: A slice is a share. You need the total to find a count.

Feedback C: 4 tells how many quarters fit. Take a quarter of 36.

### Set F. Comparing and scatter graphs (Keeper's Desk)

**F1 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: Ford trips: 31, 32, 33, 34, 36, 38, 76. What is the range?
Answer: 45 minutes.
Worked steps: 1. Highest: 76. 2. Lowest: 31. 3. 76 − 31 = 45.
Tests: "31 to 76" (giving the two ends, not the difference); 7 (counting trips).
Hint: Highest minus lowest.
Explanation: The range is one number: how spread out the trips are.

**F2 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which road is usually faster?
- A. The ford. Its median is 34; the bridge's is 42. *(right)*
- B. The bridge. One ford trip took 76 minutes. *(tests: one outlier taken as typical)*
- C. Can't tell. The means are almost the same. *(tests: comparing means only)*
Hint: Find each road's middle trip.
Explanation: Usually means typical. The ford's middle trip is 8 minutes faster.
Feedback A: Yes. Typically 8 minutes faster.
Feedback B: The 76 was one flooded day. Look at the usual trips.
Feedback C: The 76 pulls the ford's mean up. Try the medians.

**F3 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Hide the 76. What happens to the ford's mean and median?
- A. Both drop by the same amount. *(tests: an outlier moves both alike)*
- B. The median drops a lot; the mean stays. *(tests: mixing up the two)*
- C. The mean drops a lot; the median barely moves. *(right)*
Hint: The mean uses every value. The median uses the middle.
Explanation: Mean 40 becomes 34. Median 34 becomes 33.5. The far value pulled the mean.
Feedback A: Try it: the mean falls 6, the median half a minute.
Feedback B: Swap them. The middle hardly changes.
Feedback C: Yes. The mean chases the far value.

**F4 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which line fits the usual days best?
- A. A zig-zag joining every dot. *(tests: joining the dots)*
- B. A straight line from the corner, 0 and 0. *(tests: forcing the line through the corner)*
- C. A straight line with as many dots above as below. *(right)*
Hint: A best-fit line is one straight line through the middle of the dots.
Explanation: It follows the trend and balances the dots, four above and four below.
Feedback A: A zig-zag copies each dot. It shows no trend.
Feedback B: The line needn't touch the corner. Balance the dots.
Feedback C: Yes. Straight, and balanced.

**F5 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: The 12 mm day sits far above the others. What should you do?
- A. Find out why. The note says ice jammed. *(right)*
- B. Rub it out. It spoils the pattern. *(tests: deleting an outlier without a reason)*
- C. Bend the line to go through it. *(tests: letting one point steer the line)*
Hint: Read the note pinned to that day.
Explanation: An outlier needs a reason. Ice held the water back that day.
Feedback A: Yes. Keep it, mark it, and leave it out of the line.
Feedback B: It really happened. Find out why before deciding.
Feedback C: One odd day shouldn't steer the line for all the others.

**F6 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: More umbrellas at market go with a higher brook. Do umbrellas raise it?
- A. Yes. The pattern proves it. *(tests: goes with read as causes)*
- B. Maybe. Patterns can never tell us anything. *(tests: refusing more than the evidence requires)*
- C. No. Rain brings out umbrellas and raises the brook. *(right)*
Hint: What else happens on umbrella days?
Explanation: Rain moves both. Two things rising together needn't cause each other.
Feedback A: Umbrellas stay dry in the market. What fills the brook?
Feedback B: Patterns are good clues. This one points at rain.
Feedback C: Yes. Something else moved both.

**F7 (Keeper's Desk, say why; spoken to a grown-up, not stored)**
Prompt: A day brings 80 mm of rain. Can the line tell you the rise? Why?

## "I'm stuck" worked examples

Step 2 of the ladder: an easier case, then how to start this one.

- **Sorting items:** "Apples and pears in a bowl, and one sock. The sock goes in Other. Start: what is your rule?"
- **Total and gap items:** "5 apples and 3 pears: 8 fruit. 5 − 3 = 2 more apples. Start: add, or find the gap?"
- **Grid items:** "A red ball goes in the red row, ball column. Start: which row, then which column?"
- **Line plot items:** "Two pencils are 4 inches: two Xs above 4. Start: how long is yours?"
- **Eighth items:** "2/8 + 2/8 = 4/8, which is 1/2. Start: count the eighth-steps."
- **Table items:** "Row: Tuesday. Column: lunch. Read where they meet. Start: put a finger on the row."
- **Time items:** "2:45 to 3:00 is 15 minutes. Start: how long to the next o'clock?"
- **Bar and picture items:** "Each square is 10: 3 squares is 30. Start: what is one square worth?"
- **Line graph items:** "Between 10 and 30, a steady line passes 20 halfway. Start: rise or height?"
- **Pie items:** "A quarter of the circle is 90 degrees. Start: what share of everyone?"
- **Comparing items:** "2, 3, 4, 5, 20: the middle is 4. Start: put them in order."
- **Scatter items:** "Taller children often have bigger feet. Height doesn't make feet. Start: what else could move both?"

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Other**: the group for things that fit no other group.
- **two-way table**: a table sorted by two things at once, with totals at the ends.
- **tally**: marks bundled in fives for counting.
- **pictogram**: a chart of pictures; the key says what one picture stands for.
- **scale**: how much each step or square on an axis stands for.
- **bar chart**: bars whose heights show how many in each group.
- **line plot**: a number line with an X for each thing measured.
- **line graph**: points in time order, joined to show change.
- **pie chart**: a circle split into slices, each a share of one whole.
- **mode**: the most common value or group.
- **median**: the middle value, once they are in order.
- **mean**: the total shared out evenly.
- **range**: highest value minus lowest value.
- **outlier**: a value far from the others.
- **scatter graph**: one dot for each pair of measures.
- **line of best fit**: one straight line through the middle of the dots.
- **correlation**: the pattern in the dots: uphill (positive), downhill (negative) or none.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** sorting with one rule, then two, with an Other group; counting, totaling, ordering and comparing groups; line plots of measured lengths in whole units and eighths; reading timetables and two-way tables, with durations counted on across the hour; scaled bar charts, pictograms with keys, tallies and grouped tables; line graphs for change over time and when not to use them; pie charts as shares of one whole; comparing two data sets by median and range, and an outlier's pull on the mean; plotting scatter graphs, a balanced line of best fit, reading inside the data, outliers, and "goes with is not causes".

**What it does not claim:** every number is made up for Willowbank. It does not teach the mean from the start (other benches do) or name correlation strength in numbers. A pie chart and a line graph here are drawn from given numbers; nothing measures your child's surroundings.
