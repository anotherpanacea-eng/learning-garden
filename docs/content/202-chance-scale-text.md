# The Chance Window: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 202](../specs/202-chance-scale.md). Use as written, after independent spec review and a math check. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion and is never gendered. Pip is he/him, Dot she/her, Bramble she/her (with her magpie), Nuria she/her, Wren Halloway she/her, the orchard keeper she/her, Hollis they/them (Act IV only). Real-world facts are in the [source audit](../source-audits/202-chance-scale.md); items still marked `[check]` stay off screen until cleared. Nothing here is scored or timed.

## Labels used on every card

- **A story from Willowbank**: the town, its stall, bags, spinners, packets, letters and every character are made up.
- **A true thing about math**: checked math, explained simply.
- **A true thing from history**: something people really did or wrote.
- **A made-up model**: a simple game rule, shown on the card, that is not a real measurement.

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer. Worked steps appear one tap at a time.

**T1. Five likely words** (A true thing about math)
> Impossible: it can't happen. Unlikely. Even chance. Likely. Certain: it must happen. Only certain and impossible are promises.
Worked example: 1. Bag: only reed packets. 2. Get a reed? Certain. 3. Get a sunflower? Impossible.

**T2. Reasons come from the bag** (A true thing about math)
> Look at what is in the bag. Most are reed, so reed is likely. Wishing doesn't change the bag.
Worked example: 1. Nine reed, one apple. 2. Apple is unlikely. 3. Unlikely is not impossible: one apple is there.

**T3. Out of the whole** (A true thing about math)
> To compare bags, ask how many out of the whole. More reeds is not enough. Look at the whole bag.
Worked example: 1. Bag A: 3 reed of 4. 2. Bag B: 6 reed of 12, half. 3. 3 of 4 is more. Bag A wins.

**T4. Equally likely** (A true thing about math)
> Outcomes are equally likely when each has the same chance. On a spinner, look at the size of each part, not the number of colors.
Worked example: 1. Sun: half. Cloud: a quarter. Rain: a quarter. 2. Sun's part is bigger. 3. Not equally likely.

**T5. Random is not the same as fair** (A true thing about math)
> An uneven spinner is still random. You can't know the next spin. But a bigger part comes up more often.
Worked example: 1. Spin the weather spinner 20 times. 2. Sun: about 10. 3. Cloud and rain: about 5 each.

**T6. Chance as a fraction** (A true thing about math)
> When outcomes are equally likely: chance = favorable outcomes over all outcomes. Count the ones you want, then count them all.
Worked example: 1. Spinner: 3 equal parts. 2. Rain is 1 part. 3. Chance of rain: 1/3.

**T7. The bottom counts everything** (A true thing about math)
> The bottom number is all the outcomes, the ones you want included. Don't compare one kind with the other kind.
Worked example: 1. 3 beans, 7 peas. 2. All seeds: 3 + 7 = 10. 3. Chance of a bean: 3/10.

**T8. The 0-to-1 line** (A true thing about math)
> Every chance sits between 0 and 1. 0 is impossible. 1/2 is even chance. 1 is certain. Nothing goes past 1.
Worked example: 1. 1/6 sits near 0. 2. 1/2 sits in the middle. 3. 3/4 sits near 1.

**T9. One point, three ways** (A true thing about math)
> A chance can be a fraction, a decimal or a percent. 1/4, 0.25 and 25% are the same point on the line.
Worked example: 1. 5 marigolds of 20 seeds: 5/20. 2. That is 1/4. 3. 1/4 = 0.25 = 25%.

**T10. Simplest form** (A true thing about math)
> Count, write the fraction, then simplify. The simpler fraction is the same point on the line.
Worked example: 1. 12 seeds, 4 sunflowers. 2. 4/12. 3. Divide top and bottom by 4: 1/3.

**T11. The whole is 1** (A true thing about math)
> List every outcome. If no two can happen together, their chances add to 1. Think of one bar, cut into parts.
Worked example: 1. Red 1/4, blue 1/4, yellow 1/8, green 3/8. 2. 2/8 + 2/8 + 1/8 + 3/8. 3. 8/8 = 1.

**T12. Not A** (A true thing about math)
> The chance something does not happen is 1 take away the chance it does. Not A = 1 − A.
Worked example: 1. Chance of rain: 0.7. 2. No rain is the rest. 3. 1 − 0.7 = 0.3.

**T13. Watch for overlap** (A true thing about math)
> Only add chances of things that can't happen together. If they share an outcome, you count it twice.
Worked example: 1. Even: 2, 4, 6. Prime: 2, 3, 5. 2. Both have 2. 3. Even or prime: 2, 3, 4, 5, 6. That is 5/6.

**T14. Fill the middle first** (A true thing about math)
> A box holds everything. Each circle holds a group. Fill the overlap first. Then each circle's "only" part. Then "neither".
Worked example: 1. 10 packets: 6 beans, 4 tall, 2 both. 2. Beans only: 6 − 2 = 4. Tall only: 2. 3. Neither: 10 − 8 = 2.

**T15. Chance from a diagram** (A true thing about math)
> Pick one at random. Chance = how many in that part over how many in the whole box. Use one circle only when told.
Worked example: 1. 24 packets, 5 in both. 2. Chance of both: 5/24. 3. From wet-ground packets only: 5/9.

**T16. Two-way tables** (A true thing about math)
> Two spinners make pairs. Rows for one, columns for the other. Every cell is one pair. Count rows times columns.
Worked example: 1. Two colors, three seeds. 2. 2 rows, 3 columns. 3. 2 × 3 = 6 pairs.

**T17. A record gives an "about"** (A true thing about math)
> Floods aren't spinners. We can't count equal parts. A record gives an "about", with a reason, never a promise.
Worked example: 1. Big floods: 4 of 40 years. 2. About 1 in 10. 3. Last 20 years: 4 of 20, 1 in 5.

## Real-world and history cards

**H1. A fair split of an unfinished game** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> In 1654, Blaise Pascal and Pierre de Fermat wrote letters about a puzzle. A game stops early. How do you share the prize fairly?
Keeper's note:
> They were French mathematicians. They never met; they solved it by letter. Their idea: share by how the game could still have ended, not by the points so far. Many people count their letters as a beginning of probability.

**H2. Venn's circles** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> In 1880, John Venn, an English thinker about logic, printed a paper with overlapping circles. They show how groups share members.
Keeper's note:
> Others drew similar pictures before him, including Leonhard Euler in 1768. Venn's paper made the overlapping circles famous. We still call them Venn diagrams.

**H3. Pingala's rhythms** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> In India, more than 2,000 years ago, Pingala wrote about poetry rhythms. Each syllable is short or long. He listed every pattern.
Keeper's note:
> One syllable: 2 patterns. Two syllables: 4. Three syllables: 8. Each new syllable doubles the list. This fixes the number of syllables, not the duration: a long syllable lasts twice a short one. Counting patterns of a fixed duration is a different question.

## Bench (a): The Likelihood Line

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> The middle of the line is shown as regions. Any spot in the right region counts.

**Opening:** Peg each card on the line. Then say why.
**Peg labels:** impossible · unlikely · even chance · likely · certain
**Reason chips:** "Look at the bag." · "I want it." · "It's random."
**After a wish chip:** Wishing is fun. Now look at the bag. What is in it?
**After a random chip:** Random means you can't know one draw. The bag still tells you which is likely.
**Order mode opening:** Put these bags in order, least likely to most likely.

**Guided item:** Pip's bag: nine reed packets, one apple. Where does "get the apple" go?
- Step 1: Is there any apple packet? *(Yes, one.)*
- Step 2: So it can happen. Not impossible.
- Step 3: Only one of ten. Unlikely.

## Bench (b): The Spinner Table

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> A screen spinner. Each spin lands at a random spot. A short run can wobble. It proves nothing about fairness.

**Buttons:** Spin · Spin again · Build · Start a run of 20 · Show the tally
**After a run:** This run is kept. Another run of 20 may look different.
**Build prompt:** Drag the edges. Make every part the same size.
**When parts match:** All parts are the same size. Each outcome is equally likely.

**Guided item:** The weather spinner. Are sun, cloud and rain equally likely?
- Step 1: How big is sun's part? *(Half.)*
- Step 2: Cloud and rain? *(A quarter each.)*
- Step 3: Different sizes, so not equally likely.

## Bench (c): The Fraction Bag

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> Same-size seeds, well mixed. Real seeds vary in size.

**Buttons:** Add a seed · Take a seed · Pick a kind · Simplify · Three ways
**Fraction builder:** Top: seeds you want. Bottom: all the seeds.
**Line caption:** Here is your chance on the 0-to-1 line.

**Guided item:** 3 bean seeds, 7 pea seeds. What is the chance of a bean?
- Step 1: Same-size seeds, mixed? *(Yes. Equally likely.)*
- Step 2: Beans you want: 3.
- Step 3: All seeds: 3 + 7 = 10.
- Step 4: Chance of a bean: 3/10.

## Bench (d): The Whole Bar

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> Made-up tables. Only outcomes that can't happen together share one bar.

**Buttons:** Cover a part · Uncover · Not · Check a table · Overlap lens
**Cover prompt:** One part is hidden. Predict it before you look.
**Check a table caption:** The parts must fill the bar exactly.

**Guided item:** The chance of rain is 0.7. What is the chance of no rain?
- Step 1: Rain and no rain fill the whole bar.
- Step 2: The whole bar is 1.
- Step 3: 1 − 0.7 = 0.3.

## Bench (e): Wren's Venn Desk

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> Made-up seed packets and spinners. The flood ledger is part of Willowbank's story.

**Opening:** Every packet goes somewhere in the box.
**Part names:** Both · Flowers only · Wet ground only · Neither
**Shade buttons:** Both · Either or both · Not a flower
**Symbol layer (optional):** both is A ∩ B · either or both is A ∪ B · not A is A′
**Count prompt:** How many in this part? Type it before the next part.
**Table opening:** Tap a cell to see its pair.
**Flood card title:** Before the end of Act III, and in play without the story: "The old Keeper's flood ledger". From the Act III ending on: "Hollis's flood ledger".

**Guided item:** 13 flowers, 9 like wet ground, 5 are both. 24 packets. Fill the circles.
- Step 1: Middle first: 5 both.
- Step 2: Flowers only: 13 − 5 = 8.
- Step 3: Wet ground only: 9 − 5 = 4.
- Step 4: Neither: 24 − 17 = 7.

## Problem sets

Eighteen three-choice items put the right answer at A six times, B six times and C six times.

### Set A. Likely words and the line (Sprout and Seedling)

**A1 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: The bag has only reed packets. Will you get a reed?
- A. Certain. Every packet is reed. *(right)*
- B. Likely. Most packets are reed. *(tests: `likely-means-will` reversed, a graded word for an endpoint)*
- C. Even chance. It could be anything. *(tests: `random-means-equal`)*
Hint: Is there anything in the bag except reed?
Explanation: Only reed in the bag, so a reed must come out.
Feedback A: Yes. Nothing else is in there.
Feedback B: Look again. Is there any packet that isn't reed?
Feedback C: It can only be reed. Nothing else is in the bag.

**A2 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Lots of reed packets, one apple. Will Pip get the apple?
- A. Impossible. There's only one. *(tests: `rare-means-impossible`)*
- B. Unlikely, but it could happen. *(right)*
- C. Likely, because Pip wants it. *(tests: `wish-reason`)*
Hint: Is the apple packet in the bag?
Explanation: One apple packet is there, so it can happen. It's just unlikely.
Feedback A: The apple is in the bag. So it can happen.
Feedback B: Yes. Rare, but possible.
Feedback C: Wanting doesn't change the bag. What's in it?

**A3 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Reed and apple packets only. Will you get a sunflower?
- A. Unlikely. Sunflowers are rare. *(tests: `rare-means-impossible` reversed)*
- B. Even chance. Anything can happen. *(tests: `random-means-equal`)*
- C. Impossible. No sunflower is in the bag. *(right)*
Hint: Is there a sunflower packet in the bag?
Explanation: No sunflower packet, so a sunflower can't come out.
Feedback A: Unlikely means it could happen. Can it, with none there?
Feedback B: Only reed or apple can come out.
Feedback C: Yes. It can't happen at all.

**A4 (Seedling, order)**
Prompt: Each bag has 10 packets. Order them: least likely reed first.
- Bag with 2 reed → Bag with 5 reed → Bag with 7 reed → Bag with 10 reed
Tests: `more-count-wins` does not apply here (same totals); ordering by apple count instead of reed.
Hint: Same size bags. Which has the fewest reed?
Explanation: 2, 5, 7, 10. Five of ten is even chance. Ten is certain.

**A5 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: One apple, five reed. Is apple "even chance"?
- A. No. Only 1 of the 6 is apple. *(right)*
- B. Yes. There are two kinds, so even. *(tests: `two-kinds-even`)*
- C. Yes. It's random, so every kind is even. *(tests: `random-means-equal`)*
Hint: Even chance needs the same number of each.
Explanation: 1 apple and 5 reed are not equal, so apple is unlikely.
Feedback A: Yes. Even chance needs equal numbers.
Feedback B: Two kinds, but not the same number of each.
Feedback C: Random, yes. But 5 reed and 1 apple aren't equal.

**A6 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Bag A: 3 reed, 1 apple. Bag B: 6 reed, 6 apple. Better for reed?
- A. Bag B. It has more reed. *(tests: `more-count-wins`)*
- B. Bag A. 3 out of 4 are reed. *(right)*
- C. You can't tell. It's all random. *(tests: `random-cant-tell`, withholds what the bags show)*
Hint: How many reed out of the whole bag?
Explanation: Bag A is 3 of 4. Bag B is 6 of 12, only half.
Feedback A: More reed, yet half the bag is apple. Look at the whole.
Feedback B: Yes. Out of the whole, Bag A has more reed.
Feedback C: One draw is random. The bags still show which is likelier.

### Set C. Fractions of the bag, on the line (Seedling and Sapling)

**C5 (Sapling, 3-choice)**

**C6 (Sapling, number)**

### Set D. The whole is 1 (Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**D1 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: The chance of rain is 0.7. Chance of no rain?
Answer: 0.3.
Worked steps: 1. Rain and no rain fill the whole bar. 2. 1 − 0.7 = 0.3.
Tests: 0.7 (`not-equals-same`); 1.7 (adding instead of taking away).
Hint: The whole bar is 1.
Explanation: No rain is the rest of the bar: 0.3.

**D2 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: A 1-to-6 spinner, equal parts. Chance of NOT landing on 3?
Answer: 5/6.
Worked steps: 1. Chance of 3: 1/6. 2. Not 3: 1 − 1/6. 3. 5/6.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **impossible**: it cannot happen. Chance 0.
- **unlikely**: it could happen, but probably won't.
- **even chance**: as likely to happen as not. Chance 1/2.
- **likely**: it will probably happen. Not a promise.
- **certain**: it must happen. Chance 1.
- **outcome**: one thing that could happen.
- **equally likely**: each outcome has the same chance.
- **random**: you can't know the next result.
- **fair**: every outcome has the same chance.
- **probability**: a number from 0 to 1 for how likely something is.
- **favorable outcome**: an outcome you are counting.
- **complement (not A)**: everything except A. Its chance is 1 − A.
- **set**: a group of things.
- **Venn diagram**: circles in a box that sort things into groups.
- **overlap (both)**: the part in both circles.
- **either or both**: every part inside the circles, counted once.
- **neither**: inside the box, outside every circle.
- **two-way table**: rows for one thing, columns for another; each cell is a pair.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** the five likelihood words with reasons from what's in the bag; ordering by "out of the whole"; equally likely versus unequal outcomes, and random versus fair; probability as favorable over total; the 0-to-1 line in fractions, decimals and percents; probabilities adding to 1 and P(not A) = 1 − P(A); Venn diagrams and two-way tables for counting outcomes.

**What it does not claim:** spinners and bags are screen models; a run of 20 spins proves nothing about fairness. It does not teach tree diagrams, drawing without putting back, or experimental versus theoretical probability (those belong to the Seed Stall's deeper pack). The flood card is not a forecast; the ledger is part of the story. There is no betting, money or coins.

**Sources in brief:** Pascal and Fermat's 1654 letters via encyclopedia and university history pages; Venn's 1880 *Philosophical Magazine* paper via the publisher's record and survey articles; Pingala's *prastāra* via university papers on Indian combinatorics. Full list in the source audit.
