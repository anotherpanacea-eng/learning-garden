## Story scenes (A story from Willowbank)

### Act II, *a town of our own*: Dot's soggy post bag

**Hook:** Dot's post bag soaks the letters. The baker offers a basket. Which one should Dot use?

**Short:**
> Dot tips out three damp letters. The baker holds up a willow basket. "Use this!" "Let's test both the same way," says {guide}.

**Longer:**
> Dot comes into the Workshop holding her satchel upside down. Three limp letters fall out.
>
> "Rain got in," she says. "Again."
>
> The baker follows with a willow basket over one arm. "Use this. It's huge. You could carry a cake in it."
>
> "It has holes," says Pip, looking through it.
>
> "Let's test them," says {guide}. "Same letters, same watering can, same time. Three tries each."
>
> Dot puts her hat straight. "And we write down every letter that gets damp."
>
> When the table is full, the satchel kept letters drier and the basket carried more. Dot looks at both. "So neither one is just better," she says. "Each one is better at something."

**Keeper's note:**
> Comparing two designs means testing them the same way and letting the results decide. Most designs have strengths and weaknesses. The next step is often to improve one: here, a basket with a waterproof lining might keep the space and lose the leaks.

### Act V, project *the new courier route*: What We Found

**Hook:** The *Gazette* has a new column. Anyone can send a report. It has to say how sure you are.

**Short:**
> The librarian pins up a card: *What We Found.* "Question, what you did, what you found, and how sure you are," they say.

**Longer:**
> "New column," says the librarian, pinning up a card: *What We Found.* "The new courier route team has already sent a report."
>
> Pip reads it aloud. "Our bags got wet. We fixed it. The end."
>
> "A start," says the librarian kindly. "A reader wants your question, what you did, what you found, with numbers, and what it means." They tap the card. "And how sure you are. People forget that line."
>
> "Why does that matter?" asks Pip.
>
> "Because then the next person knows whether to trust it," says the librarian, "or try it again."

**Keeper's note:**
> Scientists share reports so other people can check what was done and repeat it. Saying how sure you are, and why, is part of honest science. An odd result goes in the report too, with what you think caused it.

### Act VI, chapter 2, *Compare routes and keep a plan*: The Ways crate

**Hook:** The first exchange needs a crate for letters and honey jars. Wren has written what it must do.

**Short:**
> Wren reads the brief: dry letters, six jars, light enough to carry. Four ideas go on the table. None of them does everything.

**Longer:**
> The first exchange with Vadoalto leaves soon: letters up the river path, honey jars back.
>
> Wren Halloway unrolls a sheet on the Design Table. "Before anyone builds anything, here's what the crate must do," she says. "Keep twelve letters dry in the rain test. Carry six jars through the bump test. Weigh five kilos or less, empty, so one courier can carry it."
>
> "And the limits?" asks {guide}.
>
> "Shed materials only. Ten coins at most. Ready in three days."
>
> Ideas arrive: Hob's oak chest, Dot's oilcloth sack, the miller's willow basket, Pip's yoke with two baskets. Wren draws a grid.
>
> The chest costs too much, so it leaves first. On the grid, nobody's idea meets every must. Bramble folds her arms. "So we pick the least bad one?"
>
> Wren slides the grid across. "Which is most promising? Point at the cells. Then we make it better."

**Keeper's note:**
> Engineers rarely find a design that wins on every measure. They check the limits first, compare what is left on the same grid, choose the most promising, and improve it. Then they test again, because an improvement is only a prediction until it is tested.

## Station 1: Shape for a job

### Teach card (A true thing from science)
> A sketch can show how a **shape** helps something work. It doesn't need to be pretty. Label each part with its job.

### Worked example: Dot's post box (one step per tap)
1. The problem: rain sits on the flat lid and drips in.
2. Change one shape: tilt the lid into a slope.
3. Label it: "Sloped lid: rain runs off the edge."
4. Check it against the problem: no puddle, no drip.

### Guided item: the café lantern
**Prompt:** The lantern keeps tipping over. Which base would help?
- Step shown 1: "Look at the bottom. Narrow or wide?"
- Step shown 2: "Pick a label: how does that shape help?"
- Label chips: *A wide base is hard to tip* ✓ · *A narrow base looks neat* · *Any base works the same*
- Feedback (wide): "Yes. A wider base is harder to tip."

### Problem cards and label chips
- **Post box lid:** flat / sloped. Chips: *sends rain off the edge*, *looks tidier*.
- **Café lantern:** narrow / wide base. Chips: *hard to tip over*, *uses less wood*.
- **Hen-house ramp:** smooth / ridged. Chips: *gives feet a grip*, *looks smart*.
- Board line: *A sketch shows the idea. It doesn't have to look finished.*

## Station 2: Two bags, one test (A made-up model)

### Teach card (A true thing from science)
> To compare two designs, test them **the same way**. Write down every result. Then let the results decide.

### The rain test table

| Try | Satchel: damp letters (of 10) | Basket: damp letters (of 10) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | 0 | 3 |
| 2 | 1 | 4 |
| 3 | 0 | 2 |
| Total | 1 of 30 | 9 of 30 |

**Carry test:** satchel holds 6 parcels; basket holds 10.

### Worked example (one step per tap)
1. Same letters, same can, same time. Only the bag changes.
2. Add the satchel's damp letters: 0 + 1 + 0 = 1.
3. Add the basket's: 3 + 4 + 2 = 9.
4. The satchel kept letters drier. The basket carries more.

### Guided item
**Prompt:** Which bag kept more letters dry?
- Step shown: "Totals: satchel 1 damp, basket 9 damp."
- Choices: The satchel ✓ · The basket · About the same
- Feedback (satchel): "Yes. Fewer damp letters means drier."

### Strength and weakness tags
- Satchel: *Strength: keeps letters dry.* *Weakness: holds only 6 parcels.*
- Basket: *Strength: holds 10 parcels.* *Weakness: lets rain in.*
- One bag tagged "best at everything" (`single-winner`): "Check the carry test. Does one bag win both?"

### Recommendation frame
> Better for ___ (*dry letters* / *lots of parcels*) because the data showed ___ (*1 damp, not 9* / *10 parcels, not 6*).

### Improve it (choose one)
- *Line the basket with oilcloth.* "That might keep the space and stop the rain. Test it to find out."
- *Paint the basket green.* (`sketch-is-decoration`) "Would paint stop rain?"
- *Stop testing; use the satchel.* "It's small. Could a change help?"

### Side door: an unfair test
> Bramble watered the basket two minutes, the satchel one. "See? The basket's worse!"
- **Prompt:** Can that test tell which bag is better?
- Feedback: "Not fairly. The basket got more water. Test them the same way."

## Station 3: Write the brief (the Ways crate)

### Teach card (A true thing from science)
> Before building, write a **brief**: the need, who it's for, the **musts** (criteria) and the **limits** (constraints). Every must should be checkable.

### Worked example: making "a good crate" checkable (one step per tap)
1. "A good crate." Good at what? We can't check that.
2. "Keeps letters dry." Better. How many, for how long?
3. "Keeps 12 letters dry in the rain test." Now we can check it.
4. Musts say what success looks like. Limits say what you must work within.

### The brief card
- **Need:** carry letters and honey jars along the river path.
- **Who it's for:** the courier, walking.
- **Musts (criteria):** keeps 12 letters dry in the rain test · carries 6 jars through the bump test · weighs 5 kg or less, empty
- **Limits (constraints):** shed materials only · 10 coins or less · ready in 3 days

### Can we check it? (vague tiles flip to rewrites)
- *"Really strong"* → *"Carries 6 jars through the bump test"*
- *"Not too heavy"* → *"Weighs 5 kg or less, empty"*
- *"Nice"* → "Nice for whom? Try a must you can test."
- Picking a crate before the brief (`solution-first`): "Maybe. First, what must any answer do?"

### Guided item
**Prompt:** Is "costs 10 coins or less" a must or a limit?
- Step shown: "Does it say what success looks like, or what you work within?"
- Answer: Limit. Feedback: "Yes. It limits your choices. It doesn't say what the crate does."

## Station 4: Three ideas, one yardstick (A made-up model)

### Teach card (A true thing from science)
> Have **at least three ideas** before choosing. Check the limits first: pass or fail. Then compare what's left on **one grid**.

### The ideas

| Idea | Coins | Days | Dry letters (of 12) | Jars | Empty weight |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Willow basket, oilcloth lid | 7 | 2 | 11 | 6 | 3 kg |
| Oak chest | 12 | 3 | 12 | 8 | 9 kg |
| Oilcloth sack | 3 | 1 | 12 | 2 | 1 kg |
| Yoke with two baskets | 9 | 3 | 8 | 8 | 6 kg |

### Worked example: the gate, then the grid (one step per tap)
1. Gate: the oak chest costs 12 coins. The limit is 10. It leaves.
2. Grid, basket: dry 11 (missed by 1), jars 6 (met), 3 kg (met).
3. Sack: dry 12 (met), jars 2 (missed), 1 kg (met).
4. Yoke: dry 8 (missed), jars 8 (met), 6 kg (missed).
5. Nobody meets every must. The basket misses by just one letter.

### Guided item
**Prompt:** Which idea leaves at the gate?
- Step shown: the coin row lights up. "Which costs more than 10?"
- Answer: the oak chest. Feedback: "Yes. It breaks a limit, however good it is."

### Pick the most promising (because-chips)
- *Meets two musts and misses one by a single letter* ✓
- *It was the first idea* (`one-idea-only`): "First isn't a reason. Point at the grid."
- *The sack is cheapest, so it wins* (`own-yardstick`): "Cheap is good, but it carries only 2 jars."
- Waiting for a winner on every row (`must-win-every-row`): "None does. Pick the most promising, then improve it."

### Improve it
> Add an oilcloth packet for the letters. 1 coin. 7 + 1 = 8 coins, under the limit of 10.
> **Re-test** (A made-up model): 12 of 12 dry.

## Station 5: Test, predict, test again (A made-up model)

### Teach card (A true thing from science)
> Results can help you **predict** something new. A prediction is not a result. Predictions beyond what you tested need testing most.

### The footbridge results (coins held, middle of three tries)

| Folds | 0 | 4 | 8 | 12 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Coins | 2 | 6 | 10 | 14 |

### Worked example (one step per tap)
1. Each 4 extra folds held 4 more coins.
2. So the pattern is one more coin per fold.
3. 6 folds sits between 4 and 8. Predict 6 + 2 = 8 coins.
4. "I predict 8 because the results showed one more coin per fold."
5. Run the check: 8 coins. The prediction held.

### Guided item
**Prompt:** Predict for 10 folds. Use the pattern.
- Step shown: "8 folds held 10. Two more folds means two more coins."
- Answer: 12. Feedback: "Yes. And 10 is inside what we tested, so we can be fairly sure."

### Plan the check (reuses the Question Bench tokens)
- **Change:** the number of folds. **Measure:** coins held. **Keep the same:** the paper, the gap, the coins.

### Beyond the tested range
> Predict 20 folds: the pattern says 22. **Run the check:** 15. The pleats squashed flat.
> Band label: *Tested 0 to 12 folds. Beyond this, test it.*

## Station 6: How sure? Whose evidence?

### Teach card (A true thing from science)
> How sure you are depends on **how you tested**. Repeats and close results make you surer. Keep an odd result and find out why.

### Worked example: Wren's five tries (A made-up model; one step per tap)
1. Wren's tries, dry letters of 12: 12, 12, 12, 11, 12.
2. Five tries, all close together.
3. One letter got damp once. Note it.
4. Trust scale: **fairly sure**. Reasons: five repeats, close results.

### Guided item
**Prompt:** Which reason makes us surer about the crate?
- Step shown: the five tries are highlighted.
- Chips: *Wren repeated it five times* ✓ · *Pip said it was perfect* · *The crate is a nice color*
- Feedback (repeats): "Yes. Repeated, close results are worth trusting more."

### The odd try
> One extra try kept only 6 dry. Wren's note says: *lid left open.* The try stays in the table, marked, and Wren tries again.

### Evidence board (Bramble's claim)
> **Claim:** "Willow baskets always leak."
- Card 1: *Unlined basket, three rain tests: 3, 4 and 2 damp of 10.*
- Card 2: *Basket with oilcloth lid and packet, five rain tests: 59 of 60 dry.*
- Card 3: *"Baskets are pretty."*
- Card 4: *"My gran says baskets leak."*
- Mats: **Supports** · **Refutes** · **Doesn't bear on it**
- Strength ladder (low to high): *Someone said so* · *One try* · *Repeated, same-way tests*

### Keeper's Desk card: the Wrights' wind tunnel (A true thing from history)
> In 1901, the Wright brothers' glider lifted far less than published tables said. They built a wooden box with a fan and tested about 200 small model wings the same way.
>
> Their results showed the old figures didn't fit their wings, and a long-trusted number was too big. They designed their 1902 glider from their own numbers. On 17 December 1903, Orville flew their powered Flyer 120 feet (about 37 meters) in 12 seconds.
>
> Good evidence can refute an idea everyone trusted.

## Station 7: The report board

### Teach card (A true thing from science)
> A report tells others what you asked, what you did, what you found, what it means, and **how sure** you are.

### Worked example: the two-bag report (read aloud, one slot per tap)
1. **Our question:** Which bag keeps letters drier?
2. **What we did:** Watered both bags the same way, three times each.
3. **What we found:** Satchel 1 damp letter of 30. Basket 9 of 30.
4. **What it means:** The satchel keeps letters drier. The holes let rain in.
5. **How sure we are:** Fairly sure. Three tries each, results close.
6. **What we'd try next:** Line the basket and test it again.

### Guided item
**Prompt:** Pick the question for the footbridge report.
- Step shown: the footbridge table sits beside the slot.
- Tiles: *Do more folds make the bridge hold more?* ✓ · *We folded paper all morning.* · *Bridges are useful.*
- Feedback: "Yes. A question the results can answer."

### Keeper's Desk card: sharing what you found (A true thing from history)
> In 1665, the Royal Society in London began *Philosophical Transactions*, one of the first science journals. It is still published.
>
> Reports let others check what was done and try it again. Much later, other scientists checking a report before it is published (peer review) became usual.

## Mary Anderson card (A true thing from history; read at station 3)
> On a snowy day in New York, soon after 1900 (the exact year isn't known), Mary Anderson saw a streetcar driver lean out to clear the window. She designed a lever inside that swung a rubber blade across the glass, patented in 1903.
>
> One way to read her story: the need was a driver who could see in snow without leaving the seat.

### Set A: Shapes, bags, briefs and grids

- Hint: Is a cone easier to tip on its point or its wide end?
- I'm stuck: A jug with a wide bottom stays up. Now pick a base.
- Explanation: A wider base makes a thing harder to tip over.

**A3 (Seedling).** Pip's ramp sketch is messy but shows labeled ridges. Is it useful?

- Hint: What is a sketch for?
- I'm stuck: Dot's lid sketch was just lines and a label, and it worked. Now decide.

**A4 (Sprout, A made-up model).** Satchel: 1 damp letter. Basket: 9 damp. Which kept letters drier?
- Choices: The satchel ✓ · The basket, because the baker made it (`like-not-data`) · The basket, because it holds more parcels (`own-yardstick`)

- I'm stuck: One cup spilled 1 drop, another spilled 9. Which spilled less? Now try.

**A5 (Seedling, number, A made-up model).** Damp letters: satchel 0, 1, 0; basket 3, 4, 2. How many more for the basket?
- Answer: **8**

- Hint: Add each bag's tries, then take away.
- I'm stuck: 2, 2 makes 4; 1, 0 makes 1; 4 − 1 = 3. Now try.
- Explanation: The basket let in 8 more damp letters over three tries.

**A6 (Seedling).** Bramble watered the basket twice as long. Can that test pick the better bag?

**B8 (Sapling, sort).** Claim: "Willow baskets always leak." Sort each card.
- Card 1, unlined basket 3, 4, 2 damp: **Supports** (for unlined baskets).
- Card 2, lined basket 59 of 60 dry: **Refutes** "always". Feedback if put on Supports (`evidence-only-supports`): "Evidence can go against a claim. Does this one?"
- Card 3, "Baskets are pretty": **Doesn't bear on it** (`irrelevant-as-evidence`): "True maybe, but is it about leaking?"
- Card 4, "My gran says so": **Supports**, lowest on the ladder (`opinion-as-evidence`): "Someone saying so is weak. Was it tested?"
- Hint: First ask: is this card about leaking?
- I'm stuck: Claim: "Cats hate water." "My cat swims" refutes it. "Cats are fluffy" doesn't bear on it. Now sort.
- Explanation: Evidence can support, refute or miss the point, and tested evidence is stronger than a saying.

**B9 (Keeper's Desk).** Which is the strongest evidence that the crate keeps letters dry?
- Choices: Five rain tests, done the same way ✓ · Ten neighbors who all say it's dry (`all-evidence-equal`) · One try that went really well (`one-try-enough`)
- Feedback: five: "Yes. Repeated, same-way tests are strongest." Neighbors: "Many sayings still aren't a test." One: "Good, but one try can be lucky."
- Hint: Which one was actually tested more than once?
- I'm stuck: The strength ladder: someone said so, one try, repeated tests. Now pick.
- Explanation: Strength comes from how evidence was gathered, not how many people agree.

**B10 (Keeper's Desk, A true thing from history).** The Wrights' tests disagreed with the old tables. What did they do?
- Choices: Kept the old tables, since everyone trusted them (`evidence-only-supports`) · Used their own careful results to redesign ✓ · Stopped measuring and just guessed shapes
- Feedback: kept: "Trusted isn't the same as right. They followed the evidence." Redesigned: "Yes. Their 1902 glider used their own data." Guessed: "They measured more, not less: about 200 wings."
- Hint: What did they build in 1901?
- I'm stuck: Dot's scale disagreed with the baker's weight. She checked it carefully and trusted the check. Now decide.
- Explanation: Careful tests can refute a trusted idea, and good designers follow them.

**B11 (Keeper's Desk, A made-up model).** Which "How sure" tile fits Wren's five tries?
- Choices: Completely sure: tests are never, ever wrong (`false-certainty`) · Fairly sure: five close tries, one odd one ✓ · Not sure at all: one letter got damp once (`repeats-tell-nothing`)
- Feedback: completely: "Honest reports leave room for doubt." Fairly: "Yes. Say how sure, and why." Not at all: "One damp letter in 60 is a small doubt."
- Hint: How many tries, and how close were they?
- I'm stuck: Three close spinner times make you fairly sure. Now pick a tile.
- Explanation: A good report gives a degree of trust with reasons, not false certainty.

## Key terms

- **Sketch:** a quick drawing that shows how an idea works.
- **Brief:** the need, who it's for, the musts and the limits.
- **Criteria (musts):** what a design must do to count as a success.
- **Constraints (limits):** what you must work within, like coins, time or materials.
- **Trade-off:** getting more of one good thing by having less of another.
- **Prediction:** what you think will happen, with a reason, before you test.
- **Odd result:** a result that doesn't fit. Keep it, note why, test again.
- **Evidence:** what was seen or measured that supports or refutes an idea.
- **Refute:** to show an idea is wrong.
- **Report:** telling others your question, what you did, what you found, and how sure you are.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** the design cycle (sketch, compare two designs fairly, write a brief, compare several ideas on one grid, improve), predicting from results and testing the prediction, judging how sure to be, weighing evidence, and reporting. It builds on the Fair Test Bench and the Question Bench.

**What it doesn't claim:** that your explorer can design, build or test a real object alone, draw graphs, or write a full science report. Reports here are assembled from tiles and spoken; nothing typed is stored. All bag, crate, footbridge and trust numbers are made up.

**The real history, in brief:** Mary Anderson patented a window wiper in 1903. The Wright brothers tested about 200 model wings in a homemade wind tunnel in 1901, found published lift figures didn't fit their wings, and flew on 17 December 1903. The Royal Society began *Philosophical Transactions* in 1665. "Criteria and constraints" come from US science standards (NGSS). Full list in the source audit.
