## The story scenes (A story from Willowbank)

### Act I. The pages on the sill

**Short (30 words max):**
> The parcel pages dry on the bakery sill. Pip wants to use the oven. "Too hot," says the baker. "Paper likes slow." The librarian wonders how others kept books safe.

**Longer (150 words max):**
> The parcel pages lie in a row on the bakery windowsill, curling a little at the corners. Warm air drifts out from the ovens and over them.
>
> "This is taking forever," says Pip. "Why not pop them in the oven? Just for a minute."
>
> The baker shakes his head. "My oven bakes bread, and it would bake those pages too. Brown and crisp. Paper likes slow, warm air. Heat spoils it."
>
> Dot lifts the corner of one page with the tip of her hat brim. "Still damp. But the words are holding."
>
> The librarian has come to look. "Some people kept books safe for hundreds of years," they say, "in places much hotter and dustier than this. And some of those books got wet too." They tap the archive key on their palm. "There's a shelf about them. Shall we find out how they did it?"

**Keeper's note (80 words max):**
> Real book conservators dry wet paper with moving air and clean blotting paper, and they work fast, because damp paper can grow mold in about two days. They avoid heat, which can warp or scorch a soaked page and can make mold grow faster. If they cannot dry a book in time, they freeze it until they can. The baker's sill is warm, dry and breezy, which is about right.

### Act III. Nuria's chest

**Short:**
> Nuria writes that Vadoalto keeps its flood records in an old family chest. "My grandmother kept it. Now I do."

**Longer (Nuria's letter):**
> Dear Willowbank,
>
> You asked where Vadoalto keeps its flood records. In a chest. It is made of chestnut wood, with a lid that sticks, and it lives in my grandmother's front room, up off the floor, away from the window. She kept it, and her mother before her. Now I do.
>
> Inside are the flood-post books, wrapped in cloth. Every spring we open the lid on a dry day, check for damp and little holes, and air the pages. Then we close it again.
>

> Nuria

(The Spanish line needs a fluent reader before build.)

**Keeper's note:**
> Nuria's chest is a story. Family chests of books are real history too. In Timbuktu, in Mali, families have kept handwritten books in trunks and chests for generations, opening them to read, copy and check for damage. You can read about them in *The City of Books* on the archive shelf.

### Act VI. The keepers (closing card)

> Hollis wrote that the Keeper is whoever keeps the record. People have always done this job. Families in Timbuktu kept their books in chests. Scholars in Baghdad copied and translated. Printers in China carved and set type. And in Willowbank, you kept the record too.

## Book 1. The City of Books: Timbuktu and the Mali Empire (A true thing from history)

**Short:**
> Long ago, the Mali Empire in West Africa grew rich from gold and trade. In the city of Timbuktu, scholars taught and wrote. Families there still keep their handwritten books.

**Longer:**
> Around 700 years ago, the Mali Empire was large and powerful. It had gold mines, and traders crossed the Sahara Desert to buy gold and bring salt.
>
> In 1324, Mali's ruler, Mansa Musa, set out on a long journey to Mecca, a holy city for Muslims. He crossed the desert with a huge company carrying gold. In Cairo, a writer later reported that Musa's group spent and gave away so much gold that gold became cheaper there for years.
>
> The city of Timbuktu grew on the edge of the desert, near the Niger River. Scholars taught in its mosques and homes. People wrote and copied books about law, history, the stars, medicine and numbers.
>
> Families kept these handwritten books, called manuscripts, in chests and trunks for hundreds of years. Some families still do.

**Keeper's note:**
> In 2012, fighting reached Timbuktu. Librarians and families, led by a library keeper named Abdel Kader Haidara, quietly carried about 350,000 manuscripts to Mali's capital, Bamako, about 700 km away, to keep them safe. Some books left behind were burned. Many of the saved books have since been photographed so copies exist. In 2025, some of the books began going home to Timbuktu.

**Teach cards:**

1. **A city of scholars.** Timbuktu was a trading city and a place of learning. Scholars taught students in mosques and in their homes.
2. **Manuscripts.** A manuscript is a book written by hand. Timbuktu's manuscripts are mostly in Arabic. Some write local languages in Arabic letters.
3. **Who kept them?** Families did, for generations, in chests and trunks. Rain, termites and mice were always the danger.
4. **Worked example: follow one book.**
   - Step 1: A scholar writes a book about the stars.
   - Step 2: A student copies it, so there are two.
   - Step 3: A family keeps one copy in a chest for 300 years.
   - Step 4: In 2012, the family helps carry it to safety.
   - Step 5: Today it is photographed, so its words are much safer.

## Book 2. Numbers on the Move: Baghdad and al-Khwarizmi (A true thing from history)

**Short:**
> About 1,200 years ago, scholars in Baghdad studied numbers. One, al-Khwarizmi, wrote about Indian numerals. His name gave us the word "algorithm."

**Longer:**
> About 1,200 years ago, Baghdad was a great city. Its rulers paid scholars to translate books from Greek, Persian and Sanskrit into Arabic. Scholars also wrote new books about the stars, medicine and mathematics.
>
> Many books call one center of this work the House of Wisdom. Historians still argue about what it was: a palace library, a place where scholars met, or more. We know more about the scholars than the building.
>
> One scholar was al-Khwarizmi. His book about solving problems gave us the word "algebra." He also wrote about numbers from India, which used place value and a zero.
>
> Later, his book was translated into Latin. It began with his name, written "Algoritmi." That is where our word "algorithm" comes from. In 1202, an Italian merchant's son, Leonardo of Pisa, wrote a book that taught these numbers in Europe.

**Keeper's note:**
> The numerals we call "Arabic" began in India. The oldest dated inscription everyone agrees uses zero this way is from 876, in Gwalior. Leonardo of Pisa, later called Fibonacci, learned the "method of the Indians" as a boy in Bugia, in North Africa. His *Liber Abaci* (1202) helped bring the numerals to European merchants. Europe took several centuries more to use them widely.

**Teach cards:**

1. **Numbers that traveled.** The digits you write began in India. They passed through the Islamic world, then reached Europe.
2. **Place value.** Where a digit sits tells you its size. In 352, the 3 means three hundreds.
3. **Zero holds a place.** Zero means "nothing in this place." It keeps 105 different from 15.
4. **Algorithm.** An algorithm is a list of exact steps. Follow them in order and you always get there.
5. **Worked example: where "algorithm" came from.**
   - Step 1: al-Khwarizmi writes about Indian numerals in Arabic.
   - Step 2: Someone translates his book into Latin.
   - Step 3: It begins with his name, "Algoritmi."
   - Step 4: Over time, the name becomes our word "algorithm."

## Book 3. Paper, Print and a Water Clock: Song China (A true thing from history)

**Short:**
> Paper and printing began in China. About 1,000 years ago, Bi Sheng made clay letters he could use again. Su Song built a clock tower that ran on water.

**Longer:**
> Paper was made in China more than 2,000 years ago. Later, Chinese printers carved a whole page into a block of wood, inked it and pressed paper on top. The oldest whole printed book with a date that survives was made this way, in 868.
>
> In the 1040s, a craftsman named Bi Sheng tried something new. He made each character out of clay and baked it hard. He set the characters in an iron frame to print a page, then took them out to use again. We know about him because a writer, Shen Kuo, described his method.
>
> In the city of Kaifeng, an official named Su Song led the building of a clock tower about 12 meters tall. A water wheel turned it, one small step at a time. It showed the hours and turned a model of the sky.

**Keeper's note:**
> Shen Kuo noticed that movable type was slow for a few copies and very fast for hundreds or thousands. Su Song's tower used an escapement, a part that lets a wheel move one step at a time. His book about it was first printed in 1094, and later copies keep its drawings. In Korea, a book printed with metal type in 1377 survives. Gutenberg's press in Europe came about 1450.

**Teach cards:**

1. **Woodblock printing.** Carve a whole page into wood. Ink it. Press paper on it. Print the same page many times.
2. **Movable type.** Make each character separately. Arrange them into a page. Afterwards, take them apart and reuse them.
3. **The water clock tower.** Each scoop on the water wheel filled, then tipped the wheel on one step. That kept steady time.
4. **Worked example: printing one page with movable type.**
   - Step 1: Pick the clay characters you need.
   - Step 2: Set them in rows in an iron frame.
   - Step 3: Ink them and press paper on top.
   - Step 4: Print as many copies as you need.
   - Step 5: Take the characters out for the next page.

**Comparison card: printing in other places (A true thing from history):**
> **Korea, 1377:** monks printed a book called *Jikji* with metal type. One volume survives, in Paris.
> **Europe, about 1450:** Johannes Gutenberg built a printing press with metal type in Mainz. His Bible was finished about 1455. Printing spread quickly across Europe. China and Korea had printed books long before. Gutenberg's way changed Europe.

## The Numeral Bench

**Card:** Build one number three ways. Then add two numbers three ways.

**Tray labels:** Roman · Egyptian · Place value (our digits)

**Tile labels:**
- Roman: I is 1 · V is 5 · X is 10 · L is 50 · C is 100
- Egyptian: stroke is 1 · hobble is 10 · coil of rope is 100 (see the Nile Year number tray)
- Place value: hundreds · tens · ones

**Teach card:**
> Roman and Egyptian numerals show size with different symbols. Our numerals show size by place. That is why we need a zero.

**Worked example: 47 + 38 three ways** (one step per tap)

*Egyptian*
1. 47 is 4 hobbles and 7 strokes. 38 is 3 hobbles and 8 strokes.
2. Put them together: 7 hobbles and 15 strokes.
3. Swap 10 strokes for 1 hobble: 8 hobbles and 5 strokes.
4. That is 85.

*Roman*
1. 47 is XLVII. 38 is XXXVIII.
2. XL is tricky: it means 10 before 50, so 40. Rewrite it as XXXX.
3. Put them together: XXXXXXX, VV, IIIII.
4. Tidy up: five I's make V, two V's make X. Now you have eight X's and a V.
5. Five X's make L. That leaves L, XXX, V: LXXXV. That is 85.

*Place value*
1. Write 47 above 38, ones under ones.
2. Ones: 7 + 8 = 15. Write 5, carry 1 ten.
3. Tens: 4 + 3 + 1 = 8.
4. That is 85.

**After the worked example:**
> Same answer every way. Count what you wrote: Roman needed 5 symbols for 85, Egyptian 13, and our way just 2 digits.

**Zero card:** Write 105 and 15 in each tray.
> Roman: CV and XV. Egyptian: 1 coil and 5 strokes, and 1 hobble and 5 strokes. Our way: 105 and 15. Only our way needs a 0 to show "no tens."

## Follow the Steps

**Card:** An algorithm is a list of exact steps. Follow each one in order.

**About the name (A true thing from history):** The word "algorithm" comes from al-Khwarizmi's name. He wrote about step-by-step ways to calculate.

**Note on the methods:** Nobody knows who first used halve and double. Scribes in ancient Egypt multiplied by doubling too.

### Procedure 1: Halve and double

**Steps:**
1. Write the two numbers side by side.
2. Halve the left number. Ignore any half. Double the right number.
3. Keep going until the left number is 1.
4. Cross out rows where the left number is even.
5. Add the right numbers that are left.

**Worked example: 13 × 6** (one row per tap)

| Left (halve) | Right (double) | Keep? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 13 | 6 | odd: keep |
| 6 | 12 | even: cross out |
| 3 | 24 | odd: keep |
| 1 | 48 | odd: keep |

> Add the kept rows: 6 + 24 + 48 = 78. So 13 × 6 = 78.

### Procedure 2: Balance both sides

**Teach card:** A balance stays level if you do the same thing to both sides. That is the idea of balancing.

**Worked example:** ? + 3 = 10
1. Take 3 from the left side. Only ? is left.
2. Take 3 from the right side too. 10 − 3 = 7.
3. So ? = 7. Check: 7 + 3 = 10.

### Fix the steps

**Card:** One step is not clear. Choose the clearer step.

- Vague: "Do the rest of the rows." · Clear: "Halve the left number. Double the right number. Repeat until the left number is 1."
- **After choosing:** Exact steps work for anyone. "Do the rest" only works if you already know how.

## Keeping Paper Safe

**Card:** Sort each card. Does it harm paper, help keep it, or does it depend?

| Card | Bin | Feedback after sorting |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A damp cellar | harms | Damp makes paper soft and lets mold grow. |
| Termites in the wood | harms | Termites eat paper and wood. Families checked for little holes. |
| A candle left by the pages | harms | Fire is the quickest way to lose a book. |
| A dusty shelf | harms | Dust holds damp and scratches pages. A cloth wrap helps. |
| An oven to dry wet pages | harms | Too hot. It can scorch or warp wet paper. |
| A dry room that stays the same | helps | Steady, dry air is what old paper likes. |
| A fan and clean paper towels | helps | Moving air and blotting dry wet pages gently. |
| A copy kept in another house | helps | If one copy is lost, the words survive. |
| Photos of every page | helps | A photo copy keeps the words even if the paper fades. |
| Many families each keeping some | helps | One flood or fire cannot reach them all. |
| A wooden chest | depends | A chest keeps out dust and light. It can trap damp, so air it on dry days. |
| A sunny window | depends | Sunlight fades ink and yellows paper. A warm, shaded sill is better. |

### Set A. Numbers that traveled

**A1 · Sprout · 3-choice**
- **Prompt:** In 105, what does the 0 tell you?
- **Choices:** (a) There are no tens. ✔ · (b) The whole number is nothing. · (c) You can leave it out.
- **Misconceptions:** (b) zero makes the whole number worth nothing; (c) zero is only decoration, so 105 = 15.
- **Feedback:** (a) Yes. The 0 holds the tens place empty. · (b) 105 is a lot! The 0 only says "no tens." · (c) Without the 0, 105 turns into 15.
- **Hint:** Read the places: hundreds, tens, ones.
- **Easier one first:** In 20, the 0 says "no ones." So 20 is 2 tens. Now look at the middle digit of 105.
- **Explanation:** Zero keeps a place empty so the other digits stay in the right places.

**A2 · Sprout · 3-choice**
- **Prompt:** You build 23 in Egyptian. How many hobbles do you need?
- **Choices:** (a) 3 · (b) 2 ✔ · (c) 23
- **Misconceptions:** (a) mixes up tens and ones; (c) one sign for every unit.
- **Feedback:** (a) 3 is the ones. Hobbles show the tens. · (b) Right. 2 hobbles and 3 strokes. · (c) A hobble stands for 10, so you need far fewer.
- **Hint:** One hobble is worth 10.
- **Easier one first:** 12 is 1 hobble and 2 strokes. How many tens are in 23?
- **Explanation:** 23 is 2 tens and 3 ones: 2 hobbles and 3 strokes.

**A3 · Seedling · 3-choice · guided**
- **Prompt:** What number is XIV?
- **Steps shown before answering:** X is 10. · IV is 1 before 5, so 4. · 10 + 4 = ?
- **Choices:** (a) 16 · (b) 4 · (c) 14 ✔
- **Misconceptions:** (a) reads IV as 6, adding when the I comes first; (b) reads only the last part.
- **Feedback:** (a) I before V means take away: IV is 4. · (b) Don't forget the X at the front. · (c) Yes. X is 10 and IV is 4.
- **Hint:** A small symbol before a bigger one means take it away.
- **Easier one first:** VI is 5 + 1 = 6. IV is 5 − 1 = 4. Now add the X.
- **Explanation:** XIV is 10 + 4 = 14.

**A4 · Seedling · number answer**
- **Prompt:** Add XXIII and XIV. Write your answer in our digits.
- **Answer:** 37
- **Worked steps:** XXIII is 23. XIV is 14. 23 + 14 = 37. In Roman: XXXVII.
- **Hint:** Change each to our digits first.
- **Easier one first:** XI + II: 11 + 2 = 13. Now try 23 + 14.
- **Explanation:** Roman numerals are hard to add on paper. Our digits let you add in columns.

**A5 · Seedling · 3-choice**
- **Prompt:** Which way of writing numbers needs a sign for "nothing here"?
- **Choices:** (a) Roman numerals · (b) Our digits ✔ · (c) Egyptian numerals
- **Misconceptions:** (a) thinks Roman numerals have a zero; (c) thinks every system needs a zero.
- **Feedback:** (a) Roman numerals have no zero. Each symbol has its own size. · (b) Yes. A 0 keeps a place empty. · (c) Egyptian signs show size by shape, so empty places need no sign.
- **Hint:** Which way shows size by place?
- **Easier one first:** Write 105 in Roman: CV. No zero needed. Now try it our way.
- **Explanation:** Only place value needs a zero, because the place gives each digit its size.

**A6 · Sapling · number answer**
- **Prompt:** Add 256 and 137 in columns. What is the total?
- **Answer:** 393
- **Worked steps:** Ones: 6 + 7 = 13; write 3, carry 1. Tens: 5 + 3 + 1 = 9. Hundreds: 2 + 1 = 3. Total 393.
- **Hint:** Start with the ones column.
- **Easier one first:** 16 + 7: 6 + 7 = 13, write 3, carry 1; 1 + 1 = 2. So 23.
- **Explanation:** The carry moves ten ones into the tens place. Place value makes that one small step.

**A7 · Sapling · 3-choice**
- **Prompt:** Halve and double for 12 × 5. Which right-side numbers do you add?
- **Table shown:** 12 | 5 · 6 | 10 · 3 | 20 · 1 | 40
- **Choices:** (a) 5 and 10 · (b) 20 and 40 ✔ · (c) All four rows
- **Misconceptions:** (a) keeps the even rows; (c) skips the cross-out step.
- **Feedback:** (a) Those rows have even left numbers. Cross them out. · (b) Yes. 20 + 40 = 60. · (c) Step 4 crosses out even rows first.
- **Hint:** Keep rows where the left number is odd.
- **Easier one first:** 3 × 4: rows 3 | 4 and 1 | 8. Both odd. 4 + 8 = 12.
- **Explanation:** Rows 3 | 20 and 1 | 40 are odd, so 12 × 5 = 20 + 40 = 60.

**A8 · Sapling · 3-choice**
- **Prompt:** ? + 5 = 12. What do you take from both sides?
- **Choices:** (a) 7 · (b) 12 · (c) 5 ✔
- **Misconceptions:** (a) mixes up the step with the answer; (b) takes away the total.
- **Feedback:** (a) 7 is the answer. The step is what you take away. · (b) Taking 12 leaves nothing on the right. · (c) Yes. Then ? = 12 − 5 = 7.
- **Hint:** What is added to the ? on the left?
- **Easier one first:** ? + 2 = 5. Take 2 from both sides: ? = 3.
- **Explanation:** Taking 5 from both sides keeps the balance level and leaves ? = 7.

**A9 · Keeper's Desk · 3-choice**
- **Prompt:** Fibonacci's book appeared in 1202. Which Roman numeral is 1202?
- **Choices:** (a) MCCII ✔ · (b) MCMII · (c) MMCII
- **Misconceptions:** (b) reads CM as 200 (CM is 900); (c) writes one M too many for the thousands.
- **Feedback:** (a) Yes. M is 1000, CC is 200, II is 2. · (b) CM means 900, so that is 1902. · (c) MM is 2000, so that is 2102.
- **Hint:** Split 1202 into 1000, 200 and 2.
- **Easier one first:** 1100 is M and C: MC. Now add one more C and II.
- **Explanation:** 1000 + 200 + 2 is M + CC + II, written MCCII.

**A10 · Keeper's Desk · say why (a grown-up reads it)**
- **Prompt:** Why is adding easier with place value? Tell a grown-up.
- **Grown-up note:** Listen for any of these: each place means ten of the one to its right, so you add column by column; you carry when a column reaches ten; zero keeps places lined up; the numbers stay short. There is no single right wording.

- **Prompt:** Shen Kuo said movable type was slow for a few copies. Why?
- **Choices:** (a) Clay letters print slower than ink · (b) Setting the type takes time first ✔ · (c) Printers only made big books
- **Misconceptions:** (a) blames the material; (c) a guess the source does not support.
- **Feedback:** (a) Printing each sheet was quick. The slow part came first. · (b) Yes. Setting a page takes time, then copies are fast. · (c) The source does not say that.
- **Hint:** Think about the copying bench's start-up time.
- **Easier one first:** In the model, carving took 60 days before any print. Movable type has a start-up step too.
- **Explanation:** Setting type is a start-up cost. Spread over many copies, it hardly matters.

### Set C. Who kept the record?

**C1 · Sprout · 3-choice**
- **Prompt:** Which of these harms old paper?
- **Choices:** (a) A dry, steady room · (b) A copy in another house · (c) A damp cellar ✔
- **Misconceptions:** (a) and (b) both help; a reader who picks them may think any storage harms paper.
- **Feedback:** (a) Dry, steady air is good for paper. · (b) A copy keeps the words safe. · (c) Yes. Damp softens paper and lets mold grow.
- **Hint:** What did the parcel pages get?
- **Easier one first:** The parcel got soaked in the storm. Was that good for it?
- **Explanation:** Damp is one of paper's worst enemies.

**C2 · Sprout · 3-choice** (A story from Willowbank)
- **Prompt:** Pip wants to dry the pages in the oven. Is that a good idea?
- **Choices:** (a) Yes, heat dries paper best · (b) No, ovens are too hot for paper ✔ · (c) Yes, if they only stay in a minute
- **Misconceptions:** (a) heat dries paper best; (c) a short time in strong heat is harmless.
- **Feedback:** (a) Heat can scorch or warp wet paper. · (b) Yes. Slow, warm, moving air is safer. · (c) Even a minute can brown a damp page.
- **Hint:** What did the baker say?
- **Easier one first:** What happens to bread in the oven? It goes brown and crisp.
- **Explanation:** Book keepers dry paper with moving air, never with an oven.

**C3 · Seedling · true or not true from this source · guided**
- **Source card (A true thing from history; a modern summary):** Ibn Battuta, a traveler from Morocco, visited Mali about 1352. Later, in Fez, a scholar named Ibn Juzayy wrote down his story.
- **Steps shown before answering:** Who traveled? · Who wrote it down? · Where?
- **Statement:** Ibn Battuta wrote his book himself while he was in Mali.
- **Answer:** Not true from this source.
- **Misconception tested:** an eyewitness account is written on the spot by the eyewitness.
- **Feedback (true):** Look again: someone else wrote it down, later, in Fez. · **(not true):** Yes. Ibn Juzayy wrote it down later, in Fez.
- **Hint:** Find the words "wrote down."
- **Easier one first:** Dot reads Nuria's letter aloud and Pip writes it down. Who wrote the words?
- **Explanation:** Ibn Battuta told his story; Ibn Juzayy wrote it down years later. That matters when we judge details.

**C4 · Seedling · number answer**
- **Prompt:** Musa's journey was 1324. Ibn Battuta came about 1352. How many years apart?
- **Answer:** 28 (about 28)
- **Worked steps:** 1352 − 1324. 1352 − 1300 = 52. 52 − 24 = 28.
- **Follow-up shown after:** So Ibn Battuta did not see Musa's journey. He heard about Mali's past from others.
- **Hint:** Take 1324 away from 1352.
- **Easier one first:** 1330 − 1324 = 6 years.
- **Explanation:** About 28 years apart. A visitor in 1352 could not see something from 1324.

**C5 · Sapling · match the source to who made it**
- **Sources:** al-Umari's report on Musa's gold · The Catalan Atlas, with a ruler holding gold · Ibn Battuta's account of Mali · Timbuktu's family manuscripts
- **Makers:** A writer in Cairo · A mapmaker on Majorca, an island far from Mali · A traveler, written down later in Fez · Scholars and families in Timbuktu
- **Answer:** al-Umari → writer in Cairo; Catalan Atlas → mapmaker on Majorca; Ibn Battuta → traveler, written down in Fez; manuscripts → scholars and families in Timbuktu.
- **Misconception tested:** all sources about a place were made in that place.
- **Hint:** Only one source was made in Timbuktu itself.
- **Easier one first:** Nuria's chest holds Vadoalto's own records. Who made them? Vadoalto's people.
- **Explanation:** Three of these were made far from Mali. Each maker knew different things.

**C6 · Sapling · 3-choice**
- **Prompt:** Why does keeping books in many homes help keep them safe?
- **Choices:** (a) One flood or fire can't reach them all ✔ · (b) Books last longer when they are moved often · (c) More homes means less damp
- **Misconceptions:** (b) moving books protects them; (c) confuses number of places with dryness.
- **Feedback:** (a) Yes. If one place is lost, others survive. · (b) Moving books can harm them. Spreading them out is the help. · (c) Each home still has to keep its books dry.
- **Hint:** Think about a fire in one house.
- **Easier one first:** You keep two copies of a drawing, at home and at a friend's. One gets wet. What's left?
- **Explanation:** Spreading books out means no single disaster can take them all.

**C7 · Keeper's Desk · 3-choice**
- **Prompt:** Three sources say Mali had great gold. What does that agreement show best?
- **Choices:** (a) Every number in them is exact · (b) They must have copied each other · (c) Mali's gold was famous far away ✔
- **Misconceptions:** (a) agreement proves every detail; (b) agreement always means copying, so it shows nothing.
- **Feedback:** (a) They agree on gold, not on every number. · (b) They were made in different places by different people. · (c) Yes. Cairo, Majorca and a traveler all knew of it.
- **Hint:** Where was each source made?
- **Easier one first:** Dot, the miller and Pip all say the bridge creaked. What can you be fairly sure of?
- **Explanation:** Separate sources agreeing makes the main point stronger. Details still need checking one by one.

**C8 · Keeper's Desk · number answer, then say why**
- **Source card (A true thing from history; a modern summary of a translation):** al-Umari, writing in Cairo, reported that a mithqal of gold was worth at least 25 dirhams before Musa came, and 22 or less after.
- **Prompt:** By his report, how many dirhams did the price fall?
- **Answer:** 3 (at least 3)
- **Worked steps:** 25 − 22 = 3. He says "22 or less," so the fall was at least 3.
- **Say why (a grown-up reads it):** What would you want to check about his report?
- **Grown-up note:** Good answers ask how he knew (he wrote years later, from what people in Cairo told him), whether other records of prices agree, or what a mithqal and a dirham were. No single right answer.
- **Hint:** Take the after price from the before price.
- **Easier one first:** A loaf cost 10 coins and now costs 8. It fell by 2.
- **Explanation:** A fall from 25 to 22 is 3 dirhams, about one-eighth. One writer's numbers are a clue to check, not the last word.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** three real medieval centers of learning outside Europe (Timbuktu in the Mali Empire, Abbasid Baghdad, Song China) with dated facts; how numerals with place value and zero traveled from India through the Islamic world to Europe and why they make written arithmetic easier; that an algorithm is a list of exact steps (the word comes from al-Khwarizmi's name); that printing began in China long before Gutenberg and pays off for many copies; how paper is harmed and kept safe; and how to ask who made a source and check it against others.

**What it does not claim:** it does not describe the House of Wisdom's building, because historians debate what it was; it does not convert Mansa Musa's wealth into modern money or rank him as "the richest person ever"; it does not say al-Khwarizmi invented the halve-and-double method; the copying bench's numbers are invented for comparison and are not historical rates. Willowbank, its animals and Nuria's chest are fiction.

**About 2012-2013:** the Timbuktu reading mentions, briefly and without detail, that fighting reached the city and that librarians and families moved the manuscripts to safety. It names no one hurt. You may want to read it first.
