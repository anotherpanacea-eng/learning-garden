## Arriving

**Place title:** The Snow Well

**First visit (A story from Willowbank):** Up the hill from the mill, a round stone hut covers an old pit. Long ago, the town packed winter snow in it to keep ice for summer. Now it holds a long table, a globe and a tank of water.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. The ice won't stay under (Act II, chapter 2: a garden across the seasons, in winter)

**Short:** Ice covers the garden trough. Pip pushes it down. It bobs right back up. "It won't stay under!" he says.

**Longer:** Winter had come to the garden beds. A lid of ice sat on the water trough. Pip pressed it with both paws. Down it went, and up it bobbed.

"It won't stay under," he said. "It's cheating."

Snow began to fall. A flake landed on Pip's dark sleeve. The librarian held up a lens. "Look. Six arms. Snow is made of tiny ice crystals."

"Can I eat it?" asked Pip.

"It's water," said {guide}. "Frozen water. But not from the ground, please."

The miller leaned on the gate. "Up the hill there's an old snow well. Long ago, people packed snow in it to keep ice for summer."

"Ice in summer," said Pip. "Show me everything."

**Keeper's note (A true thing from history):** In the Spanish Pyrenees, people once built snow wells. They packed snow in layers with straw until it turned to ice. In summer they cut blocks and carried them to towns at night. About 150 old snow wells have been found in one province, Huesca.

### Scene 2. Penguins on strings (Act II, chapter 4: the first show)

**Short:** Pip wants the penguins to fly onto the stage on strings. Dot shakes her head, and her hat wobbles. "Penguins don't fly," she says. "They swim."

**Longer:** The Story Loft was building its first show. Pip had painted six paper penguins and tied each one to a string.

"They fly in from the top," he said. "Whoosh."

Dot tipped back her hat. "Penguins don't fly. Their wings are flippers. They swim."

"Then what do they do all show?"

Bramble stood up with a paper egg balanced on her boots. "I'm the emperor father," she said. "I hold the egg on my feet all winter. In the dark. In the wind."

The magpie tried to take the egg.

"Nobody touches it," said Bramble.

"We said on the poster it's a true show," said {guide}. "So let's make every scene true. Where they live. How they swim. How they keep warm."

Pip untied the strings. "Fine," he said. "They can waddle in."

### Scene 3. Above the trees (Act III, chapter 3: the route by the high ford)

**Short:** High on the path, the trees stop. Only moss, grass and tiny flowers grow. Snow still lies in the hollows. "Is this the Arctic?" asks Pip.

**Longer:** {courier} led the way up the old path, past the last faded ribbon. The trees grew shorter, then stopped. Up here there were only mosses, tough grasses and tiny flowers pressed close to the ground. Snow still lay in the hollows, long after it had melted in the valley.

Pip sniffed a cushion of moss. "Is this the Arctic?"

"It looks a little like the tundra," said {guide}. "Cold, windy, no trees. But we're nowhere near the North Pole."

"Then why is it so cold?"

"Because we're high up," said {courier}. "Climb high enough, almost anywhere, and it gets cold."

Far below, the brook glittered.

"Where does all this snow go when it melts?" asked Pip.

{guide} pointed down the valley. "Follow the water."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** Bare slopes high on mountains, above the trees, can look like tundra. The Arctic tundra is cold because it is far from the equator. Mountains are cold because they are high. Same look, different cause.

### Scene 4. A damp letter (Act III, chapter 4: letters up and down the river)

**Short:** Léon writes from Les Saules: "Every spring our river runs full and fast. Where does all that water come from?"

**Longer:** The letter from Les Saules was a little damp, like Léon himself.

"Every spring our river runs full and fast," he wrote. "The ferry folk say the mountains are melting. Is there a mountain made of ice up there?"

{guide} read it twice. "Not a mountain of ice. Snow. All winter it piles up on the peaks. In spring it melts and runs down to the brook, then on to Léon's river, then to the sea."

"And then?" asked Pip.

"Water rises from the sea into the air, makes clouds, and snows on the mountains again," said {guide}. "Some of the world's water stays frozen much longer. Thousands of years, in great sheets of ice."

"Let's write back," said Pip. "With a map of everywhere water hides."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** In the high Pyrenees, many rivers run highest in late spring, when the winter snow melts.

### Scene 5. Many voices (Act IV, chapter 3: the newspaper's two explanations, and what other places did)

**Short:** The librarian adds a thin folder to the Travelers' Archive: "Many voices at the top of the world." "Who decides about a frozen sea?" asks Dot.

**Longer:** The *Gazette* had printed its two stories side by side, and the town was still arguing. The librarian added a thin folder to the Travelers' Archive. On the cover: *Many voices at the top of the world.*

Inside were cards: a fishing captain, a shipping company, a scientist, a person from an Arctic Indigenous community, a government, a group that protects the sea.

"Who's right?" asked Pip.

"Each of them needs something real," said the librarian. "Sometimes the needs clash. Then people have to keep talking."

Dot turned a card over. "This one's people have lived there for thousands of years."

"That matters," said {guide}.

Bramble folded her arms. "This isn't about our fields."

"No," said the librarian. "It's someone else's hard question. Worth knowing, that's all."

### Scene 6. A question for the blank page (Act V: a question of your own)

**Short:** On a page headed *For the next Keeper*, you write: "Why does cold water sink?" {guide} smiles. "The Cold Sea Engine can help you start."

**Longer:** The blank pages lay open on the library table. Pip had already written *SNACKS* on one and crossed it out.

You write a question instead: *Why does cold water sink?*

"Good question," said {guide}. "Cold water from the far north and south sinks and moves around the whole world. Slowly."

"How slowly?" asked Pip.

"A trip all the way round can take about a thousand years."

Pip looked at his page. "I'll write faster than that."

## Teach cards

Each worked example reveals one step per tap.

**T1. Top and bottom** (A true thing about science). Earth has a North Pole at the top and a South Pole at the bottom. They are the coldest places.
Worked example: (1) Find the middle line, the equator. (2) Go up to the very top: the North Pole. (3) Go down to the bottom: the South Pole.

**T2. Ice and snow are water** (A true thing about science). Water can be a liquid you pour. Frozen, it is solid: ice and snow. It's the same water.
Worked example: (1) An ice cube on a warm plate. (2) It melts into a puddle. (3) Put the puddle in the cold. (4) Ice again.

**T3. Ice floats** (A true thing about science). A cup of ice is lighter than a cup of water. So ice floats, a tiny cube or a giant iceberg.
Worked example: (1) Drop a cube in the tank. (2) It floats. (3) Push it down. (4) It bobs back up.

**T4. Most of an iceberg hides** (A true thing about science). An iceberg is a huge chunk of ice floating in the sea. Only its tip shows. Most of it is under the water.
Worked example: (1) Look at the cube from the side. (2) Find the water line. (3) Most of the cube is below it.

**T5. Snow is tiny ice crystals** (A true thing about science). A snowflake is made of tiny ice crystals. Many snow crystals have six sides or six arms.
Worked example: (1) Catch a flake on a dark sleeve. (2) Look through the lens. (3) Count the arms: six.

**T6. Penguins are swimming birds of the south** (A true thing about science). Penguins are birds. Each wing is a stiff flipper, so they can't fly, but they swim very well. Wild penguins live in the south of the world.
Worked example: (1) A penguin has feathers and a beak: a bird. (2) Its wings are stiff flippers. (3) It flies through water, not air.

**T7. An emperor winter** (A true thing about science). Emperor penguins are the biggest penguins. In winter they huddle to share warmth. The father holds the egg on his feet.
Worked example: (1) The mother lays one egg. (2) She passes it to the father's feet. (3) He tucks it under a warm fold of skin. (4) Later, both parents take turns with the chick.

**T8. The tundra** (A true thing about science). The Arctic tundra is wide, cold land with no trees. Just under the surface, the ground stays frozen all year. That's permafrost.
Worked example: (1) Look at the cutaway. (2) Top: a thin layer of soil. (3) Below: frozen ground. (4) Tree roots can't go deep.

**T9. A short, busy summer** (A true thing about science). In summer, only the top layer thaws. For a few weeks, mosses, lichens, grasses and flowers grow fast, and animals come to eat.
Worked example: (1) Winter: all frozen. (2) July: the top layer thaws. (3) Plants grow. (4) Caribou arrive to graze.

**T10. Slanted sunlight** (A true thing about science). Near the equator, the Sun shines down from high up. Near the poles, sunlight comes in low and spreads out, so each patch warms less.
Worked example: (1) Shine the beam straight down: one small bright patch. (2) Tilt the card. (3) Same beam, bigger patch. (4) Each part gets less.

**T11. Five climate zones** (A true thing about science). Scientists sort climates into five main zones: tropical, dry, temperate, continental and polar. In a polar climate, even the warmest month stays below 10 °C (50 °F).
Worked example: (1) Slide from the equator. (2) Warm all year: tropical. (3) Further: temperate. (4) Near the poles: polar.

**T12. A desert of ice** (A true thing about science). A desert is a place with very little rain or snow. Heat doesn't matter. The middle of Antarctica gets less than 50 mm a year.
Worked example: (1) Deserts get less than 250 mm a year. (2) Antarctica's middle gets less than 50. (3) So it's a desert, a cold one.

**T13. The cryosphere** (A true thing about science). The cryosphere is all the frozen water on Earth: snow, sea ice, glaciers, ice sheets and frozen ground. Most fresh water is frozen.
Worked example: (1) Of 100 drops of fresh water: (2) About 69 are frozen. (3) About 30 are underground. (4) About 1 is in lakes, rivers, air and living things.

**T14. Ice in the water cycle** (A true thing about science). Water rises from the sea, falls as snow, piles up as ice, and melts back to the sea. Ice on land adds water to the sea when it melts.
Worked example: (1) Ice on a rock in a glass. (2) It melts. (3) The water level rises. (4) Floating ice barely changes it.

**T15. Cold, salty water sinks** (A true thing about science). Cold water is heavier for its size than warm. Salty water is heavier than fresh. Cold, salty polar water sinks and drives slow currents around the world.
Worked example: (1) New sea ice freezes. (2) Most salt stays in the water. (3) The water gets saltier and colder. (4) It sinks.

**T16. Bright ice, dark sea** (A true thing about science). Bright ice bounces most sunlight back to space. Dark ocean takes most in and warms. How much bounces back is called albedo.
Worked example: (1) Ice bounces back 60 of 100 parts. (2) Ocean bounces back 6. (3) Melt the ice: more sunlight warms the sea. (4) That melts more ice.

**T17. Rising water feeds the south** (A true thing about science). Around Antarctica, deep water rises up. It carries nutrients. Tiny drifting plants, phytoplankton, grow on them, and krill eat the plants.
Worked example: (1) Deep water rises. (2) Nutrients reach the sunny top. (3) Phytoplankton grow. (4) Krill feast, then whales, seals and penguins.

**T18. Two poles, two ways of living** (A true thing about science). The Arctic is a frozen sea ringed by land, with land animals and people. Antarctica is an icy continent ringed by sea. Its life is in the sea.
Worked example: (1) Arctic: tundra and sea. (2) Caribou, foxes, people. (3) Antarctica: ice and sea. (4) Penguins and seals feed at sea.

**T19. Then and now** (A true thing from history). From 1897 to 1922, teams raced to reach places first, with ships, dogs and sledges. Today, scientists use satellites, GPS and icebreakers to ask what the ice is doing.
Worked example: (1) Then: goal, be first. (2) Then: tools, dogs and a compass. (3) Now: goal, understand the ice. (4) Now: tools, satellites and ice drills.

**T20. Many voices, shared rules** (Keeper's Desk; A true thing from history). Many people have a stake in the poles. Agreements help them share. Agreements only work while members keep agreeing.
Worked example: (1) A protected sea is proposed. (2) Every member must agree. (3) Some want fishing. (4) Talks can take years.

## History cards

**Snow wells of the Pyrenees (A true thing from history).** In Spain, people built snow wells in the mountains. They packed snow in layers with straw. In summer they cut ice blocks and carried them to towns at night.

**Ice houses far away (A true thing from history).** In Iran, people built tall domed ice houses called yakhchāl. Some are hundreds of years old and still stand. In Korea, a stone ice store at Gyeongju still stands too.

**The snowflake watchers (A true thing from history).** In 1611, Johannes Kepler wrote a little book asking why snowflakes have six corners. In 1885, Wilson Bentley first photographed a snowflake. In 1936, Ukichiro Nakaya in Japan grew the first snow crystal in a lab, on a rabbit hair.

**Shirase's journey (A true thing from history).** In January 1912, a Japanese team led by Nobu Shirase reached Antarctica on the ship *Kainan Maru*. A small party reached 80° south. They named the plain the Snow Plain of Japan. Everyone came home.

**The Heroic Age (A true thing from history).** From 1897 to 1922, about seventeen teams from many countries went to Antarctica. They had ships, dogs, skis and sledges. Many did real science, but being first mattered most.

**Keeper's note (A true thing from history; Sapling and Keeper's Desk only):** The Heroic Age was dangerous. Twenty-two people on those expeditions died, far from home. Today's stations have heat, radios, doctors and planes.

**Six at the Pole (A true thing from history).** On 12 November 1969, six women became the first women at the South Pole. They linked arms and stepped off the plane together, so all six were first.

**Watching from above (A true thing from history).** Satellites have watched the Arctic sea ice every year since 1979. Now scientists can see the whole Arctic at once.

**The Ross Sea (A true thing from history).** In 2016, the countries that look after Antarctica's sea life all agreed to protect the Ross Sea. It is about 1.55 million km². Every member had to say yes.

**A seat at the table (A true thing from history).** The Arctic Council began in 1996. Eight Arctic countries are members. Six Indigenous peoples' organizations sit with them, and their views must be asked.

**The Paris Agreement (A true thing from history).** In 2015, nearly every country agreed to try to keep Earth's warming well below 2 °C. Countries can join and can leave. Keeping everyone in is hard.

**A disagreement about a passage (A true thing from history, Keeper's Desk).** Canada says the Northwest Passage is Canadian water. The United States says ships may pass through freely. Since 1988 they have agreed to disagree.

## Problem sets

Right answers are spread evenly across A, B and C (9 each among the 27 three-choice items). *Say why* items are spoken to a grown-up and never stored. Sprout items use no numbers.

### Set 1. Ice and snow (Sprout 1.1-1.5)

**1.1 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: What is the ice on the trough made of?
- A. Cold air, packed tight *(misconception: ice is frozen air)*
- B. Water that has frozen solid *(right)*
- C. Something new, not water *(misconception: ice-not-water)*
Hint: What is left when ice melts?
Feedback: A: Ice melts into water, not air. B: Yes. Ice is frozen water. C: Melt it and see. It turns back into water.
Explanation: Ice is water that froze solid. Warm it, and it's water again.

**1.2 (Sprout, 3-choice)**

## "I'm stuck" worked examples (step 2 of the ladder)

- **Ice is water:** A snowman melts in the sun. What is left? Start: look at the puddle.
- **Floating:** A big log floats. A small pebble sinks. Does big mean sink? Start: look at the log.
- **Hidden iceberg:** A cube in a glass. Look from the side. Start: find the water line.
- **Penguins:** An ostrich can't fly, but it's a bird. What about a penguin? Start: look for feathers.
- **Huddle:** Three friends share one blanket. Who is warmest? Start: the one in the middle.
- **Tundra:** A plant pot with ice at the bottom. Can roots go deep? Start: feel where the ice is.
- **Slanted light:** A flashlight straight down, then tilted. Which patch is brighter? Start: the small patch.
- **Desert:** A place gets 20 mm of rain a year. Desert or not? Start: is it under 250 mm?
- **Land or sea ice:** An ice cube floats in a glass. Another sits on a stone. Start: which one is already in the water?
- **Sinking water:** Salty water and fresh water, same cold. Which sinks? Start: salt makes water heavier.
- **Shine:** Out of 100, snow bounces back 90. How much does it take in? Start: 100 − 90 = 10.
- **Then and now:** A letter by ship, then a message by phone. What changed? Start: how long each takes.
- **Share of a share:** 10 apples, half are red, half of the red are big. How many big red? Start: half of 10 is 5.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Pole:** the very top (North Pole) or very bottom (South Pole) of the Earth.
- **Equator:** the imaginary line around Earth's middle.
- **Horizon:** the line where the land or sea seems to meet the sky.
- **Ice:** water frozen solid.
- **Crystal:** a solid with a regular shape, like a snow crystal with six arms.
- **Iceberg:** a huge chunk of ice from a glacier, floating in the sea.
- **Flipper:** a stiff, flat wing or limb used for swimming.
- **Huddle:** a tight group standing close to share warmth.
- **Tundra:** cold land with no trees, over frozen ground.
- **Permafrost:** ground that stays frozen for years.
- **Lichen:** a tough, low living thing that grows on rocks and soil.
- **Climate:** the usual weather of a place over many years.
- **Latitude:** how far north or south of the equator a place is, in degrees.
- **Desert:** a place with very little rain or snow, hot or cold.
- **Cryosphere:** all of Earth's frozen water.
- **Ice sheet:** a huge sheet of ice covering land, like Antarctica.
- **Glacier:** a slow river of ice on land, made from pressed snow.
- **Sea ice:** frozen sea water floating on the ocean.
- **Albedo:** how much sunlight a surface bounces back.
- **Nutrients:** the stuff living things need to grow.
- **Upwelling:** deep water rising toward the surface.
- **Phytoplankton:** tiny drifting living things that use sunlight, like plants.
- **Krill:** small shrimp-like animals that eat phytoplankton.
- **Ecosystem:** the living things in a place and how they connect.
- **Midge:** a tiny insect, like a small fly. Antarctica's midge has no wings.
- **Copepod:** a tiny sea animal, smaller than a grain of rice, that eats phytoplankton.
- **Sledge:** a sled for carrying loads over snow and ice.
- **Continent:** one of Earth's seven great areas of land, like Antarctica.
- **Thought experiment:** a "what if" you think through. It is not a forecast of what will happen.
- **Treaty:** a written agreement between countries.
- **Satellite:** a machine that circles Earth and watches or sends signals.
- **GPS:** a way to find exactly where you are, using satellite signals.
- **Icebreaker:** a strong ship that breaks a path through sea ice.
- **Ice core:** a long tube of ice drilled out to study old snow and air.
- **Indigenous peoples:** the first peoples of a place, who still live there.
- **Agreement:** a promise countries make together.
- **Protected area:** a place where rules limit fishing, mining or building.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** ice and snow as frozen water, floating ice and the hidden iceberg; penguins as swimming birds of the south, the emperor huddle and the father with the egg; the Arctic tundra and permafrost; latitude, slanted sunlight and the polar climate; the cryosphere and its place in the water cycle; cold, salty water sinking, albedo and upwelling; how the two polar ecosystems differ; then-and-now polar exploration; and, at the Keeper's Desk, the many voices in polar decisions.

**What it does not claim:** the benches are made-up models with stated rules. The tank, the thaw depths, the beam patches, the glasses and the shine numbers are simplified pictures. The 65 m figure is a thought experiment, not a forecast. The voice cards are invented people whose needs are drawn from public positions; nothing here tells a child what to think about a real dispute.

**Cold line:** The ice on this table stays on the screen. Off screen, a grown-up leads. Don't eat snow or ice from outside.
