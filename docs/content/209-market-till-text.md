# Market Till: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 209](../specs/209-market-till.md). Use as written, after independent spec review and a math check. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion and is never gendered; `{courier}` is the explorer's courier. Pip is he/him, Dot she/her, Bramble she/her (with her magpie), the baker he/him, the orchard keeper she/her. Ember the pocket-dragon follows spec 120's Act II text (her birthday). Real-world facts are in the [source audit](../source-audits/209-market-till.md); items still marked `[check]` stay off screen until cleared. Nothing here is scored or timed. All money is pretend: no real prices, accounts or records.

## Labels used on every card

- **A story from Willowbank**: the town, the café, its prices and every character are made up.
- **A true thing about math**: checked math, explained simply.
- **A true thing from history**: something people really did, made or wrote.
- **A made-up model**: a simple game rule, shown on the card, that is not a real shop or real money.

## Money words on screen

Amounts are written one of three ways, and all three mean the same money:

- **$3.05** (dollar sign, a point, then two places for cents)
- **305¢** (all in cents)
- **3 dollars and 5 cents** (in words)

Read aloud as "three dollars and five cents". Never "three point five". Under one dollar, the till shows **75¢** or **$0.75**. The dollar sign and the cent sign never appear together on a correct tag. A tag such as "$0.75¢" appears only as a sign to fix, always labeled **Fix this tag**.

## Teach cards

Each card is under 30 words. Worked steps appear one tap at a time.

**T1. Cents and dollars** (A true thing about math)
> 100 cents make 1 dollar. Coins are cents: 1¢, 5¢, 10¢, 25¢. A $1 bill is worth the same as 100 pennies.
Worked example: 1. Four quarters: 25¢ + 25¢ + 25¢ + 25¢. 2. That is 100¢. 3. 100¢ is $1.

**T2. Reading a price** (A true thing about math)
> In $3.05, the 3 is dollars. The two places after the point are cents. Say it: three dollars and five cents.
Worked example: 1. $3.05: 3 dollars, 5 cents. 2. $3.50: 3 dollars, 50 cents. 3. Different money.

**T3. Two ways to write it** (A true thing about math)
> 305¢ and $3.05 are the same money. Use the dollar sign or the cent sign, never both.
Worked example: 1. 75¢ is less than a dollar. 2. Write 75¢ or $0.75. 3. One sign at a time.

**T4. Add up a bill** (A true thing about math)
> Add the cents. Add the dollars. If the cents reach 100 or more, trade 100 cents for one dollar.
Worked example: 1. $2.65 + $1.85. 2. Cents: 65 + 85 = 150¢, which is $1.50. 3. Dollars: 2 + 1 = 3. 4. Total: $4.50.

**T5. How much more?** (A true thing about math)
> Count up from what you have to the price. Jump to the next ten cents, then the next dollar.
Worked example: 1. Have $2.40. Soup: $3.05. 2. $2.40 to $2.50: 10¢. 3. To $3.00: 50¢. 4. To $3.05: 5¢. 5. 65¢ more.

**T6. Change by counting up** (A true thing about math)
> Change is the gap from the price up to what was paid. Count up in jumps, then add the jumps.
Worked example: 1. Soup $3.05, paid $5. 2. To $3.10: 5¢. 3. To $4.00: 90¢. 4. To $5.00: $1. 5. Change: $1.95.

**T7. Change by taking away** (A true thing about math)
> You can also subtract: paid minus price. Change both to cents first. Check by adding the change back on.
Worked example: 1. $5.00 − $3.05. 2. 500¢ − 305¢ = 195¢. 3. 195¢ is $1.95. 4. Check: $3.05 + $1.95 = $5.00.

**T8. A bill, then change** (A true thing about math)
> With several things, add the bill first. Then find the change from that total.
Worked example: 1. Honey cake $1.75 + cocoa $1.40 = $3.15. 2. Paid $10. 3. $3.15 to $3.20 to $4.00 to $10.00. 4. Change: $6.85.

**T9. Round to the nearest dollar** (A true thing about math)
> Look at the cents. 50¢ or more: round up. Less than 50¢: round down.
Worked example: 1. $2.65: 65¢ is 50 or more, so $3. 2. $1.40: 40¢ is less, so $1. 3. $1.50 rounds up to $2.

**T10. Will it be enough?** (A true thing about math)
> Estimate before you work it out. If the estimate is far below your money, yes. If it's close, work it out exactly.
Worked example: 1. $2.65, $1.85, $2.35, $1.75. 2. About 3 + 2 + 2 + 2 = $9. 3. Close to $10, so check: $8.60. 4. Enough.

**T11. Which costs more?** (A true thing about math)
> Compare the dollars first. If the dollars match, compare the cents.
Worked example: 1. $2.35, $3.05, $2.65. 2. $3.05 has the most dollars. 3. $2.35 and $2.65: 35¢ is less. 4. Order: $2.35, $2.65, $3.05.

**T12. The price for one** (A true thing about math)
> To compare packs, find the price for one. Share the price by how many are in the pack.
Worked example: 1. 4 muffins for $6.00: $1.50 each. 2. 6 muffins for $8.40: $1.40 each. 3. The six-pack costs less each.

**T13. Best buy for you** (A true thing about math)
> Cheaper for each is not always best. Buy what you need. Fewer muffins can cost less in all.
Worked example: 1. You need 4 muffins. 2. Four-pack: $6.00. 3. Six-pack: $8.40. 4. The four-pack costs less to pay.

**T14. Two steps: name each number** (A true thing about math)
> In a two-step problem, name each number. First find the total cost. Then find the change. Check it makes sense.
Worked example: 1. 2 cocoa at $1.40 and a muffin at $1.20. 2. Total cost: $4.00. 3. Paid $5. Change: $1.00. 4. Check: $4.00 + $1.00 = $5.00.

**T15. Draw the money** (A true thing about math)
> A bar can show it. The whole bar is what was paid. The parts are the costs and the change.
Worked example: 1. Whole bar: $5. 2. One part: $4.00 cost. 3. Missing part: change. 4. $5 − $4 = $1.

## Real-world and history cards

**H1. A law makes dollars and cents** (A true thing from history; Seedling and up)
> In 1792, a United States law made the dollar the main unit of money. It split one dollar into 100 cents.
Keeper's note:
> The Coinage Act of 1792 also set up the United States Mint in Philadelphia. It named a tenth of a dollar a "disme" (we say dime) and a hundredth a cent. Counting money in tens and hundreds made adding prices easier.

**H2. Paper money in China** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> About a thousand years ago in Sichuan, China, coins were iron and heavy. Merchants gave paper notes for coins left with them.
Keeper's note:
> In the 1020s, the Song government took over making these notes, called jiaozi. Many historians call them the first paper money made by a government. Our pretend bills follow the same idea: paper that stands for an amount.

**H3. Very old coins** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> More than 2,600 years ago in Lydia, now part of Turkey, people made some of the first coins. Many show a lion's head.
Keeper's note:
> They were made of electrum, a natural mix of gold and silver. Coins made trading easier because people could count them instead of weighing metal each time.

- C. No. Four things cost too much. *(tests: counting items, not money)*
Hint: Is the estimate close to $10, or far below?
Explanation: An estimate far below your money is enough to say yes. Exact: $3.80.
Feedback A: Yes. Far below means enough. No exact sum needed.
Feedback B: Here the estimate is far below $10. That already tells you.
Feedback C: Count the money, not the things. About $3 is small.

**D3 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Five sandwiches at $2.40. Estimate: about $10. Ember has $10. Now what?
- A. Say yes. The estimate is $10. *(tests: trusting a close estimate)*
- B. Work it out exactly. It's close, and each price rounded down. *(right)*
- C. Say no. Five is too many things. *(tests: counting items, not money)*
Hint: Did $2.40 round up or down?
Explanation: Each sandwich lost 40¢ in rounding. Five lots of $2.40 make $12. Not enough.
Feedback A: Every price rounded down, so the real total is more. Check.
Feedback B: Yes. Exact is $12, so $10 is not enough.
Feedback C: The number of things doesn't decide. Their total does.

**D4 (Sapling, number, estimate then exact)**
Prompt: Loaf, jam, pie, honey cake. Estimate, then exact. Is $10 enough?
Answer: estimate about $9; exact $8.60; yes, with $1.40 left.
Worked steps: 1. Round: $3 + $2 + $2 + $2 = $9. 2. Close to $10, so work it out. 3. Cents: 65 + 85 + 35 + 75 = 260¢ = $2.60. 4. Dollars: 2 + 1 + 2 + 1 = 6. 5. $8.60.
Tests: $6.60 (forgetting both traded dollars); $7.60 (trading only one dollar).
Hint: Keep your estimate first. Then add the cents.
Explanation: The estimate was close, so the exact total settled it.

**D5 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: 95¢, $1.90, $1.85. Rounded up: $1 + $2 + $2 = $5. Is $5 surely enough?
- A. Yes. Rounding up makes the estimate at least the real total. *(right)*
- B. Not sure. An estimate can never decide. *(tests: withholding when rounding up already decides)*
- C. No. It uses all $5, so the real total is more. *(tests: thinking rounding up adds too little)*
Hint: Rounding up: is the real total bigger or smaller?
Explanation: Each real price is at most its rounded-up price. Real total: $4.70.
Feedback A: Yes. The real total can't be more than $5.
Feedback B: Rounding up is a safe estimate. If it fits, the real one fits.
Feedback C: Rounding up made every price bigger. The real total is smaller.

**D6 (Keeper's Desk, say why; spoken to a grown-up, not stored)**
Prompt: Pip rounded and got $10. "So $10 is enough." When might that not be true?
Model answer for the grown-up: If most prices rounded down, the real total can be more than $10. When the estimate is close, work it out exactly or round up to be safe.
Tests: an estimate is always exact enough; rounding never changes the answer.
Hint: Think about prices like $2.40. Up or down?
Explanation: A close estimate needs an exact check.

### Set E. Comparing prices and best buys (Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**E1 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Put these in order, cheapest first: $2.65, $3.05, $2.35.
- A. $3.05, $2.35, $2.65 *(tests: comparing the cents only)*
- B. $3.05, $2.65, $2.35 *(tests: ordering most expensive first)*
- C. $2.35, $2.65, $3.05 *(right)*
Hint: Compare the dollars first.
Explanation: $3.05 has the most dollars. Then 35¢ is less than 65¢.
Feedback A: Dollars count first. $3.05 is the most money.
Feedback B: That's the most expensive first. Which costs least?
Feedback C: Yes. Dollars first, then cents.

**E2 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: Muffins: 4 for $6.00. What's the price for one?
Answer: $1.50.
Worked steps: 1. Share $6.00 into 4 equal parts. 2. $6.00 ÷ 4 = $1.50. 3. Check: 4 × $1.50 = $6.00.
Tests: $24.00 (multiplying instead of sharing).
Hint: Share the price between the muffins.
Explanation: The price for one lets you compare packs of different sizes.

**E3 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: 4 muffins for $6.00, or 6 for $8.40. Which costs less each?
- A. The four-pack. Its price is smaller. *(tests: smaller total means cheaper each)*
- B. The six-pack: $1.40 each. *(right)*
- C. Can't tell. The packs hold different numbers. *(tests: withholding when the price for one decides)*
Hint: Find the price for one muffin in each pack.
Explanation: $6.00 ÷ 4 = $1.50. $8.40 ÷ 6 = $1.40. The six-pack costs less each.
Feedback A: A smaller price can buy fewer muffins. Find the price for one.
Feedback B: Yes. $1.40 is less than $1.50.
Feedback C: You can tell. The price for one makes them fair to compare.

**E4 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: You need just 4 muffins. Which should you buy?
- A. The four-pack. $6.00 is less to pay. *(right)*
- B. The six-pack. Cheaper each is always best. *(tests: price for one always decides)*
- C. Two four-packs, to be safe. *(tests: buying more than needed)*
Hint: How much would you pay in all for each choice?
Explanation: The best buy depends on what you need. Four muffins cost $6.00, not $8.40.
Feedback A: Yes. You pay less and get what you need.
Feedback B: Cheaper each helps only if you'd use all six.
Feedback C: Two packs cost $12.00 and give 8 muffins. You need 4.

**E5 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: Jam: 200 g for $1.80, or 500 g for $4.00. Price per 100 g?
Answer: small jar 90¢; big jar 80¢. The big jar costs less for each 100 g.
Worked steps: 1. Small: 200 g is 2 lots of 100 g. $1.80 ÷ 2 = 90¢. 2. Big: 500 g is 5 lots. $4.00 ÷ 5 = 80¢. 3. 80¢ is less.
Tests: $20.00 (multiplying); 8¢ (a slip in the cents place).
Hint: How many lots of 100 g in each jar?
Explanation: Comparing the same amount, 100 g, makes the jars fair to compare.

### Set F. Two steps, and how we know (Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**F1 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: 2 cocoa and a muffin come to $4.00. Bramble paid $5. What's $4.00?
- A. The change. *(tests: naming a different quantity)*
- B. The money Bramble paid. *(tests: muddling paid and cost)*
- C. The total cost. It's step one. *(right)*
Hint: What did the $4.00 add up?
Explanation: $4.00 is what the food costs. The change is still to find.
Feedback A: Change is what comes back. $4.00 is what she spent.
Feedback B: She paid $5. The $4.00 is something else.
Feedback C: Yes. Now find the change from $5.

**F2 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: The total cost is $4.00. Bramble paid $5. What's her change?
Answer: $1.00.
Worked steps: 1. Step one was the total cost: $4.00. 2. Step two: $5.00 − $4.00 = $1.00. 3. Check: $4.00 + $1.00 = $5.00.
Tests: $4.00 (stopping after step one); $9.00 (adding).
Hint: What does the question still want?
Explanation: The change is less than what she paid, so it makes sense.

**F3 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: The baker buys 3 loaves at $2.65 and jam at $1.85. Pays $10. Change?
Answer: 20¢.
Worked steps: 1. 3 loaves: $2.65 + $2.65 + $2.65 = $7.95. Name it: loaves' cost. 2. Add the jam: $7.95 + $1.85 = $9.80. Name it: total cost. 3. Count up $9.80 to $10.00: 20¢. Name it: change.
Tests: $5.50 (counting only one loaf); $9.80 (giving the total cost as the change).
Hint: Three steps. Name each number as you go.
Explanation: Small change makes sense: the bill was nearly $10.

**F4 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Ember has $6. A pie costs $2.35. Which bar shows what's left?
- A. A bar of $6 and a bar of $2.35 joined end to end. *(tests: adding instead of finding the part left)*
- B. One pie out of one pie, so nothing left. *(tests: counting things, not money)*
- C. A $6 bar split into $2.35 and a missing part. *(right)*
Hint: Which bar is the whole?
Explanation: $6 is the whole. Take the pie part away: $6 − $2.35 = $3.65.
Feedback A: Joining makes $8.35. She can't have more after buying.
Feedback B: Count the money, not the pies. Some money is left.
Feedback C: Yes. The missing part is $3.65.

## "I'm stuck" worked examples

Step 2 of the ladder: an easier case, then how to start this one.

- **Tag items:** "50¢ and 25¢ make 75¢. Write it 75¢ or $0.75. Start: how many dollars, how many cents?"
- **Bill items:** "A roll $1.20 and a pear 90¢: cents 20 + 90 = 110¢, that's $1.10, so $2.10. Start: add the cents."
- **How-much-more items:** "Pip has 70¢. A pear is $1. 70¢ to 80¢, then to $1: 30¢. Start: jump to the next ten."
- **Change items:** "Paid $1 for 70¢. Count up 70¢, 80¢, $1.00: 30¢ change. Start: jump to the next ten cents."
- **Rounding items:** "$1.80 is nearer $2. $1.30 is nearer $1. Start: are the cents 50 or more?"
- **Enough items:** "Two things, about $1 each, and you have $10. Far below, so yes. Start: round each price."
- **Best-buy items:** "3 for $6 is $2 each. 4 for $10 is $2.50 each. Start: share the price."
- **Two-step items:** "A $2 roll and a 50¢ jam cost $2.50. Paid $3: 50¢ change. Start: find the total cost."

## Key terms (glossary)

- **dollar ($)**: the main unit of money here. One dollar is 100 cents.
- **cent (¢)**: one hundredth of a dollar.
- **price**: how much something costs.
- **bill (money)**: paper money, like a $5 bill.
- **bill (at the café)**: the list of what you bought, with the total.
- **total**: all the prices added together.
- **change**: the money that comes back when you pay more than the price.
- **count up**: find a gap by jumping from the smaller amount to the bigger one.
- **trade**: swap 100 cents for one dollar, or one dollar for 100 cents.
- **round**: change a price to a nearby easy amount, like the nearest dollar.
- **estimate**: a sensible "about" answer, made before working it out.
- **price for one**: the cost of one thing in a pack.
- **best buy**: the choice that gives you what you need for the least money.
- **bar model**: a bar that shows a whole and its parts.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** reading and writing dollars and cents ($3.05 is three dollars and five cents); adding prices on a bill, trading 100 cents for a dollar; finding change by counting up and by subtracting, then checking; estimating a total by rounding to the nearest dollar before deciding "is it enough?"; comparing prices and simple price-for-one best buys; naming each number in a two-step money problem.

**What it does not claim:** these are pretend prices, not real shop prices or advice about spending. There is no tax, tip, sales or real payment. Coin and bill drawings are original pictures, not copies of real money. It does not teach percentages or discounts (see the percentages pack) or decimals beyond money (see the decimals pack). Families make real money choices for many reasons; this activity does not judge them.
