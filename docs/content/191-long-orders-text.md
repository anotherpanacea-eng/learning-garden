# Long Orders: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 191](../specs/191-long-orders.md). Use as written, after independent spec review and a math check. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion and is never gendered. Pip is he/him, Dot she/her, Bramble she/her, Nuria she/her, the orchard keeper she/her, the librarian they/them. The old Keeper is never named or mentioned here (spec 120 naming rule). *La Garza* is the barge from draft spec 117; its captain is called "the captain" here so this pack stands alone. Real-world facts are in the [source audit](../source-audits/191-long-orders.md); items still marked `[check]` stay off screen until cleared. Nothing here is scored or timed.

## Labels used on every card

- **A story from Willowbank**: the towns, the barge, the orders and every character are made up.
- **A true thing about math**: checked math, explained simply.
- **A true thing from history**: something people really did or wrote.
- **A made-up model**: a simple game rule, shown on the card, that is not a real shop or real cargo.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. The longest order card (Act III, chapter 1: the floating market)

**Hook:** *La Garza* ties up with an order card from Nuria. Pip wants to count every jar by hand.

**Short:**
> Nuria's card says: 36 crates, 24 jars in each. Pip starts counting jars one by one. Dot finds a faster way.

**Longer:**
> *La Garza* bumps against the landing. The captain hands Dot a card in Nuria's tidy writing: *36 crates of honey, 24 jars in each. Please check.*
>
> Pip opens the first crate and starts counting. "One jar. Two jars. Three jars."
>
> "That's 864 jars," says Dot, writing on the chalk wall.
>
> Pip stops at eleven. "How do you know?"
>
> Bramble leans in. "She guessed. Twenty-four and thirty-six. That's about sixty jars."
>
> "Sixty jars would fit in three crates," says {guide}. "Look how many crates there are."
>
> Bramble looks at the deck. It is very full of crates. "Fine. Not sixty."
>
> Dot draws a big rectangle and cuts it into four parts. "Twenty and four, down the side. Thirty and six, along the top. Each part is easy."
>
> Pip puts the jar back. "I'll check one crate. You check the rest."

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> 24 × 36 is 600 + 120 + 120 + 24 = 864. A quick estimate, 20 × 40 = 800, says the answer is in the hundreds. Bramble added the numbers. Adding gives 60, which can't be right for 36 full crates.

### Scene 2. Three times, or three more? (Act III, chapter 2: the seed exchange)

**Hook:** Vadoalto wants apple seedlings. The orchard keeper hears "three more" when Nuria said "three times as many".

**Short:**
> "Last year you sent 7 bundles," says Nuria. "We need three times as many." The orchard keeper packs 10. Nuria smiles and waits.

**Longer:**
> Nuria comes down on *La Garza* with a bundle of reeds taller than she is. "Our orchard was flattened in the storm," she says. "Last year you sent us 7 bundles of seedlings. This year we need three times as many."
>
> The orchard keeper nods and packs seven bundles, then three more. "Ten. There you are."
>
> Nuria counts them twice. "That's three more. I asked for three times as many."
>
> "Isn't that the same?"
>
> {guide} draws a bar on the chalk wall: seven little squares. Then the same bar again, and again, three bars in a row. "Three times as many is three whole copies of last year."
>
> The orchard keeper counts the squares. "Twenty-one bundles." She looks at her seedling rows. "Good thing I planted extra."
>
> Pip wants to know if he can have three times as much lunch.

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> "Three more" adds a fixed amount: 7 + 3 = 10. "Three times as many" copies the whole amount: 3 × 7 = 21. Same numbers, different question. Read which one is asked before you calculate.

**Longer:**

>

>
> Pip starts drawing chairs. He gets bored at row 2.
>
> "Draw one big rectangle," says {guide}. "Twenty and three down. Ten and five across."
>

## Teach cards

Each card is under 30 words. Worked steps appear one tap at a time.

**T1. Times a ten** (A true thing about math)
> 30 is 3 tens. So 4 × 30 is 4 lots of 3 tens: 12 tens. Twelve tens is 120.
Worked example: 1. Known fact: 4 × 3 = 12. 2. Now each 3 is 3 tens. 3. 12 tens = 120.

**T2. Ten times bigger: the digits move** (A true thing about math)
> Times 10, every digit moves one place left. Times 100, two places. Dividing moves them right. The decimal point stays still.
Worked example: 1. 2.5 × 10 = 25. 2. 2.5 × 100 = 250. 3. 3,600 ÷ 1,000 = 3.6.

**T3. Times zero, times one, three numbers** (A true thing about math)
> Six empty crates hold 0 jars. One crate of 35 holds 35. With three numbers, multiply the easy pair first.
Worked example: 1. 2 × 9 × 5. 2. Easy pair: 2 × 5 = 10. 3. 10 × 9 = 90.

**T4. Double and halve, or split** (A true thing about math)
> Halve one number, double the other: the answer stays the same. Or split a number into tens and ones, and multiply both parts.
Worked example: 1. 14 × 5 = 7 × 10 = 70. 2. 6 × 14 = 6 × 10 + 6 × 4. 3. 60 + 24 = 84.

**T5. Round, then fix it** (A true thing about math)
> Use a friendly number, then fix the difference. Known facts help with tens and dividing too.
Worked example: 1. 29 × 4 = 30 × 4, take away one 4. 2. 120 − 4 = 116. 3. 240 ÷ 6 = 40, because 24 ÷ 6 = 4.

**T6. Swap, group, split** (A true thing about math)
> In multiplying, you can swap the order, group any pair first, or split a number. Dividing can't be swapped.
Worked example: 1. 6 × 4 = 4 × 6 = 24. 2. 8 × 7 = 8 × 5 + 8 × 2 = 56. 3. 24 ÷ 4 = 6, but 4 ÷ 24 is less than 1.

**T7. Times as many, or more?** (A true thing about math)
> "3 more" adds 3. "3 times as many" makes 3 copies. Draw bars, then write a sentence with a box for the unknown.
Worked example: 1. Pip has 4 sacks. 2. 3 more: 4 + 3 = 7. 3. 3 times as many: 3 × 4 = 12.

**T8. The area model** (A true thing about math)
> Draw a rectangle. Split each side into tens and ones. Multiply each part. Add the parts.
Worked example: 1. 23 × 15: sides 20 + 3 and 10 + 5. 2. Parts: 200, 30, 100, 15. 3. 200 + 30 + 100 + 15 = 345.

**T9. Long multiplication** (A true thing about math)
> Row 1 multiplies by the ones. Row 2 multiplies by the tens, so it starts with a zero. Add the rows.
Worked example: 1. 23 × 15. 2. Row 1: 23 × 5 = 115. 3. Row 2: 23 × 10 = 230. 4. 115 + 230 = 345.

**T10. Decimals times a whole number** (A true thing about math)
> Multiply each part, ones and tenths. Or think in tenths, then move back. Estimate to check the point.
Worked example: 1. 1.6 × 4 = 1 × 4 + 0.6 × 4. 2. 4 + 2.4 = 6.4. 3. Check: about 2 × 4 = 8. 6.4 fits.

**T11. Short division** (A true thing about math)
> Share the tens first. Left-over tens become ones. Then share the ones.
Worked example: 1. 96 ÷ 4. 2. 9 tens ÷ 4 = 2 tens, 1 ten left. 3. 1 ten and 6 ones make 16. 16 ÷ 4 = 4. 4. Answer: 24.

**T12. Long division in chunks** (A true thing about math)
> Take away big chunks of the divisor. A list of its multiples helps. Add up the chunks. Multiply back to check.
Worked example: 1. 384 ÷ 16. 2. 16 × 20 = 320. 384 − 320 = 64. 3. 16 × 4 = 64. 4. 20 + 4 = 24. Check: 24 × 16 = 384.

**T13. What does the leftover mean?** (A true thing about math)
> 75 ÷ 12 = 6, remainder 3. The story decides: one more cart, only full crates, or share the rest.
Worked example: 1. Every sack must go: 7 carts. 2. Full crates only: 6. 3. Share 75 kg among 12: 6.25 kg each.

**T14. About right?** (A true thing about math)
> Round to friendly numbers before you calculate. If the answer is far from the estimate, look again.
Worked example: 1. 52 × 19. 2. About 50 × 20 = 1,000. 3. Exact: 988. 4. Close to 1,000. It fits.

**T15. Round the answer for the job** (A true thing about math)
> Some answers need rounding: money to cents, lengths to a tenth. Round once, at the end.
Worked example: 1. 47 ÷ 6 = 7.833... 2. To one decimal place: 7.8. 3. $10 ÷ 3 = $3.333... 4. To the cent: $3.33.

## Real-world and history cards

**H1. Doubling on a papyrus** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> About 3,500 years ago in Egypt, a scribe named Ahmes copied a math scroll. It multiplies by doubling, again and again, then adding the right rows.
Keeper's note:
> The Rhind papyrus is in the British Museum in London. Ahmes says he copied it from an older scroll. To find 13 × 24, double 24 to get 48, 96 and 192. 13 is 1 + 4 + 8, so add 24 + 96 + 192 = 312.

**H2. The lattice** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> Over 700 years ago in Marrakesh, a mathematician named Ibn al-Banna wrote about a grid for multiplying. Each box is cut in half by a slanted line.
Keeper's note:
> This grid is called a lattice. In Italy it was later called *gelosia*, a word for a window screen. Each box holds one small product, tens above the line and ones below. You add along the slants. It does the same work as the area model.

**H3. Napier's bones** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> In 1617, in Scotland, John Napier printed a book about rods with times tables on them. Put rods side by side, and a long multiplication becomes adding.
Keeper's note:
> People called the rods "Napier's bones". For 46 × 7, set the 4 rod and the 6 rod together and read row 7: 2 and 8, then 4 and 2. Add along the slants: 2 ones; 8 + 4 = 12 tens, carry 1; 2 + 1 = 3 hundreds. So 46 × 7 = 322.

## Problem sets

### Set A. Tens and moving digits (Sprout to Keeper's Desk)

**A1 (Sprout, 3-choice, picture)**
Prompt: Pip tips every crate's jars onto the dock. More jars, fewer, or the same?
- A. More. They take up more room now. *(tests: spread out means more)*
- B. Fewer. The crates made them look bigger. *(tests: grouping changes the amount)*
- C. The same. Nothing was added or taken away. *(right)*
Hint: Did any jar go away, or come?
Explanation: Grouping into crates changes how you count. It never changes how many.
Feedback A: Spread out looks bigger. Did any jars arrive?
Feedback B: The crates held them. Did any jar disappear?
Feedback C: Yes. Same jars, just not in crates.

**A2 (Sprout, 3-choice, picture)**
Prompt: Every crate holds the same. What is a fast way to count jars?
- A. Count one crate. Then jump that much for each crate. *(right)*
- B. Tip all the jars out. Count them one by one. *(tests: counting ones only)*
- C. Count the crates. That's how many jars. *(tests: groups confused with things)*
Hint: Every crate is the same. Do you need to count each one?
Explanation: Equal groups let you jump by the group size.
Feedback A: Yes. Count one group, then jump.
Feedback B: That works, but slowly. The crates are all the same.
Feedback C: Each crate holds many jars. Count inside one first.

**A3 (Seedling, number)**

**E3 (Seedling, number)**

Answer: 21 full boxes, 3 jars left.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **multiple of ten**: a number made of whole tens, like 30 or 400.
- **place value**: what a digit is worth because of where it sits.
- **digit**: one of the signs 0 to 9 we use to write numbers.
- **decimal point**: the dot between the ones and the tenths.
- **placeholder zero**: a 0 that holds an empty place.
- **partition**: split a number into parts, like 15 = 10 + 5.
- **compensation**: use a friendlier number, then fix the difference.
- **commutative**: you can swap the order and get the same answer.
- **associative**: you can group any pair first and get the same answer.
- **distributive**: split a number, multiply each part, then add.
- **times as many**: copies of an amount.
- **area model**: a rectangle split into parts to show a multiplication.
- **partial product**: the answer to one part of a multiplication.
- **long multiplication**: a written method with one row for each digit.
- **short division**: sharing digit by digit, carrying leftovers along.
- **long division**: dividing in chunks or steps, writing each one down.
- **divisor**: the number you divide by.
- **quotient**: the answer to a division.
- **remainder**: what is left after equal groups are made.
- **estimate**: a quick, close answer from friendly numbers.
- **degree of accuracy**: how exact an answer needs to be.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** multiplying by multiples of ten and by 10, 100 and 1,000 with decimals; mental strategies (halving and doubling, splitting, round-and-fix, ×0 and ×1, three factors); the commutative, associative and distributive properties used as tools; "times as many" against "more than"; the area model, then long multiplication; decimals times whole numbers; short and long division by 1- and 2-digit numbers; what a remainder means in a story; estimating and rounding to check.

**What it does not claim:** these are made-up orders and prices. It does not teach multiplying two decimals together, dividing by a decimal, or times-table recall; a multiples list is always available. A screen can rehearse the steps; your child's own written working on paper is still the best evidence.
