# The Meadow Comes Back: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 151](../specs/151-meadow-comes-back.md). Use as written, after independent spec review and a science check. Sources and open checks are in [the source audit](../source-audits/151-meadow-comes-back.md). `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion. Hob Pennick (he/him) farms by the ford; Bramble (she/her) is a girl witch with a magpie; Moss has no pronouns in this text. All `[check]` items were cleared in the 2026-10-25 fact-check pass. Nothing here is scored.

## Labels (used everywhere)

- **A true thing from science**: real biology, checked against the sources.
- **A story from Willowbank**: the town, its people, the meadow and its survey.
- **A made-up model**: invented numbers or a simplified picture. Always shown with the line below.
- **Model line (on every model page):** These are made-up numbers in a simple picture. Real meadows are messier.
- **Food arrow legend (on every web page):** Arrow: food → eater. Food supplies energy to the eater. Read it as "is eaten by."
- **Loop arrow legend (on the loop page):** Round arrow: where the stuff goes next.

## Arriving

**Place title:** The Water-Meadow

**Hook (30 words max):**
> Hob's meadow by the ford is a flat sheet of gray mud. The old willow lies across it. "Give it time," says Hob. "And keep a record."

**First visit:**
> Hob Pennick leans on his gate. His water-meadow, the flat field by the ford, is covered in drying mud. The big willow has fallen across the middle.
>
> "Every spring the brook comes up a little," says Hob. "This storm came up a lot."
>
> {guide} crouches by the mud. "Is anything still alive under there?"
>
> "That's the question," says Hob. He hands you a square frame made of four sticks, with string across it. "Put this down in the same spot each time you come. Write what you find. Then we'll know."

**Later visits:** The survey square is waiting by Hob's gate. The meadow has changed since you were last here.

## The story scenes

### Scene 1. Under the mud (Act I, spring)

**Short (30 words max):**
> The meadow is mud. You lay the survey square down. Two green stubs poke through. "Two kinds of plant," says {guide}. "That's not nothing."

**Longer (150 words max):**
> The mud has dried into flat gray plates. Pip steps on one and it crunches like a cracker. He looks hopeful, then disappointed.
>
> You lay Hob's survey square on the mud by the gatepost, the same place every time. String divides it into a hundred small squares.
>
> Most of the small squares are bare. In a few, green stubs of grass are pushing through. In one corner there's a clover leaf.
>
> "Two kinds," says {guide}, writing it down. "And eighty-five bare squares."
>
> "The meadow plants didn't all die?" asks Pip.
>
> "Some did," says Hob. "But the roots of others are still down there. And the mud is full of seeds. Some washed in with the flood." He taps the fallen willow with his boot. "Even this old thing isn't finished."
>
> You look closer. On the fallen trunk, tiny green buds are swelling.

### Scene 2. The first green (Act II, early summer)

**Short (30 words max):**
> Green is back, thin and fast. Fuzzy willow seedlings stand in the wet mud. "First to arrive," says Moss. "They won't be the last."

**Longer (150 words max):**
> By early summer the meadow looks like a badly mown lawn. Some patches are thick and green. Others are still bare.
>
> You count six kinds of plant in the survey square. Some are fast weeds Hob doesn't bother to name. In the wettest corner stand tiny willow seedlings, no taller than Dot's hat.
>
> "Willow seeds are fluffy and blow everywhere," says Moss, who has come to look. "But they only last a short time. They need bare, wet mud, straight away. The flood made exactly that."
>
> "So the flood helped them?" says {guide}.
>
> "It helped some," says Moss. "The first ones in are called pioneers. They grow fast on bare ground. Later, slower plants will crowd them out."
>
> Bramble's magpie lands on the gate, inspects the survey square, and leaves unimpressed.
>
> Forty bare squares now. You write it down.

**Keeper's note (80 words max):**
> Willow seed shed in early summer stays alive only days to a few weeks, and needs moist, bare ground straight away. On river floodplains, willow seedlings grow in bands where the ground was wet and open at the right moment. A flood that leaves bare mud can start a new willow thicket.

### Scene 3. Bramble and the fallen willow (Act II, autumn)

**Short (30 words max):**
> Bramble wants the fallen willow cleared away. "It's dead, and it blocks the path." She's half right. You lift a piece of bark and look underneath.

**Longer (150 words max):**
> Bramble arrives with a saw almost as tall as she is.
>
> "That willow is dead," she announces. "And the path to the ford goes right through it. Let's clear it and have a bonfire."
>
> "Part of it isn't dead," says {guide}, pointing. Along the top of the trunk, a row of new shoots has grown into straight green whips.
>
> Hob lifts a loose slab of bark from the broken end. Underneath, the wood is soft and damp. Gray woodlice scatter. White threads of fungus run through the wood like string. A beetle grub curls up, offended.
>
> "That end is dead," says Hob. "But dead isn't empty."
>
> Bramble looks at the path. "It still blocks the way."
>
> "It does," says Hob. "You're right about that."
>
> In the end they saw off one branch and drag it to the meadow edge, out of the path. The rest stays where it fell.

**Keeper's note (80 words max):**
> Willows grow new shoots strongly after being cut or broken, and their branches root easily in damp ground. So a fallen willow can stay alive and sprout. Dead wood matters too: fungi feed on it, beetle grubs grow in it, woodlice eat the rotting parts. Conservation groups advise leaving fallen wood where it falls, when safe.

### Scene 4. Seeds from upstream (Act III, the next summer)

**Short (30 words max):**
> Tall pink flowers are spreading along the bank. Nuria writes: Vadoalto has them too. Their seeds travel down the brook. "Then we'll pull them together," says {guide}.

**Longer (150 words max):**
> By the second summer, the survey square has fourteen kinds of plant, and only five bare squares. But along the brook bank, something new is growing fast: tall stems with pink flowers shaped like little hats.
>
> Dot admires the hats. Hob does not.
>
> "Himalayan balsam," he says. "Pretty. Not from here. It grows so thick that smaller plants get little light. And in winter it dies right back and leaves the bank bare for the next flood to wash away."
>
> The courier brings a letter from Nuria in Vadoalto. Their banks have balsam too. "The seed pods pop," she writes, "and the brook carries the seeds down to you. Next summer we will pull ours before the pods ripen. Will you pull yours?"
>
> "If they start upstream and we start here," says {guide}, "fewer seeds reach Les Saules."
>
> You write it in the survey book: *pulled balsam, with Vadoalto.*

**Keeper's note (80 words max):**
> Himalayan balsam is a real invasive plant on many European riverbanks, the Pyrenees among them. Its pods burst and throw seeds, and rivers carry them downstream. River groups organize volunteers to pull it by hand in late spring and summer, before the pods ripen, often starting upstream. The plant dies back in winter, leaving bare soil that floods can erode. Willowbank and Vadoalto are a story; the balsam and the method are real.

### Scene 5. The survey book (Act V or VI, the summer after)

**Short (30 words max):**
> Eighteen kinds of plant now, and grasshoppers everywhere. Hob reads your survey book from the first page. "That's the meadow coming back," he says. "And you kept the record."

**Longer (150 words max):**
> The meadow is tall and buzzing. Grasshoppers spring out of your way. A kestrel hangs in the air over the far corner, perfectly still, then drops.
>
> In the survey square you count eighteen kinds of plant and only two bare squares. You turn back to the first page: two kinds, eighty-five bare.
>
> Moss runs a finger down the columns. "Pioneers first. Then grasses. Then the meadow flowers."
>
> "And if Hob stopped cutting the hay?" asks {guide}.
>
> "Then the willows would win," says Hob. "Scrub first, then a wet wood. Lovely, but not a meadow. Cutting the hay late, after the flowers drop their seed, keeps it a meadow."
>
> Wren Halloway asks if she can copy your survey into the town ledger.
>
> "It's a record," she says. "That's what keeps the ways open."

**Keeper's note (80 words max):**
> A meadow is not the "end" of succession. On many floodplains, if nobody mows or grazes, willow moves in, then alder and ash: a wet woodland. Regular hay cutting holds the meadow at the grassland stage. So the meadow's future is partly a human choice. One square is a small sample; careful surveys use several.

## Teach cards (30 words max each)

Each worked example reveals one step per tap.

**T1. Living, dead, never alive.** Living things grow, feed, breathe and make more of themselves. Dead things were once alive. Never-alive things were never alive at all.
- Worked example: *the willow log.* Step 1: Did it ever grow? Yes, it was a tree. Step 2: Is this piece still growing? No shoots, soft rotten wood. Step 3: So it is **dead**: once alive.

**T2. Moving is not the test.** The brook moves, but it is not alive. A seed lies still, but it is alive. Ask: does it grow, feed and make more?

**T3. The survey square.** Put the square in the same spot each visit. Count bare small squares. Count kinds of plant. Same way each time, so visits compare fairly.
- Worked example: *visit 1.* Step 1: 85 small squares are bare. Step 2: The square has 100. Step 3: So 15 small squares have plants.

**T4. Food arrows.** An arrow points from the food to the eater. Grass → vole means the vole eats the grass. Say it as "is eaten by."
- Worked example: *grass → vole → barn owl.* Step 1: Find the start: grass. Step 2: Follow the arrow: grass is eaten by the vole. Step 3: The vole is eaten by the barn owl.

**T5. A web is many chains.** Most animals eat more than one thing. Many are eaten by more than one thing. Chains that share animals make a food web.
- Worked example: Step 1: grass → vole → barn owl. Step 2: grass → vole → kestrel. Step 3: Both share the vole: a web begins.

**T6. What if one goes?** Remove one living thing. Follow every arrow touching it, up and down. Say what **may** happen. Real outcomes can surprise us.
- Worked example: *remove the frogs.* Step 1: Who ate frogs? The grass snake. It may find less food. Step 2: Who did frogs eat? Grasshoppers and woodlice. There may be more of them.

**T7. Decomposers close the loop.** Fungi, bacteria, woodlice and worms break down dead things. What was in them goes back into the soil. New plants use it again.
- Worked example: *a fallen leaf.* Step 1: A woodlouse eats it. Step 2: Fungi and bacteria break down the rest and the droppings. Step 3: The stuff returns to the soil for grass.

**T8. Pioneers come first.** After a flood, fast plants grow on bare ground first. Slower plants crowd them out later. This change over time is called succession.
- Worked example: Step 1: Bare mud. Step 2: Fast weeds and willow seedlings. Step 3: Grasses and meadow flowers.

**T9. Matter loops, energy flows (older).** The stuff of living things goes round and round. Energy comes in as sunlight and leaves as heat. It does not go round.
- Worked example: Step 1: Sunlight feeds the grass. Step 2: The vole eats grass and keeps warm. Step 3: The warmth spreads away; the vole's body stuff returns to soil later.

**T10. The rough 10 percent rule (older).** At each step up a food chain, only about a tenth of the energy passes on. This is a rough rule. Real amounts vary a lot.
- Worked example: Step 1: Grass, 10,000 units. Step 2: Grasshoppers, about 1,000. Step 3: Frogs, about 100. The rest left mostly as heat.

**T11. People can help a meadow.** Leave dead wood where it is safe. Cut hay late. Pull invasive plants before they seed. Spread hay from a rich meadow.
- Worked example: *green hay.* Step 1: Cut hay from a meadow full of flowers. Step 2: Spread it fresh on bare ground. Step 3: Seeds fall out as it dries.

## The benches

### Sorting tray (`#living/regrowth`, Sort)

**Intro:** Hob's wheelbarrow holds things from the meadow. Sort each one: living, dead (once alive), or never alive.

**Cards** (each with a picture and a "Look closer" fact):

| --- | --- | --- |
| Willow trunk with shoots | New green shoots are growing from it. | Living |
| Rotten willow branch | Soft, crumbly wood. No shoots. Fungus threads inside. | Dead |
| Mushroom on the log | It grew there from tiny threads in the wood. | Living |
| Seed in the mud | It can sprout when it gets water. | Living |
| Wooden fence post | Cut from a tree long ago. | Dead |
| Feather | It fell from a bird. | Dead |
| Empty snail shell | A snail once lived in it. | Dead |

| Brook water | It moves, but it does not grow or feed. | Never alive |
| Glass jar | Made from sand, washed down in the flood. | Never alive |

**After a placement:** You put the {card} in {group}. Say why to {guide}, if you like.

**Gentle second look** (shown when a card lands somewhere unexpected, never "wrong"):
- Moving cards: Does it grow and feed, or only move? Look closer.
- Still cards: Being still isn't the test. Could it grow?
- Wood, feather, shell: Was it ever part of something alive?
- Rock and glass: Was it ever alive, even long ago?

### Web board (`#living/regrowth`, Web)

**Intro:** Build the meadow's food web from chains. Tap the food, then the eater.

**Cards:** Grass · Meadow grasshopper · Field vole · Common frog · Grass snake · Kestrel · Barn owl · Woodlouse · Dead leaves.

**Links the board accepts** (each with its evidence line):

| Link | Evidence line |
| --- | --- |
| Grass → field vole | Field voles eat mostly grass leaves and stems. |
| Grass → meadow grasshopper | Meadow grasshoppers graze on grasses in summer. |
| Field vole → barn owl | Field voles are the barn owl's main food in Britain. |
| Field vole → kestrel | Voles are a big part of a kestrel's food. |
| Meadow grasshopper → common frog | Frogs catch many kinds of insects, grasshoppers among them. |
| Meadow grasshopper → kestrel | In parts of Spain, grasshoppers are a kestrel chick's main food. |
| Common frog → grass snake | Frogs are a main food of grass snakes. |
| Dead leaves → woodlouse | Woodlice eat rotting plant material. |
| Woodlouse → common frog | Frogs eat woodlice too. |

**Read-back (on every new arrow):** This says the {food} is eaten by the {eater}. Is that right?

**A reversed arrow:** This says the {eater} is eaten by the {food}. Try reading it aloud. Does that sound right?

**Board limit (always visible):** A few real feeding links, simplified. These animals eat other things too.

**Steps:** chain one (grass → field vole → barn owl); chain two, sharing the vole (→ kestrel); the grasshopper chains; then dead leaves and the woodlouse. "Decomposers are in the web too," says Moss.

**What if? (after step 4):** Tap any card to take it away. Every arrow touching it lights up.
- **Prompt:** Follow each lit arrow. What may happen along it?
- **Line under every What if?:** "May," not "will." Real animals find other food, move, or surprise us.

### Rot log (`#living/regrowth`, Loop)

**Intro:** Follow one bit of a willow leaf all the way round. Drag or tap the cards into a loop.

**Loop cards:** A willow leaf falls · A woodlouse eats part of it · Fungi and bacteria break down the rest · The stuff goes into the soil · Grass takes it up through its roots · A vole eats the grass · An owl eats the vole · The owl's pellet rots back into the soil.

**When the loop closes:** Back to the soil. The same stuff goes round again. Nothing was used up.

**"What if nothing rotted?" (button):** Dead leaves and logs would pile up, higher and higher. The soil would run short. New plants would struggle. Decomposers keep the loop turning.

**Energy strip (older, beside the loop):** Sunlight in. Warmth out. "The stuff loops," says Moss. "The energy passes through once."

### Energy steps (`#living/regrowth`, Energy, older)

**Intro:** A made-up model. Each step up the chain passes on about a tenth of the energy.

**Where did the rest go?** (tap the arrow) Mostly warmth from living and moving. Some in droppings. Some in parts nobody ate.

**What if? slider:** Try 5 percent or 20 percent instead. The numbers change a lot. The shape stays the same: wide at the bottom, narrow at the top.

**Model line:** These are made-up numbers in a simple picture. Real meadows are messier.

## Guided item (first practice, with visible steps)

**Prompt:** Sort the rotten willow branch.
- Step 1 (shown): Was it ever part of something growing?
- Step 2 (shown): Is it growing now?
- Step 3 (child): Choose living, dead or never alive.
- **Answer:** Dead (once alive). **Feedback:** Yes. It was part of a living tree, and now it's not growing.

## Problem sets

Choices are listed A, B, C in the order shown. Each wrong choice carries its misconception tag for the builder (not shown to the child).

### Set A. What came back (Sprout and Seedling)

**A1 (Sprout, 3-choice).** Is the fallen willow trunk with new shoots alive?
- A. No, it fell down, so it is dead. `fallen-means-dead`. Feedback: Falling over didn't stop it. Look at the new shoots.
- B. Yes, it is growing new shoots. ✓ Feedback: Yes. New shoots mean it is still growing.
- C. No, wood was never alive. `wood-never-alive`. Feedback: Wood grows on trees. This wood is still growing.
- Hint: Look closer. Is anything new growing on it?
- Explanation: New shoots show the trunk is still living.

**A2 (Sprout, number).** 85 of the 100 small squares are bare. How many have plants?
- Answer: 15. Worked steps: 100 small squares in all. Take away 85 bare. 100 − 85 = 15.
- Hint: Start at 85. Count on to 100.
- Explanation: The squares with plants and the bare squares make 100 together.

**A3 (Sprout, 3-choice).** The brook moves. Is the brook alive?
- A. No, it was never alive. ✓ Feedback: Right. It moves, but it doesn't grow or feed.
- B. Yes, because it moves. `moves-means-alive`. Feedback: Moving isn't the test. Does water grow or feed?
- C. It is dead now, after the storm. `dead-equals-never-alive`. Feedback: Dead means once alive. Was the water ever alive?
- Hint: Ask: does it grow, feed and make more?
- Explanation: Water, wind and fire can move. None of them grows or feeds, so none is alive.

**A4 (Seedling, 3-choice).** What is the wooden fence post?
- A. Never alive, because people made it. `made-means-never-alive`. Feedback: People shaped it. What was it made from?
- B. Living, because wood grows on trees. `wood-still-living`. Feedback: Wood grows on a tree. Is this post still growing?
- C. Dead, once part of a tree. ✓ Feedback: Yes. It came from a living tree long ago.
- Hint: What was the post made from?
- Explanation: Things made from wood, wool or paper count as dead: once alive.

**A5 (Seedling, number).** Visit 1 had 2 kinds of plant. Visit 2 had 6. How many more kinds?
- Answer: 4. Worked steps: 6 − 2 = 4.
- Hint: Count up from 2 to 6.
- Explanation: Four new kinds arrived between spring and early summer. Fast pioneers do that.

**A6 (Seedling, 3-choice).** A seed lies still in the mud. What is it?
- A. Living, but resting. ✓ Feedback: Yes. Give it water and it can grow.
- B. Never alive, it doesn't move. `still-means-not-alive`. Feedback: Being still isn't the test. Can it grow?
- C. Dead, because it isn't doing anything. `resting-means-dead`. Feedback: Resting isn't dead. Could it sprout?
- Hint: What can a seed do when it gets water?
- Explanation: Seeds, eggs and sleeping animals are living even when still.

**A7 (Seedling, order the cards).** Put the survey pictures in time order.
- Cards: grasses and meadow flowers · bare cracked mud · fast weeds and willow seedlings.
- Answer: bare cracked mud → fast weeds and willow seedlings → grasses and meadow flowers.
- Misconception tested: `meadow-returns-all-at-once`.
- Hint: Which plants grow fastest on bare ground?
- Explanation: Pioneers grow first on bare ground. Slower plants take over later. That's succession.

**A8 (Seedling, true or not true from this survey).** Two statements; choose "True from the survey" or "The survey doesn't show this."
1. More kinds of plant were found at each visit. **True from the survey.** (Counts: 2, 6, 9, 14, 18.)
2. Every plant from before the storm came back. **The survey doesn't show this.** It counts kinds, not which ones. `survey-shows-everything`.
- Hint: The survey counts kinds. Does it list names from before the storm?
- Explanation: A record can only show what it measured. Nobody surveyed this square before the storm.

### Set B. Who eats what, and where it goes (Seedling and Sapling)

**B1 (Seedling, 3-choice).** Which arrow is drawn the right way?
- A. vole → grass. `arrow-reversed`. Feedback: Read it aloud: "vole is eaten by grass." Does that fit?
- B. grass → vole. ✓ Feedback: Yes. Grass is eaten by the vole.
- C. grass → barn owl. `skips-a-link`. Feedback: Owls don't eat grass. What eats grass first?
- Hint: Arrows point from food to eater.
- Explanation: The arrow follows the food: from what is eaten to what eats it.

**B2 (Seedling, 3-choice).** Which one is a producer, making its own food from sunlight?
- A. Grass. ✓ Feedback: Yes. Grass uses sunlight to make its food.
- B. Grasshopper. `first-animal-is-producer`. Feedback: It eats grass. Who made that food?
- C. Woodlouse. `decomposer-is-producer`. Feedback: It eats dead plants. Does it use sunlight?
- Hint: Which one uses sunlight?
- Explanation: Food webs on land start with green plants.

**B3 (Seedling, order the cards).** Make a food chain from these cards.
- Cards: grass snake · grass · common frog · meadow grasshopper.
- Answer: grass → meadow grasshopper → common frog → grass snake.
- Misconception tested: `chain-starts-with-biggest`.
- Hint: Start with the one that makes its own food.
- Explanation: Chains start with a producer. Each arrow points to the eater.

**B4 (Sapling, 3-choice).** Which animal is food for two different hunters on this board?
- A. Common frog. `eats-two-vs-eaten-by-two`. Feedback: Frogs eat two things here. Who eats frogs?
- B. Field vole. ✓ Feedback: Yes. Kestrels and barn owls both eat voles.
- C. Grass snake. `top-of-chain-confusion`. Feedback: Count the arrows leaving the snake. Any?
- Hint: Count arrows leaving each card.
- Explanation: Arrows leaving a card go to its eaters. The vole has two.

**B5 (Sapling, 3-choice, What if?).** The voles vanish. What may happen to the barn owls?
- A. The owls may start eating lots of grass. `hunters-switch-to-plants`. Feedback: Owls hunt animals. Follow the arrows into the owl.
- B. The owls will surely all vanish. `certain-collapse`. Feedback: Owls may hunt other food or move. "May" fits better.
- C. The owls may find much less food. ✓ Feedback: Yes. Voles are a big part of their food.
- Hint: Follow every lit arrow from the vole.
- Explanation: Losing a food may hurt its eaters, depending on what else they eat.

**B6 (Sapling, 3-choice, What if?).** The voles vanish. What may happen to the grass?
- A. The grass may grow taller. ✓ Feedback: Yes. Fewer animals are eating it.
- B. Nothing; only owls and kestrels change. `effects-only-go-up`. Feedback: Follow the arrow into the vole too.
- C. The grass may die out too. `everything-crashes`. Feedback: What did voles do to grass?
- Hint: Arrows go into the vole as well as out.
- Explanation: Effects spread both ways along the arrows.

**B7 (Sapling, order the loop).** Put the loop cards in order.
- Cards: grass takes it up · the stuff goes into the soil · a woodlouse eats part of a willow leaf · fungi and bacteria break down the rest.
- Answer: a woodlouse eats part of a willow leaf → fungi and bacteria break down the rest → the stuff goes into the soil → grass takes it up.
- Misconception tested: `decomposers-omitted`.
- Hint: Start with the leaf on the ground.
- Explanation: Decomposers turn dead things back into soil stuff that plants use again.

**B8 (Sapling, 3-choice).** A fallen leaf rots away. Where does its stuff go?
- A. It is used up and gone. `matter-used-up`. Feedback: Rotting doesn't make stuff vanish. Where does it go?
- B. Into the soil, then new plants. ✓ Feedback: Yes. Decomposers return it to the soil.
- C. It stays a leaf if nothing eats it. `nothing-rots`. Feedback: Fungi and bacteria break it down anyway.
- Hint: Think about the rot log.
- Explanation: The stuff of living things is recycled again and again.

**B9 (Sapling, say why to a grown-up).** What does the willow log do for the meadow? What did Bramble get right?
- A good answer: It feeds fungi, woodlice and beetle grubs, and gives them a home. Bramble was right that it blocked the path. (Spoken, not saved.)
- Hint: Look under the bark. Then look at the path.
- Explanation: A fair answer can agree with part of someone's point.

### Set C. The Keeper's Desk: energy, change and resilience

**C1 (number with steps).** Grass holds 10,000 energy units. About how many reach the grass snake?
- Shown under the prompt: grass → grasshopper → frog → grass snake. Use the rough 10 percent rule.
- Answer: about 10. Worked steps: 10,000 ÷ 10 = 1,000 (grasshoppers); 1,000 ÷ 10 = 100 (frogs); 100 ÷ 10 = 10 (snake).
- Misconception tested: `energy-passes-efficiently`.
- Hint: Divide by 10 once for each arrow.
- Explanation: Three arrows, three divisions. Little reaches the top.

**C2 (3-choice).** At each step, where does most of the energy go?
- A. It is destroyed. `energy-destroyed`. Feedback: Energy isn't destroyed. It goes somewhere else.
- B. It piles up and is stored in the top hunter. `top-has-most`. Feedback: The snake gets the least. Check the numbers.
- C. Mostly warmth from living and moving. ✓ Feedback: Yes. Plus droppings and parts nobody ate.
- Hint: Tap the arrow on the Energy steps bench.
- Explanation: Energy leaves the chain as heat, waste and uneaten parts. It isn't used up.

**C3 (number, what if).** Grass holds 50,000 units. Frogs are two steps up. Try three rates.
- Part 1: About how much reaches frogs at 10 percent?
- Part 2: At 5 percent?
- Part 3: At 20 percent?
- Part 4: The 20 percent answer is how many times the 5 percent answer?
- Answer: 500; 125; 2,000; 16 times. Worked steps: 50,000 × 0.1 × 0.1 = 500. 50,000 × 0.05 × 0.05 = 125. 50,000 × 0.2 × 0.2 = 2,000. 2,000 ÷ 125 = 16.
- Misconception tested: `rule-is-exact`.
- Hint: Do one step at a time.
- Explanation: Small rate changes grow fast over steps, so "about 10 percent" is only a rough guide.

**C4 (3-choice).** Why is there so much grass and so few grass snakes?
- A. Little energy reaches the top. ✓ Feedback: Yes. Only enough for a few up there.
- B. Big animals need much less food. `big-needs-less`. Feedback: Big animals usually need more food.
- C. Snakes are rarer by bad luck. `chance-not-energy`. Feedback: Look at the energy numbers again.
- Hint: Look at the Energy steps pyramid.
- Explanation: Less energy at each level supports fewer living things there, so chains rarely get long.

**C5 (number).** Visit 5: the meadow has 18 kinds of plant, the lawn 4.
- Part 1: How many more kinds does the meadow have?
- Part 2: How many times as many?
- Answer: 14 more; 4.5 times as many. Worked steps: 18 − 4 = 14. 18 ÷ 4 = 4.5.
- Hint: Subtract for "how many more." Divide for "how many times."
- Explanation: Both ways of comparing are honest. They answer different questions.

**C6 (3-choice).** A dry summer comes. Which square is more likely to keep some green?
- A. The lawn, because it has fewer kinds. `fewer-is-safer`. Feedback: Fewer kinds means fewer ways to cope.
- B. The meadow, with many more kinds. ✓ Feedback: Yes. If one kind wilts, another may cope.
- C. Both the same; kinds don't matter. `diversity-irrelevant`. Feedback: Grassland studies found kinds can matter.
- Hint: If one kind wilts, is there another?
- Explanation: In long grassland studies, plots with more kinds of plants held up better in drought. "More likely," never "certain."

**C7 (3-choice).** Hob stops cutting hay for many years. What may grow there?
- A. It stays a meadow forever. `meadow-is-final`. Feedback: What did Hob say would win?
- B. It slowly turns back into bare mud. `goes-backwards`. Feedback: Bare mud came after the flood. What grows next?
- C. Willow scrub, then wet woodland. ✓ Feedback: Yes. Cutting is what keeps it a meadow.
- Hint: Remember Scene 5.
- Explanation: On many floodplains, without cutting or grazing, willow arrives, then other trees.

**C8 (say why to a grown-up).** Why start pulling balsam upstream?
- A good answer: Its seeds wash downstream, so clearing the top stops new seeds arriving below. (Spoken, not saved.)
- Hint: Which way does the brook carry seeds?
- Explanation: River groups often start at the top of the river for this reason.

**C9 (3-choice).** Which plan helps the bare patch come back with more kinds of plant?
- A. Mow it short every week to keep it tidy. `tidy-is-healthy`. Feedback: Weekly mowing kept the lawn at four kinds.
- B. Sow just one strong kind of grass everywhere. `one-kind-is-enough`. Feedback: One kind gives one kind. What gives many?
- C. Spread fresh hay from a rich meadow. ✓ Feedback: Yes. Seeds fall out of the hay.
- Hint: Where could lots of different seeds come from?
- Explanation: Green hay is a real way to move meadow seeds. Not every kind takes.

## Key terms

- **Living:** grows, feeds, breathes and makes more of its kind.
- **Dead:** was once alive, and isn't now.
- **Never alive:** was never alive at all, like stone or water.
- **Survey:** counting what is in a place, the same way each time.
- **Producer:** a living thing that makes its own food from sunlight. Green plants are producers.
- **Consumer:** a living thing that eats other living things.
- **Decomposer:** a living thing that breaks down dead things, like fungi and bacteria.
- **Food web:** many food chains joined together.
- **Fungi:** living things such as mushrooms and molds. They are not plants.
- **Pioneer plant:** one of the first plants to grow on bare ground.
- **Succession:** how the living things in a place change over time.
- **Invasive plant:** a plant from somewhere else that spreads and crowds out others.
- **Biodiversity:** how many different kinds of living things a place has.
