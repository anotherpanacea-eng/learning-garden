## Page 1. Hot, wet and near the middle

**Teach card 1:** A tropical rainforest is a thick, tall forest. It is hot, it is wet, and it grows near the Equator.

**Teach card 2:** It is warm all year, usually above 20 °C (68 °F). Many get more than 2,000 mm of rain a year.

**Teach card 3:** Rainforests hold a huge share of the world's kinds of plants and animals. Scientists think it may be about half.

**Worked example: is it a rainforest? (one step per tap)**
1. Card: *a forest in the Congo Basin, hot all year, rain most days.*
2. Hot? Yes. Wet? Yes.
3. Near the Equator? The Congo Basin is right on it.
4. So it is a tropical rainforest.

**Contrast card:** A desert can be hot too. But a desert is dry. Hot and wet together make a rainforest.

**Compare card: our wood and a rainforest**
| Willowbank's willow copse | A tropical rainforest |
| --- | --- |
| Cool winters, warm summers | Warm all year |
| Leaves fall in autumn | Green all year |
| Rain some days | Rain most days |
| Many living things | Far more kinds of living things |

**Map card (Teach card 4):** Tropical rainforests grow in a belt around the middle of the Earth. Three great ones: the Amazon, the Congo Basin and Southeast Asia.

**Worked example: reading the map**
1. Find the line around the middle. That's the Equator.
2. The green band hugs it.
3. Tap the Amazon in South America, the Congo Basin in Africa, and the islands of Southeast Asia.
4. No green band reaches Europe or Antarctica. They have no tropical rainforest.

**Map note (always shown):** A simplified map. Not to scale. Smaller rainforests in Central America, Madagascar and Australia are shown as dots.

## Page 2. Up through the canopy

**Teach card 1:** A rainforest has layers, like floors of a very tall building. From the ground up: forest floor, understory, canopy, emergent.

**Climb lines (tap Up to climb, one layer at a time):**
- **Forest floor:** Dark and damp. Fallen leaves rot fast. Fungi and insects are busy here.
- **Understory:** Shady. Young trees and shrubs with big leaves catch what light gets through.
- **Canopy:** A thick roof of treetops. Sunny and full of fruit and flowers. Most rainforest animals live up here.
- **Emergent:** A few giant trees poke out above the roof, often 40 to 60 m tall. Bright sun, strong wind.

**Teach card 2:** Light is the big difference. The canopy catches most of the sunlight. Very little reaches the floor.

**Light meter line (beside the climb):** Only about 1 to 5 of every 100 parts of sunlight reach the floor, depending on the spot.
**Light meter note:** A simplified picture. Real forests vary from place to place and hour to hour.

**Worked example: why so much life up high?**
1. Plants need light to make food.
2. The canopy gets lots of light, so it has lots of leaves, fruit and flowers.
3. Animals come for the food.
4. So most animals live in the canopy, not on the dark floor.

**Card placing (after the climb):** Put each card on its layer. A kapok tree's crown → emergent. A howler monkey → canopy. A young palm with wide leaves → understory. Fungi on a fallen log → forest floor. Not checked.

**Revisit line:** You may have seen a rainforest postcard at the bird pass. This page climbs all four layers.

## Page 3. Leaf and root bench

**Teach card 1:** An adaptation is a feature that helps a living thing survive where it lives. Rainforest plants have some clever ones.

**Teach card 2:** Each adaptation solves a problem. Too much rain. Too little light down low. Thin soil for a giant tree.

**Predict first (Leaf bench):** Two leaves sit under the rain spout: one round, one with a long pointed tip. *Which one will shed water faster?* Tap your guess, then tap **Rain**.

**Leaf bench result:** The pointed tip drains the water. The round leaf holds a puddle.
**Leaf bench line (always shown):** A made-up model. It shows the idea, not real timings.

**Teach card 3:** That pointed end is a drip tip. Water runs off fast, so less moss and mold grow on the leaf.

**Worked example: structure and problem (one row per tap)**
1. **Drip tip** → problem: rain all the time → solves it: water runs off.
2. **Buttress roots** → problem: a giant tree in thin soil → solves it: wide flat roots brace the trunk, like a tent's ropes.
3. **Epiphyte** (an orchid or bromeliad on a branch) → problem: the floor is too dark → solves it: grow up high, near the light.
4. **Liana** (a woody vine) → problem: building a thick trunk takes a long time → solves it: climb another tree to the light.

**Contrast card 1:** An epiphyte is not a thief. It only sits on the tree. It makes its own food from light and catches water from rain and air.

**Contrast card 2:** Buttress roots spread wide, not deep. The soil is thin, so the roots stay near the top.

**Contrast card 3:** No plant chose its adaptation. Parent plants pass features on to their seeds, over many, many generations.

## Page 4. The forest's own rain

**Teach card 1:** Roots take up water. Leaves let water out as an invisible gas called water vapor. This is transpiration.

**Teach card 2:** The vapor rises, cools and turns into tiny drops. That's a cloud. Rain falls back on the forest.

**Worked example: follow one drop (one step per tap)**
1. Rain soaks into the soil.
2. A tree's roots take it up.
3. A leaf lets it out as vapor.
4. The vapor rises, cools and joins a cloud. It rains again.

**Teach card 3:** Rain also blows in from the ocean. But in the Amazon, about a third of the rain began as water from the forest itself.

**Teach card 4 (Sapling):** In the dry season, at one forest studied, up to about 7 in 10 parts of the rain came from this recycled water. Fewer trees can mean less of it.

**Loop bench:** Five arrow cards to put in a circle. The bench has a **Trees** dial with three settings: many, half, few. At each setting the cloud over the forest grows or shrinks and a rain gauge fills more or less.
**Loop bench line (always shown):** A made-up model. Real rain depends on winds, the ocean and much more.

**Contrast card:** Transpiration is not breathing. It is water leaving a leaf as vapor.

## Page 5. Where the nutrients are

**Teach card 1:** Plants make food from sunlight. Animals eat plants, or eat other animals. Decomposers, like fungi, break down what dies.

**Food arrows card:** A fig tree's fruit → a toucan. Fallen leaves → fungi. The arrow points from the food to the eater, like the food links in the garden.

**Teach card 2:** Nutrients are what living things need to grow. In most rainforests, they are stored in the living plants, not the soil.

**Teach card 3:** Warm and wet means decomposers work fast. Roots and fungi grab the nutrients back before rain washes them away.

**Bramble's claim card:** *A story from Willowbank.* Bramble says: "Look how much grows there. The soil must be the richest anywhere."

**Worked example: test Bramble's claim**
1. Look at the soil picture. The dark top layer is thin.
2. Under it is reddish clay with few nutrients.
3. Most nutrients are in the trees, leaves and fungi.
4. So the forest is rich, but the soil is not. Bramble is right that it grows fast.

**Nutrient bank (tokens):** Ten tokens stand for one forest's nutrients. Rainforest: 8 in living things, 2 in the soil. Willowbank's wood: 4 in living things, 6 in the soil. Tap **Clear the trees** on either side.
**Nutrient bank line (always shown):** A made-up model. Real numbers differ from forest to forest. It shows where most nutrients sit.

**Cleared plot (Sapling):** A made-up plot is cleared and farmed. Harvests: year 1, 10 baskets. Year 2, 6. Year 3, 3.
**Cleared plot line:** Made-up numbers for practice. They show a pattern that real farms on thin soil often follow.

**Contrast card:** In a cool wood, many nutrients stay in deep soil. Clear the trees there and much of the soil's store is still there.

## Page 6. People who know the forest

**Page note (always shown):** These are real people living today. Their ways of life change, like everyone's. A card can only tell a little.

**Teach card 1:** Indigenous peoples have lived in rainforests for thousands of years. Many live there today and know the forest in great detail.

**Card: the Yanomami** · A true thing about people today
- About 35,000 Yanomami people live in the Amazon rainforest, in Brazil and Venezuela.
- The name comes from their own word *yanõmami*, for human beings.
- In many villages, the whole community lives in one large round house, a *yano* or *shabono*.
- Families grow plantains, bananas and peach palm in gardens. They hunt, fish and gather.
- One study with one community recorded at least 113 plants and fungi they use as medicine.
- Yanomami people run their own groups, such as the Hutukara Association, to look after their land.

**Card: the Baka** · A true thing about people today
- The Baka live in the Congo Basin rainforest, in Cameroon, Gabon, the Republic of the Congo and the Central African Republic. *Baka* is their own name.
- They gather wild yams, honey, nuts and mushrooms, and they fish and set snares.
- The Baka language has many words for forest plants, medicines and honey gathering.
- One survey in one region of Cameroon recorded 88 wild plants that Baka people eat.
- Many Baka families today also farm and live in villages by roads, and still go into the forest to gather.

**Teach card 2:** Some families farm a small garden for a few years. Then they clear a new one nearby and let the old one grow back. This is shifting cultivation.

**Cycle card (four steps around a circle):** Clear a small patch → grow food for a few years → leave it to rest → the forest grows back.

**Contrast card:** Clearing a huge area for good is different. The thin soil wears out, and the forest can't easily return.

**Teach card 3 (Sapling):** Studies of satellite pictures find less forest loss in many Indigenous territories than on similar unprotected land.

**Teach card 4 (Keeper's Desk):** Scientists and Indigenous experts often work together. Each knows things the other does not.

**History card: curare (Keeper's Desk)** · A true thing from history
> Hunters of the Amazon, including Achuar and Canelos Quichua people in Ecuador, have long made a plant mixture called curare for their darts. It relaxes muscles. In 1942, doctors first used a medicine made from it to relax muscles during an operation.

## Page 7. From forest to table

**Teach card 1:** Many well-known foods began in tropical forests. Chocolate, bananas, vanilla and Brazil nuts all grow where it is hot and wet.

**Café table:** Tap a food on Ember's birthday table to travel back to its plant.
- **Chocolate:** Made from the beans of the cacao tree. The pods grow straight out of the trunk. Each pod holds about 40 beans.
- **Banana:** The banana plant looks like a tree, but it's a giant herb. Its "trunk" is leaf stalks rolled tight.
- **Vanilla:** A vanilla pod comes from an orchid, a vine that climbs trees. It first grew in Mexico and Central America.
- **Brazil nut:** Brazil nuts grow inside a hard, round pod on a giant emergent tree. Each pod holds about 10 to 25 nuts.

**Teach card 2:** Where a food first grew and where most is grown now can be different places.

**Worked example: cacao on the map (one step per tap)**
1. Wild cacao first grew in the upper Amazon, in South America.
2. People later carried it to other hot, wet places.
3. Today most cacao is grown in West Africa.
4. In 2018, Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana grew more than half of it.

**Card: the Brazil nut needs the forest** · Science
> Large bees called orchid bees carry pollen between Brazil nut flowers. Animals called agoutis bury the nuts, and some forgotten ones sprout. So most Brazil nuts are gathered from wild forest trees.

**History card: vanilla (Keeper's Desk)** · A true thing from history
> The Totonac people of Mexico have grown vanilla for centuries, and many still do. Taken to faraway islands, the vines made few pods, because the insects that pollinate them in Mexico were missing. In 1841, on the island of Réunion, Edmond Albius, a boy of about 12 who was enslaved, found a quick way to pollinate the flowers by hand. Growers still use his method. Madagascar grows the most vanilla today.

**Grown-up line under the table:** This table is Ember's, in the story. Nothing here asks about your own food.

## Page 8. Cool rainforests

**Teach card 1:** Not every rainforest is hot. Temperate rainforests grow in cool, very wet places near the coast.

**Teach card 2:** Find them on the Pacific coast of North America, in southern Chile, New Zealand, Tasmania and Norway, and in western Britain and Ireland.

**Worked example: tropical or temperate?**
1. Card: *the Hoh Rain Forest, Washington State, USA. Cool winters. More than 3,000 mm of rain a year.*
2. Wet? Very.
3. Hot all year? No. It has cool winters.
4. So it is a temperate rainforest.

**Teach card 3:** Britain and Ireland's rainforests are small patches now. Mosses, liverworts and lichens cover their trees and rocks.

**Map note:** So Europe has rainforests after all. They are temperate, not tropical.

## Page 9. Two needs, one forest (Keeper's Desk)

**Page note:** A real place far from Willowbank. Read it as a comparison. It does not decide the town meeting.

**Teach card 1:** Forests are cleared for many reasons. In Brazil, most cleared rainforest land becomes pasture for cattle.

**Teach card 2:** In Indonesia and Malaysia, logging and palm oil farms have cleared large areas. Mining and roads matter in many places.

**Four people cards** (each says what that person needs; no card speaks for anyone):
- **A ranching family** needs land and an income.
- **An Indigenous community** needs to keep its land and make its own choices about it.
- **A country's government** wants jobs, roads and money for schools and hospitals.
- **People far away** buy beef, soy, palm oil and chocolate.

**Teach card 3:** There is good news with evidence. Since 2006, big soy buyers in Brazil agreed not to buy soy from newly cleared Amazon land. Soy's part in clearing fell.

**Teach card 4:** Recognized Indigenous lands lose less forest. But illegal mining and logging by outsiders inside some territories grew between 2013 and 2021.

**Trade-off card:** No choice here helps everyone equally. A good plan says who gains, who loses, and what evidence shows.

## Key terms

- **Rainforest:** A thick forest that gets a lot of rain. Some are hot (tropical), and some are cool (temperate).
- **Equator:** The imaginary line around the middle of the Earth.
- **Habitat:** A home that gives living things what they need.
- **Adaptation:** A feature, passed down from parents, that helps a living thing survive where it lives.
- **Drip tip:** A pointed leaf end that lets rain run off.
- **Buttress roots:** Wide, flat roots at the bottom of a big tree that hold it up.
- **Epiphyte:** A plant that grows on another plant without feeding on it.
- **Liana:** A woody vine that climbs trees to reach the light.
- **Transpiration:** Water leaving a plant's leaves as vapor.
- **Decomposer:** A living thing, like a fungus, that breaks down dead plants and animals.
- **Nutrients:** Things living things need to grow, which pass from soil to plants to animals and back.
- **Indigenous peoples:** The first peoples of a place, and their families who live there now.
- **Shifting cultivation:** Farming a small plot for a few years, then letting the forest grow back.
