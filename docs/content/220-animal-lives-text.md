# Animal Lives: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 220](../specs/220-animal-lives.md). Use as written. `{guide}` is the chosen companion; `{courier}` is the explorer's courier; the explorer is "you". US spelling, no em-dashes. Facts marked [check] stay out of the build until the source audit clears them. Depth tags (Sprout, Seedling, Sapling, Keeper's Desk) describe the task, never the child.

## Labels used on every card

- **A story from Willowbank** (fiction: the town, its people, its show, its garden and its letters)
- **A true thing about science**
- **A true thing from history**
- **A story people told** (old tales that later evidence did not back up; always paired with what we know now)
- **A made-up model** (bench rules, always with their model line)

**Wild-animal line** (word for word on arrival, on the Lookout Meadow and Partners benches, and in the Grown-up panel): *Wild animals are for watching from a distance. Off screen, a grown-up leads, and nobody touches or picks up a wild animal.*

## Arriving

Hook (A story from Willowbank): The librarian has a new shelf: *Animal Lives*. Who is biggest? Who lives with whom? How do we know what animals think?

Doors:
- **Record Parade**: Biggest, tallest, fastest, smallest.
- **Ocean Key**: Fish, mammal, or no backbone at all?
- **Lookout Meadow**: Why live in a herd?
- **Partners Grid**: Who gains? Who loses?
- **How Do We Know?** Test what animals can do.
- **Ocean Year**: Follow a whale and a turtle.

## Story scenes

A kept bench in a scene's chapter can count once, for one chapter. No scene decides anything for the town.

### Scene 1. The biggest show on the green (Act II, chapter 4: the first show)

**Short:** The first show needs a parade of record animals. Pip says the biggest animal ever was a dinosaur. Dot turns the card over. It says something else.

**Longer:** For the town's first show, the librarian painted cut-outs of record-breaking animals. Pip held up the dinosaur. "Biggest animal ever. Everybody knows that."

Dot adjusted her hat and turned the card over. "The back says blue whale."

"A big blue whale is longer than two school buses end to end," read {guide}. "And heavier than any dinosaur anyone has found."

Pip looked at the dinosaur. Then at the whale. "Can the dinosaur be the tallest, then?"

"The giraffe is the tallest," said Dot. "The dinosaur can still be in the show."

"Every record is its own kind of most," said {guide}. "Biggest, tallest, fastest, smallest. Let's stand each one next to something we know."

Pip put the cheetah at the front. Nobody argued with that.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** No animal ever found is bigger or heavier than the blue whale. A few long-necked dinosaurs might have been about as long, but none was as heavy. The biggest animal on land today is the elephant.

### Scene 2. Ants on the bean plants (Act II, chapter 2: a garden across the seasons)

**Short:** Tiny black aphids crowd the bean stalks. Ants hurry among them. "The ants are eating them!" says Pip. {guide} looks closer. "Are they?"

**Longer:** By midsummer the bean stalks were crowded with tiny black aphids. Ants hurried up and down among them.

"The ants are eating them," said Pip.

{guide} crouched down. "Watch that one."

An ant stroked an aphid with its feelers. The aphid gave up a sweet, sticky drop, and the ant drank it.

A ladybug landed on the stalk. Three ants ran at it, and it flew off.

"They're guarding them," said Dot.

The orchard keeper leaned on the fence and pointed up at the old apple tree. High in its branches hung a round green ball of leaves that were not apple leaves. "Mistletoe," she said. "That one only takes."

"So some partners both win," said {guide}, "and some don't. Let's sort them out."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** Mistletoe makes some of its own food from sunlight, but its roots grow into the branch and take water and nutrients from the tree. A little mistletoe rarely kills a tree; a lot can weaken it.

### Scene 3. Whistles on the high ford (Act III, chapter 3: the route by the high ford)

**Short:** On the high path, a herd of izards grazes. One whistles. Every head comes up at once. "Who told her to whistle?" asks Pip.

**Longer:** The old path to Vadoalto climbed above the trees. {courier} stopped. On the slope ahead, a herd of izards was grazing, heads down.

"Wild mountain goats?" whispered Pip.

"Cousins of goats," said {guide}. "Stay here. We watch from a distance."

High above, a golden eagle circled. One izard lifted her head and gave a sharp whistle. Every head came up. The whole herd trotted up the rocks and stood still, watching.

The eagle drifted away over the ridge.

"Who told her to whistle?" asked Pip.

"Nobody," said {guide}. "She saw it, and the whistle carried. Count the heads that were up before she whistled."

Pip counted. "Just one or two. The rest were eating."

"So in a herd," said {guide}, "most of them get to eat, and somebody is still watching. Is that true for an izard on her own?"

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** The izard is the Pyrenean chamois. Females and young live together in herds. Older males spend much of the year alone. A whistle or a stamp warns the herd.

### Scene 4. A box from the river mouth (Act III, chapter 4: letters up and down the river)

**Short:** Léon sends a box of sea-creature cards. They came up the big river from the coast. His note says, "Tous des poissons! All fish!" Dot isn't so sure.

**Longer:** The courier's bag held a flat box tied with blue string. Inside were ten picture cards from the market where the big river meets the sea: a jellyfish, an octopus, a crab, a starfish, a shark, a seahorse, a cod, a seal, a dolphin and a sea turtle.

Léon's note said: *Tous des poissons, bien sûr! All fish, of course! They all come from the sea.*

"A starfish isn't a fish," said Dot. "Is it?"

"Its name says fish," said Pip. "So does jellyfish."

{guide} laid the cards in a row. "Names can fool us. Living in the sea can fool us too. Let's ask each card three questions, in order. The first one is: does it have a backbone?"

Pip picked up the jellyfish and turned it over, looking for one.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** Most kinds of ocean animals have no backbone at all. Snails, crabs, jellyfish, worms, corals and many more are invertebrates. Fish, whales and seals are the minority.

### Scene 5. The magpie in the Gazette (Act IV, chapter 3: the newspaper's two explanations)

**Short:** Bramble writes to the *Gazette*: "My magpie knows who's in the mirror." The librarian prints it, with a question underneath: "How would we know?"

**Longer:** The *Willowbank Gazette* had a new corner called *Letters*. Bramble's was first.

*My magpie is the cleverest bird in the valley. It knows who is in the mirror.*

Underneath, the librarian had printed: *How would we know?*

Bramble marched into the library with the magpie on her shoulder. "It looks in the mirror every morning."

"So do I," said Pip. "I'm not sure I know who it is."

The magpie hopped onto the reading table, saw itself in the brass lamp and pecked at it.

"It thinks that's another magpie," said Pip.

"Or it's checking its feathers," said Bramble.

"Scientists had the same argument," said the librarian. "So they found a test. Would you like to see what happened?"

Bramble folded her arms. "Only if the magpie wins."

"The test decides," said {guide}. "Not us."

**Keeper's note (A true thing from history):** In 2008, scientists reported that two of five hand-raised magpies passed the mirror mark test. In 2020, a second team tested wild-caught magpies, and none passed. One test, done twice, gave two answers. Scientists say it isn't settled.

### Scene 6. Whales past the river mouth (Act VI, chapter 4: the first journey, and someone new)

**Short:** At supper, Hollis talks about whales passing the coast every year. "I watched them for years," says Hollis. "I never found out where they were going."

**Longer:** The café was full. Hollis sat by the window, still a little damp from the river path.

"On the coast," said Hollis, "whales went by every year. Far out, at the same time each year. A spout, a back, then gone."

"Where were they going?" asked Pip.

"I never found out," said Hollis. "I kept a record of when I saw them. That's all."

{guide} brought a book from the library shelf. "Some whales keep a timetable too. Here is one that scientists have followed across a whole ocean."

Hollis leaned in to look. "Food at one end," Hollis read, "and a safe warm place for the young at the other."

"Like coming home," said Dot.

Hollis laughed. "I came home for supper. The whale's trip is a good deal longer."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** Some humpback whales swim about 3,000 miles (about 4,800 km) between Alaska and Hawaii, sometimes in as few as 28 days. Some humpback groups travel about 5,000 miles.

## Teach cards

Each worked example reveals one step per tap.

**T1. A record is a kind of most** (A true thing about science). Biggest, tallest, fastest and smallest are different records. Different animals hold them.
Worked example: (1) Biggest: blue whale. (2) Tallest: giraffe. (3) Fastest on land: cheetah. (4) Smallest bird: bee hummingbird.

**T2. Stand it next to something you know.** A record means more next to something familiar.
Worked example: (1) Blue whale: longer than two school buses end to end. (2) Giraffe: tall enough to look in an upstairs window.

**T3. Say exactly what the record is.** The cheetah is fastest on land, for a short dash. A diving falcon is faster. The bee hummingbird is the smallest bird, not the smallest animal.
Worked example: (1) Ask: fastest where? (2) On land: cheetah. (3) Diving through the air: peregrine falcon.

**T4. The backbone question comes first.** A backbone is a row of bones down an animal's back. Animals with no backbone are called invertebrates.
Worked example: (1) Jellyfish: backbone? No. (2) So it is an invertebrate. (3) Its name doesn't change that.

**T5. Gills or air?** Fish breathe through gills. Marine mammals breathe air at the surface and feed their babies milk.
Worked example: (1) Shark: backbone? Yes. (2) Gills? Yes. (3) Fish. (4) Dolphin: backbone, no gills, milk: mammal.

**T6. Features decide, not looks.** Fins, the sea and a name don't decide the group. The key's questions do.
Worked example: (1) Dolphin has fins. (2) But it breathes air and feeds milk. (3) Mammal.

**T7. A group can help each animal** (Seedling). Animals in a herd, pack or colony can each be safer, find food or raise young more easily.
Worked example: (1) One izard looks up. (2) Nine others eat. (3) Danger is spotted. (4) Every izard gets the warning.

**T8. Say what happens, not what they feel.** Explain a group by what it does for each animal, not by "they like company".
Worked example: (1) Not: "they are friends". (2) Instead: "more eyes spot the eagle sooner".

**T9. Groups cost something too.** In a big group, there is less food for each animal. A good argument weighs both sides.
Worked example: (1) Ten izards, one patch of grass. (2) Each gets less. (3) Bigger is not always better.

**T10. Claim, evidence, reasoning** (Sapling). A claim says what you think. Evidence is what you saw. Reasoning links them.
Worked example: (1) Claim: a herd helps each izard. (2) Evidence: someone watched all ten minutes. (3) Reasoning: danger seen sooner.

**T11. Symbiosis** (Sapling). Symbiosis is two different kinds of living things that live closely together for a long time.
Worked example: (1) Clownfish lives in an anemone. (2) Different kinds. (3) Together for life. (4) Symbiosis.

**T12. Ask what each partner gains or loses.** Gains: +. No clear change: 0. Loses: −. Mutualism is + and +. Commensalism is + and 0. Parasitism is + and −.
Worked example: (1) Tick drinks deer's blood. (2) Tick: +. (3) Deer: −. (4) Parasitism.

**T13. Not every meeting is symbiosis.** An owl catching a mouse is quick. Symbiosis is a long life side by side.
Worked example: (1) Owl and mouse: one quick meeting. (2) Not symbiosis. (3) Tick and deer: days together. (4) Symbiosis.

**T14. How do we know an animal is thinking?** Seeing something isn't enough. Scientists look for a simpler explanation, then design a test.
Worked example: (1) Seen: a horse taps out sums. (2) Simpler idea: it reads its owner's face. (3) Test: an asker who doesn't know.

**T15. The mirror mark test.** Put a mark where the animal can't see it. Give it a mirror. Does it check the mark on its own body?
Worked example: (1) Mark the dolphin's side. (2) It swims to the mirror. (3) It turns to see the mark. (4) Self-recognition.

**T16. Many kinds of clever.** Crows make tools, octopuses open jars, meerkats teach. Each is clever in ways that fit its life. There's no single ladder.
Worked example: (1) Octopus: eight arms, no bones, opens lids. (2) Crow: beak tools. (3) Different, not higher or lower.

**T17. A migration is a round trip** (Sapling). Many ocean animals travel the same long route every year, there and back.
Worked example: (1) Summer: humpback feeds in cold northern water. (2) Winter: swims to warm water. (3) Spring: back north.

**T18. Food at one end, young at the other.** Cold water fills with food in summer. Warm, calm water is safer for newborn calves.
Worked example: (1) Summer: krill swarm up north. (2) The whale eats and stores blubber. (3) Winter: calves born in warm water.

**T19. Home beach** (Keeper's Desk). A female sea turtle swims back to nest on the beach where she hatched, or very near it.
Worked example: (1) She hatches on a beach. (2) She travels for years. (3) She returns to that coast to lay eggs.

## Activity (a): Record Parade

Opening: Line up the record animals. Stand each one next to something you know.
Cards: blue whale · giraffe · cheetah · bee hummingbird · elephant · peregrine falcon.
Beside-things: two school buses · an upstairs window · a car in town · a pencil · a nickel.
Guided first item (2 steps shown): (1) Pick the blue whale. (2) Slide the school buses beside it. Which is longer?
Placement replies:
- Blue whale beside the buses: Longer than two school buses end to end!
- Giraffe beside the window: Tall enough to peek in an upstairs window.
- Cheetah beside the car: Faster than a car on a town street, for a short dash.
- Bee hummingbird beside the pencil: Small enough to perch on a pencil.
- Bee hummingbird beside the nickel: Lighter than a nickel coin.
- Any other pairing: Try another. Which thing shows this record best?
**Kind of most** sorter: Put each record in its row: **biggest**, **tallest**, **fastest**, **smallest**.
Sorter reply (giraffe in biggest): The giraffe is the tallest. Which animal is the heaviest?
Sorter reply (cheetah in fastest, with the falcon card): Fastest on land. The falcon dives faster.
Dinosaur card (A true thing about science): Some dinosaurs were huge. No dinosaur yet found was as heavy as a blue whale.

## Activity (c): Lookout Meadow

Wild-animal line (shown on entry).
Opening: How many izards in the herd? Predict, then watch ten minutes.
Buttons: herd **1** / **2** / **5** / **10**; alone only: look up **2** / **6** / **10** minutes; **Watch ten minutes**.
Readouts: Minutes someone is watching: {w} of 10. Bites of grass each: {b} (each izard needs 4).
Rules card (A made-up model): Each izard looks up for 2 minutes in every 10. They take turns as much as they can. The patch has 20 bites of grass. Grazing gives half a bite a minute.
Guided first run (3 steps): (1) Herd of 1, looking up 2 minutes. (2) Watch. (3) Only 2 minutes are watched. Now try a herd of 5.
Results:
- 1 izard, look up 2: Watched 2 minutes of 10. Lots of time with nobody looking.
- 1 izard, look up 6: Watched 6 minutes. But only 2 bites. Not enough grass.
- 1 izard, look up 10: Always watching. No time to eat at all.
- 2 izards: Watched 4 minutes. 4 bites each.
- 5 izards: Watched all 10 minutes. 4 bites each.
- 10 izards: Watched all 10 minutes. Only 2 bites each. The grass runs short.
Model line (A made-up model): Real izards look up for different lengths of time, and herds change size through the year. This bench uses four herd sizes and one patch of grass.

**Claim Builder** (Sapling). Opening: Build an argument. Pick a claim, two pieces of evidence and a reason.
Claim cards: A herd helps each izard survive. · Izards live in herds because they are friends. · Bigger herds are always better.
Evidence cards: Alone: watched 2 minutes of 10. · Herd of 5: watched 10 minutes, 4 bites each. · Herd of 10: only 2 bites each. · Izards look nice together.
Reason cards: More eyes spot danger sooner, and each still gets enough to eat. · Animals like company. · The leader tells them what to do.
Reply when a feelings card is picked: That's a feeling. What does the herd do for each izard?
Reply when "always better" is picked: Check the herd of 10. What happened to the grass?
Reply when the evidence doesn't match the claim: Does this evidence show your claim? Look at the readouts.
Finished line: Claim, evidence, reasoning. That's an argument.

**Three ways a group helps** (A true thing about science), one card each:
- **Staying safe:** Meerkats take turns as lookouts on a high spot. The lookout calls, and the others keep digging for food.
- **Getting food:** Wolves in a pack hunt big animals, like elk, together. A few wolves catch elk more often than one wolf does. Honeybees dance to tell the hive where flowers are (Bee Meadow link).
- **Raising young:** In an ant colony, workers feed and guard the young that the queen lays. Each worker has a job.

## Activity (d): Partners Grid

Wild-animal line (shown on entry).
Opening: For each pair, mark what each partner gains or loses.
Grid marks: **+ gains** · **0 no clear change** · **− loses** · **? not enough evidence**
Decision path: Partner A gains? Partner B gains, loses, or no clear change? Then name it.
Kinds: **Mutualism (+ +)** · **Commensalism (+ 0)** · **Parasitism (+ −)** · **Not symbiosis**
Guided first pair (3 steps shown): (1) Clownfish: safe among stinging arms: +. (2) Anemone: cleaned and guarded: +. (3) + and +: mutualism.
Pairs and replies:
- Clownfish and sea anemone: Both gain. Mutualism.
- Remora and shark: The remora gets a ride and scraps. The shark seems unchanged. Commensalism. Some scientists think the shark gains a little.
- Tick and deer: The tick feeds for days. The deer loses blood. Parasitism.
- Ants and aphids: Ants get a sweet drink. Aphids get guards. Mutualism.
- Mistletoe and apple tree: Mistletoe takes water from the tree. Parasitism, by a plant.
- Coral and tiny algae: The algae make food inside the coral. The coral gives them a home. Mutualism.
- A bird nesting in an oak: The bird gets a home. Does the tree lose anything? Probably not: commensalism. ? is fair here too.
- Owl and mouse: One quick catch. Not a long life together. Not symbiosis.
- Two blackbirds and one worm: Same kind of bird, one quick squabble. Not symbiosis.
Reply when a parasitism pair is marked "not symbiosis": Symbiosis means living closely together. It doesn't have to be friendly.
Reply when the remora pair is marked + +: Check the shark. Does it clearly gain?
Reply when ants and aphids are called a deal: They didn't agree. Each does what is good for itself, and both gain.
History card (A true thing from history): In 1879, the scientist Anton de Bary used the word *symbiosis* for any different kinds living together, including parasites.

## Activity (e): How Do We Know?

Opening: Open a case file. What was seen? Is there a simpler idea? What test would tell?
Case file steps, shown as three tabs: **What was seen** · **A simpler idea** · **The test, and what it showed**
Guided first case (3 steps shown): Clever Hans. (1) Seen: a horse taps out sums. (2) Simpler idea: he watches the asker. (3) Test: an asker who doesn't know.
Case files:
1. **Clever Hans** (A true thing from history). Seen: in Berlin, about 1904, a horse tapped out answers to sums. Simpler idea: he watched the asker. Test (reported in 1907): when the asker didn't know the answer, or Hans couldn't see them, he failed. Shows: Hans read tiny moves in people's faces and bodies. That's clever too, just not arithmetic.
2. **The dolphin and the mirror** (A true thing about science). Seen: dolphins look at mirrors. Simpler idea: they think it's another dolphin. Test (2001): a mark on a spot they couldn't see. They went to the mirror and twisted to look at the mark. Shows: self-recognition. Chimpanzees passed the same kind of test in 1970.
3. **The magpie and the mirror** (A true thing about science). Seen: magpies peck at mirrors. Test: in 2008 some passed; in 2020 others didn't. Shows: not settled yet.
4. **The crow and the wire** (A true thing about science). Seen: in 2002 at Oxford, a crow named Betty bent a straight wire into a hook and lifted out a bucket of food. Simpler idea: wild crows bend twigs this way already. Shows: tool-making, though scientists still debate how new the idea was.
5. **The rooks and the tube** (A true thing about science). Seen: an old fable tells of a thirsty crow dropping pebbles into a jug. Test (2009): rooks got a floating worm by dropping in stones. They picked bigger stones and stopped when the worm was in reach.
6. **The meerkat lessons** (A true thing about science). Seen: older meerkats give pups scorpions. Test (2006): helpers gave younger pups dead or stingless scorpions, then live ones as pups grew. Pups that practiced learned faster. Shows: teaching.
7. **The octopus and the jar** (A true thing about science). Seen: octopuses in aquariums unscrew lids to reach a crab. Shows: problem-solving with eight bendy arms.
8. **Elephants and their dead** (Keeper's Desk). Seen: elephants stop at elephant bones, touch them with trunks and feet, and stay a while. Simpler ideas: curiosity, or learning who is gone. Shows: nobody knows yet if this is grief.
**Kinds of clever** panel: Put each animal on its own shelf: **makes tools** · **solves puzzles** · **knows itself** · **teaches**. Reply if an animal is put on a "smartest" ladder: There is no ladder here. Each animal is clever in its own way.
Fable card (A story people told): Aesop's fable says a thirsty crow dropped pebbles into a jug until the water rose. In 2009, rooks really did it, in a test.

## Activity (f): Ocean Year

Opening: Turn the month dial. Where is the whale? Why?
Panels: **Humpback year** · **Sea turtle life**
Month dial: twelve months, January to December. Map spots: **cold north, feeding** · **open ocean, traveling** · **warm south, calves**.
Food-bloom layer: In summer, the cold north turns green with tiny plants, and krill swarm. In winter, the bloom fades.
Guided first run (3 steps): (1) Set the dial to July. (2) Where is the food? (3) Put the whale there.
Results (A made-up model: one North Pacific humpback's year, simplified):
- June to September: Feeding in the cold north. Storing blubber.
- October to December: Swimming south, thousands of miles.
- January to March: In warm water. Calves are born. Stored blubber carries the whale through the winter.
- April and May: Swimming north again.
Model line (A made-up model): Real whales leave at different times, and not every whale goes south every year. This dial shows one simple year.
Sea turtle panel: (1) A hatchling crawls to the sea. (2) She drifts and swims for years. (3) She grows up far away. (4) She returns to nest on the beach where she hatched, or very near it.
Turtle note (A true thing about science): Sea turtles seem to find home by Earth's magnetic field, which is a little different on every stretch of coast.
Explain prompt (say why, to a grown-up): Why does the whale leave all that food in winter?

## History cards

**Herodotus and the crocodile bird (A story people told).** About 2,450 years ago, the Greek writer Herodotus wrote that a little bird in Egypt walked into crocodiles' open mouths to eat the leeches inside. Nobody has ever photographed a bird doing this. A good story, not good evidence.

**The ship-holding fish (A story people told).** About 2,000 years ago, the Roman writer Pliny wrote that a small sucking fish could stop a warship by holding on. Its Latin name, *remora*, means delay. Real remoras ride big animals and ships. They can't stop them.

**De Bary names symbiosis (A true thing from history).** In 1879, the German scientist Anton de Bary used the word *symbiosis*, "living together", for different kinds of living things that share a life, helpful or not.

**Clever Hans (A true thing from history).** In Berlin, a horse named Hans seemed to do sums. In a 1907 book, Oskar Pfungst showed he was reading the asker's tiny movements. Scientists still use his lesson: look for the simpler explanation first.

**A fishing stick at Gombe (A true thing from history).** In 1960, at Gombe in Tanzania, Jane Goodall watched a chimpanzee she called David Greybeard poke grass stems into a termite mound and pull out termites. People had thought only humans made tools.

**The mirror test (A true thing from history).** In 1970, Gordon Gallup marked chimpanzees with red dye where they couldn't see it. With a mirror, they touched the marks on their own faces. The mark test began there.

**The turtles of Tortuguero (A true thing from history).** From 1955, Archie Carr and his team put tags on female green turtles nesting at Tortuguero, Costa Rica. More than a thousand came back and were seen nesting there again. In all those years, none was found nesting anywhere else.

## Problem sets

Right answers are spread across A, B and C. *Say why* items are spoken to a grown-up and never stored. Sprout items use no numbers.

### Set 1. Record holders (Sprout 1.1-1.6)

**1.1 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which is the biggest animal that ever lived?
- A. A giant dinosaur *(misconception: dinosaur-biggest)*
- B. The blue whale *(right)*
- C. The elephant *(misconception: biggest-is-on-land)*
Hint: It lives in the sea.
Feedback: A: No dinosaur yet found was as heavy. B: Yes. Bigger than any dinosaur. C: The elephant holds the record on land only.
Explanation: No animal we know of, ever, was bigger than the blue whale.

**1.2 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which animal runs fastest on land?
- A. The cheetah *(right)*
- B. The giraffe *(misconception: one-animal-all-records, tallest is fastest)*
- C. The elephant *(misconception: biggest is fastest)*
Hint: Think of a spotted cat.
Feedback: A: Yes. Faster than a car on a town street. B: The giraffe holds the tallest record. C: Big doesn't mean fast.
Explanation: The cheetah is the fastest runner on land, for a short dash.

**1.3 (Sprout, order the cards)**
Prompt: Put them in order. Tallest first.
Cards: horse · giraffe · bee hummingbird · elephant
Answer: giraffe → elephant → horse → bee hummingbird.
Misconception tested: biggest and tallest as one record.
Hint: Who can look in an upstairs window?
Explanation: The giraffe is the tallest. The tiny hummingbird comes last.

## "I'm stuck" worked examples (step 2 of the ladder)

- **Records:** Which is taller, a giraffe or a horse? Start: which one could look in an upstairs window?
- **Kind of most:** The elephant is the biggest on land. Is it the fastest too? Start: one record each.
- **Ocean key:** A crab. Backbone? Start: its hard part is outside, like a shell. So, no backbone.
- **Groups:** Three izards, each looking up 2 minutes. Start: 3 × 2 = 6 minutes watched.
- **Argument:** Claim: herds help. Start: find a readout that shows it.
- **Partners:** A flea on a fox. Start: does the flea gain? Does the fox lose?
- **Evidence:** A dog "smiles". Start: what else could the face mean?
- **Migration:** A whale swims 600 km in 6 days. Start: 600 ÷ 6 = 100 km a day.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Record:** the most of something: biggest, tallest, fastest or smallest.
- **Backbone:** a row of bones down the middle of an animal's back.
- **Invertebrate:** an animal with no backbone.
- **Gills:** body parts a fish uses to breathe in water.
- **Marine mammal:** a sea animal that breathes air and feeds its babies milk.
- **Key:** a set of yes-or-no questions that sorts things into groups.
- **Reptile:** a backboned animal with scaly skin that breathes air, like a turtle.
- **Izard:** the wild goat-antelope of the Pyrenees, also called the Pyrenean chamois.
- **Herd, pack, colony:** groups of animals of one kind that live together.
- **Lookout:** an animal that watches for danger while others eat.
- **Claim:** what you think is true.
- **Evidence:** what was seen or measured.
- **Reasoning:** how the evidence supports the claim.
- **Symbiosis:** two different kinds of living things living closely together for a long time.
- **Mutualism:** both partners gain.
- **Commensalism:** one partner gains; the other has no clear change.
- **Parasitism:** one partner gains; the other loses.
- **Parasite:** the partner that gains by taking from its host.
- **Host:** the partner a parasite lives on or inside.
- **Predation:** one animal catching and eating another.
- **Aphid:** a tiny insect that sucks sap from plants.
- **Honeydew:** the sweet, sticky drop an aphid gives off.
- **Anemone:** a soft sea animal with stinging arms.
- **Remora:** a fish with a sucker on its head that rides on big animals.
- **Mistletoe:** a plant that grows on tree branches and takes water from them.
- **Self-recognition:** knowing that a reflection is yourself.
- **Mirror mark test:** checking if an animal uses a mirror to find a mark on itself.
- **Instinct:** something an animal does without having to learn it.
- **Migration:** a long trip animals make, there and back, at the same time each year.
- **Krill:** tiny shrimp-like animals that whales eat.
- **Blubber:** a thick layer of fat that stores energy and keeps a whale warm.
- **Calf:** a baby whale.
- **Hatchling:** a baby animal just out of its egg, like a young sea turtle.
- **Magnetic field:** the invisible pull that makes a compass point north.
- **Tag:** a small numbered label scientists fix to an animal to know it again.

## Off-screen invitations (grown-up-led)

1. **Pace a whale.** A grown-up paces out about 30 big steps on a path or a park. Stand at one end and look along it. That's about one big blue whale. (The grown-up chooses a safe, open place.)
2. **Ant watch.** On a garden plant or a park weed, look for ants among tiny aphids. Watch, but don't touch. What do the ants do? (A grown-up picks the plant and keeps hands off.)
3. **Same kind of most.** Sort a few toy animals into biggest, tallest, fastest and smallest. Which toy holds two records? (Toys only.)

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** animal record-holders, stated precisely and set beside familiar objects; classifying ocean animals with a three-question key (backbone, gills, milk), including invertebrates; an argument, with claim, evidence and reasoning, that groups help each animal, with a cost weighed; symbiosis as mutualism, commensalism or parasitism, worked out from what each partner gains or loses; how scientists test what animals can do, and why one result can be overturned; and ocean migrations as round trips between food and safe places for young.

**What it does not claim:** the Lookout Meadow and Ocean Year benches are made-up models with stated rules, not field data. The mirror test shows one ability, not intelligence in general, and some results (magpies, elephants) are not settled. The bench treats commensalism as "no clear change", because small effects are hard to measure. Nothing here is advice about ticks, wild animals or pets.

**Wild-animal line:** Wild animals are for watching from a distance. Off screen, a grown-up leads, and nobody touches or picks up a wild animal.

**Where Willowbank ends and the world begins:** the izards, the whales off the coast and the garden aphids appear in the story; the facts on the cards are about the real animals.
