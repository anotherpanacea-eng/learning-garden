# Whose Parcel?: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 256](../specs/256-whose-parcel.md). Use as written, after review. `{guide}` and `{courier}` are never gendered and take no pronouns here. Pronouns: Pip he/him; Dot, the orchard keeper, the miller, Nuria she/her; the baker, Hob, Léon he/him; Puff takes none. Sources: [audit](../source-audits/256-whose-parcel.md). Nothing is scored, timed or locked.

**No pages, no Hollis:** no line reads a page or names Hollis or the old Keeper. The parcel appears only as "the parcel for the Keeper of the Ways", and only its outside label is read.

**Labels used throughout:**
- **A story from Willowbank** marks the town, its people, the market and the post office (fiction).
- **A true thing about language** marks real facts about how English, Spanish and French are written.
- **A made-up model** is not used in this pack: every label, note and sign is a story prop, marked as Willowbank fiction.

**The mark itself:** child text calls ' "an apostrophe" or "the mark". Build shows one glyph throughout (the typographic ’ in text and on the tile), and read-aloud never speaks the mark's name inside a word.

## Arriving

**Place title:** Whose Parcel?

**First visit** (A story from Willowbank):
> Dot's parcel shelf runs the whole length of the post office wall. Every parcel has a label. Some labels have a tiny mark up high. Some don't.
>
> "Every mark means something," says Dot. "So does every missing one."

**What you already know (recall card, links to `#post/letters`):** You've read Dot's letters. Now look closely at her labels.

**Every bench screen (always shown):** The apostrophe is a small mark with a big job. Try any tile. Nothing here is scored.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. Ill or I'll? (Act I, deliver and explain)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** Dot pins a note to the post office door before her first round. Pip reads it and panics.

**Short:**
> Dot's note says ILL BE BACK. Pip thinks Dot is ill. She only left out the apostrophe. I'll means I will.

**Longer:**
> Dot pins a note to the post office door and hurries off with her first sack of letters.
>
> Pip reads it out. "ILL BE BACK."
>
> His ears go flat. "Dot's ill! She's out delivering, and she's ill!"
>
> {guide} looks closer. "There's a tiny gap between the I and the L."
>
> Dot comes back at a trot for a forgotten parcel. "I'm not ill. My pen ran dry."
>
> She adds a small mark in the gap: I'LL BE BACK.
>
> "That mark means some letters dropped out," says Dot. "I'll is I will, squeezed up."
>
> Pip breathes out. "Then write all the letters next time."
>
> "Short notes are quicker," says Dot. "The mark does the job."
>
> "I'll remember," says Pip. He thinks about it. "I will remember."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> In a short word like I'll, the apostrophe shows where letters were left out. Without it, some short words turn into other words: ill, well, shell, were.

### Scene 2. The label with no apostrophe (Act I, deliver and explain)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** Pip carries the parcel for the Keeper of the Ways to every door. At the post office, he wants to add a mark to its label.

**Short:**
> Pip thinks Ways needs an apostrophe because it ends in s. Dot says no. Ways just means more than one way. Nothing belongs to it.

**Longer:**
> Pip sets the parcel for the Keeper of the Ways on Dot's counter. The label is smudged, but clear: To the Keeper of the Ways, Willowbank, by Willow Brook.
>
> "It's missing a mark," says Pip. "Ways ends in s. Dot's hat has a mark. So Ways needs one."
>
> Dot tips her hat back. "Dot's hat has a mark because the hat is mine."
>
> "And the Ways?"
>
> "Ways is just more than one way. Nothing belongs to it. No mark."
>
> Pip counts on his paws. "One way. Two ways. Lots of ways."
>
> "And we never write on somebody else's label," says Dot.
>
> Pip tucks it back into his satchel. "Then I'll keep looking for the Keeper."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> Of can show who something goes with, just like 's does: the door of the mill, or the mill's door. This label uses of: the Keeper of the Ways. Both ways are right.

### Scene 3. Fresh apple's (Act II, the weekly market)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** The weekly market is back. The orchard keeper's new sign says FRESH APPLE'S. Pip waits to find out what the apple owns.

**Short:**
> The sign says APPLE'S. Pip asks what the apple owns. Nothing: the sign just means many apples. The orchard keeper rubs out the mark.

**Longer:**
> Stalls fill the square again. The orchard keeper chalks a sign: FRESH APPLE'S.
>
> Pip reads it twice. "Fresh apple's what?"
>
> "Apples," says the orchard keeper. "Just apples."
>
> "But the mark says something belongs to the apple. Its stalk? Its worm?"
>
> "There's no worm." She looks at her chalk. "Ah. Lots of apples. Nothing belongs to them." She rubs out the mark: FRESH APPLES.
>
> Dot wheels the post cart past. "Morning! The baker's flour, the hens' corn and the miller's new broom."
>
> "The hens' corn," says Pip. "Mark after the s."
>
> "Five hens," says Dot. "One sack of corn for all of them."
>
> Pip buys an apple. He checks it for worms. None.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> Shop signs sometimes put an apostrophe in a word that only means more than one. It's an easy mistake, and grown-ups make it too. Ask: does anything belong?

### Scene 4. Parcels up and down the river (Act III, letters up and down the river)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** {courier} packs two parcels: one up the river to Vadoalto, one down to Les Saules. Dot writes the labels.

**Short:**
> Dot labels Nuria's parcel and Léon's parcel. Nuria's Spanish shows belonging with de, meaning of. Léon's French uses the mark where a letter drops out.

**Longer:**
> Two parcels sit on Dot's counter. One goes up the river to Vadoalto. One goes down to Les Saules.
>
> Dot writes the labels: NURIA'S SEEDS. LÉON'S ROPE.
>
> {courier} reads them back. "One owner each. Mark before the s."
>
> Nuria's last letter lies open beside them. At the top she has written *la carta de Nuria*, and underneath, in English: Nuria's letter.
>
> "No mark in her Spanish," says Pip.
>
> "De means of," says {guide}. "The letter of Nuria."
>
> Léon's note is pinned up too: *J'arrive !* Underneath: I'm coming!
>
> "French has the mark," says Pip. "Where a letter drops out."
>
> "Just like I'm," says Dot. She stamps both parcels. "Both towns' parcels, ready."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> Spanish and French don't use 's to show belonging. Both use de, which means of: la carta de Nuria, la lettre de Léon. French does use the apostrophe where a vowel drops out: je and arrive become j'arrive.

*Language review:* the Spanish and French lines in scene 4 (*la carta de Nuria*, *J'arrive !*, *la lettre de Léon*, *je*, *j'arrive*) each sit beside English and need a fluent reader. French typography puts a thin space before ! (shown here); the reviewer may prefer it closed up in a child's handwritten note.

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer; worked-example steps appear one tap at a time. Every card is **A true thing about language**. Cards 1-5 are Sprout and Seedling; 6-10 Seedling and Sapling; 11-12 Sapling.

**Card 1. Two words, one short word**
> Sometimes two words squeeze into one short word. Some letters drop out. An apostrophe, a small mark written up high, sits where they were.

Worked example: *do not.*
1. Write do not.
2. The o in not drops out.
3. The apostrophe takes its place: don't.

**Card 2. Read it the long way**
> When you read a word with an apostrophe, try saying the two long words. I'm means I am. We'll means we will.

Worked example: *Dot's door note: I'll be back.*
1. Find the mark.
2. Say it long: I will be back.
3. So Dot is coming back.

**Card 3. The mark changes the word**
> Take away its apostrophe, and a short word can turn into a different word. Ill means sick. I'll means I will.

Worked example: *well and we'll.*
1. Well done! means good work.
2. We'll go means we will go.
3. Same letters. The mark makes the difference.

**Card 4. Spell the short way**
> To write a short word, write both words. Take out the letters that drop. Put the apostrophe where they were, not where the words meet.

Worked example: *did not.*
1. Write did not.
2. Take out the o.
3. Put the mark where the o was: didn't. Not did'nt.

**Card 5. Two odd ones**
> Can't comes from cannot, which is already one word. Won't means will not, though the letters don't match. Learn these two by heart.

Worked example: *Say them long.*
1. cannot: take out n and o. can't.
2. will not: won't.
3. I can't lift it means I cannot lift it.

**Card 6. Belongs to one**
> To show that something belongs to one person or animal, add an apostrophe and s: Dot's hat, the hen's egg.

Worked example: *A parcel for the baker.*
1. Who is it for? The baker.
2. How many bakers? One.
3. baker + 's: the baker's parcel.

**Card 7. Just more than one**
> An s that only means more than one needs no apostrophe. One parcel, two parcels. Not every s needs a mark.

Worked example: *The label on the parcel for the Keeper of the Ways.*
1. Find a word that ends in s: Ways.
2. Does anything belong to Ways? No.
3. Ways just means more than one way. No mark.

**Card 8. Belongs to many**
> When something belongs to more than one, write the word for many first. If it ends in s, add just the apostrophe: the hens' corn.

Worked example: *one girl, many girls.*
1. One girl: the girl's bag.
2. Many girls: write girls.
3. Add the mark after the s: the girls' bags.

**Card 9. Many without an s**
> Some words mean many but don't end in s: children, mice, sheep. To show belonging, add an apostrophe and s: the children's shelf.

Worked example: *Cheese for the mice.*
1. Many mice.
2. Mice doesn't end in s.
3. Add 's: the mice's cheese.

**Card 10. Same sound, three spellings**
> Hens, hen's and hens' sound the same. Only the writing shows the difference. Ask: just more than one? Belongs to one? Belongs to many?

Worked example: *Three labels at the coop.*
1. Hens on the gate: just more than one.
2. The hen's egg: one hen.
3. The hens' corn: many hens.

**Card 11. Say it long: it's and its**
> It's always means it is or it has. Its, with no apostrophe, means belonging to it, like his or hers.

Worked example: *Two sentences.*
1. It's raining. Say it long: it is raining. Works, so it's.
2. The parcel lost its string. It is string? No, so its.
3. Who's and whose work the same way.

**Card 12. Two jobs for 's**
> 's can be short for is, or it can show belonging. Say it long. Dot is here makes sense. Dot is hat doesn't.

Worked example: *Dot's here and Dot's hat.*
1. Dot's here: Dot is here. Short for is.
2. Dot's hat: Dot is hat? No. Belonging.
3. Read the whole sentence before you choose.

**Keeper's Desk card. Names that end in s (A true thing about language)**
> Some names end in s, like James. Many style guides add 's: James's hat. Many newspapers add just the mark: James' hat. Both are seen.

No problem item asks for this choice.

**Header:** Say it long. If the long words make sense, the mark stands for missing letters.

## Problem sets

Every item has the "I'm stuck" ladder. Choices appear in the order written; ✔ marks the right one. Tags in backticks name the misconception a wrong choice tests.

### Set A. Long and short (Sprout, Seedling)

**A1 (Sprout, guided).** Steps shown first: 1. Find the mark. 2. Say the two long words. 3. Pick the match.
- Prompt: Dot's note says I'm at the mill. What does I'm mean?
- Choices: I am ✔ · I made (`wrong-letters-missing`) · I mean (`wrong-letters-missing`)
- Feedback: I made: I'm has no d or e. The mark stands for one letter. · I mean: I'm has no e or n. Say it long: I am.
- Hint: The mark sits where a letter dropped out.
- Explanation: I'm means I am. The apostrophe takes the place of the a.

**A2 (Sprout, match).**
- Prompt: Match each short word to its long words.
- Pairs: we'll -> we will · don't -> do not · can't -> cannot · it's -> it is
- Feedback for a wrong pair: Say both out loud. Do they mean the same?
- Hint: Look at the letters before the mark.
- Explanation: Each short word means just the same as its long form.

**A3 (Sprout).**
- Prompt: Which note says Dot will come back?
- Choices: Ill be back. (`apostrophe-optional`) · I'll be back. ✔ · Il'l be back. (`apostrophe-anywhere`)
- Feedback: Ill: Ill means sick. Without the mark, it's a different word. · Il'l: The mark goes after I, where w and i dropped out.
- Hint: Which one could mean I will?
- Explanation: I'll means I will. The apostrophe turns ill into I'll.

**A4 (Seedling, tiles, guided).** Steps shown first: 1. Write did not. 2. Take out the o. 3. Put the mark in its place.
- Prompt: Build the short way to write did not.
- Tiles: d · i · d · n · o · t · apostrophe
- Answer: didn't.
- Feedback for didnt (`apostrophe-optional`): A letter dropped out. Show where.
- Feedback for did'nt (`apostrophe-at-join`): The mark goes where the o was, not where the words meet.
- Hint: Which letter of not drops out?
- Explanation: did + not, take out the o, add the mark: didn't.

**A5 (Seedling).**
- Prompt: Which is the right spelling for do not?
- Choices: do'nt (`apostrophe-at-join`) · dont (`apostrophe-optional`) · don't ✔
- Feedback: do'nt: The o dropped out of not. Put the mark there. · dont: A letter is missing. The mark shows where.
- Hint: Write do not. Which letter goes?
- Explanation: do not, take out the o: don't.

**A6 (Seedling).**
- Prompt: In she'll, which letters does the apostrophe stand for?
- Choices: w and i ✔ · l and l (`wrong-letters-missing`) · s and h (`wrong-letters-missing`)
- Feedback: l and l: Both l letters are still there. Look again. · s and h: She is still there at the start.
- Hint: Write she will. Which letters are gone?
- Explanation: she will, take out w and i: she'll.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Apostrophe:** a small mark written up high, like a comma in the air. It shows missing letters or belonging.
- **Contraction:** a short word made from two words, with an apostrophe where letters dropped out. Don't is a contraction.
- **Plural:** a word that means more than one: parcels, hens, children.
- **Singular:** a word that means just one: parcel, hen, child.
- **Possessive:** a word that shows something belongs to someone or goes with them: Dot's hat, the hens' corn.
- **Label:** words on a parcel that say who it is for.
- **Owner:** the one something belongs to.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** reading contractions as two words (I'm, we'll, don't); spelling them with the apostrophe where the letters were; the apostrophe for one owner (the baker's flour); for many owners, after a plural s (the hens' corn) or with 's after a plural without s (the children's shelf); and telling a plain plural (apples) from a possessive. Sapling and Keeper's Desk add it's and its, who's and whose, and 's that means is.

**England's terms:** the national curriculum calls these "contracted forms", "the possessive apostrophe" and, in Year 4, "the grammatical difference between plural and possessive -s". Child text says "short word", "belongs to" and "more than one" first, then the terms.

**Names ending in s:** style guides differ. Many add 's (James's); many newspapers add only the apostrophe (James'). Both are seen, so no item asks for one. England's guidance uses 's (Cyprus's).

**The stray apostrophe:** an apostrophe in a plain plural (APPLE'S) is sometimes called "the greengrocer's apostrophe", after market signs. Pip's scene keeps it friendly.

**It's and its:** it's is always it is or it has; its, like his and hers, never takes an apostrophe. The "say it long" test works for who's and whose too.

**What it does not claim:** every Willowbank label, note and sign is invented. Plurals of single letters and numbers are left out, because style guides differ. Possessive pronouns (mine, hers, theirs) are taught in Which Hat? (pack 262), and other sound-alike words in pack 268.
