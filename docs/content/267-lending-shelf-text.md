# The Lending Shelf: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 267](../specs/267-lending-shelf.md). Use as written, after review. `{guide}` is never gendered. Pronouns: Pip he/him; Dot, Bramble, the miller, the orchard keeper, Wren Halloway she/her; Hob Pennick and the baker he/him; the librarian and Hollis they/them. Ember, Puff and the apprentice take no pronouns. Sources: [audit](../source-audits/267-lending-shelf.md). Nothing is scored, timed or locked.

**Reading-record rule:** no screen asks how many books, which books were finished, how long anyone read, or how many days in a row. There is no reading log, tick, count or streak anywhere. Book talk is spoken and nothing is stored. The Lantern Night list is not stored either.

**Labels used throughout:**
- **A story from Willowbank** marks the town, its people and the books they wrote.
- **A true thing from history** marks real books, their authors and their dates.
- **A true thing about language** marks how book talk, recommending and comparing work.

**Every item shows what it asks about:** the book cards it names, or both stories in full, with read-aloud. No item needs a book read somewhere else.

## The two stories (A story from Willowbank)

Both are shown in full, side by side, with read-aloud, on every bench and item that asks about them.

### Fern and the Lost Bell (words by the miller)

> Fern was new on the millpond. The other ducks swam in a line, and the line never waited for her.
>
> One windy evening, the mill bell slipped off its hook. Plop. It sank in the deepest part of the pond.
>
> "Too deep," said the oldest duck. "Nobody dives there."
>
> Fern looked down at the dark water. Her feet felt cold. Nobody was watching her. Nobody would see if she turned back.
>
> She took a big breath and dived.
>
> Down through the green. Down through the weeds. Something glinted. Fern grabbed the bell's rope in her beak and kicked hard for the top.
>
> When she came up, the whole pond was quacking and cheering.
>
> That night, Fern slept in the middle of the flock, not at the edge.
>
> Being brave, she thought, is how you stop being new.

Words to know: *glinted* means gave a small flash of light.

### Bea Finds the Way (words by the baker)

> Bea had never seen so many legs. It was market day, her first day in town, and everyone was in a hurry.
>
> She had a letter to post. But where was the post office?
>
> She tried to ask a rabbit, but the rabbit hopped past. She tried a hedgehog, but she spoke too quietly.
>
> By the fountain sat a snail, holding a map upside down.
>
> "Excuse me," said Bea. "Do you know the way to the post office?"
>
> "No," said the snail. "I only got here this morning. I hoped you knew."
>
> They turned the map the right way up. They went together, slowly, because snails are slow. Left at the bakery. Right at the bridge. And there was the post office.
>
> The town was still big. Bea still didn't know most of it.
>
> But tomorrow, she would know one snail.

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer; worked-example steps appear one tap at a time. All are **A true thing about language**.

**Card 1. Book talk takes turns**
> In book talk, one person speaks at a time. Everyone else listens to the end. Then the next person takes a turn.

Worked example: *Lantern Night.*
1. Dot says why she picked her book.
2. Pip waits until she stops.
3. Then Pip takes his turn.

**Card 2. Listen, then build**
> Before you add your idea, say back the last speaker's idea in a few words. Then add yours, with a reason from the book.

Worked example: *Dot says Fern is brave.*
1. Say it back: "Dot thinks Fern is brave."
2. Add yours: "Yes, and she dives where nobody else will."
3. Your reason comes from the story.

**Card 3. Two views can both be welcome**
> Two readers can see one book differently. Say what you think, kindly, and point to the part of the book that made you think it.

Worked example: *Is Bea's ending happy?*
1. Bramble: "It's sad. She still doesn't know most of the town."
2. Pip: "It's happy. She found the post office."
3. Both point to the story. Both are book talk.

**Card 4. A recommendation fits the reader**
> To recommend a book is to tell someone it suits them, and why. Start with what they like, not with what you like.

Worked example: *Ember likes true books about animals.*
1. What does Ember like? True books. Animals.
2. Look for a card that says both.
3. The Busy Hive: a fact book about bees.

**Card 5. A reason points to the book**
> "It's good" is not a reason. A reason names something in the book: its kind, a character, where it happens, or what happens.

Worked example: *Why The Busy Hive?*
1. Not a reason: "It's good."
2. A reason: "It's true, and it's about bees."
3. A strong reason links it to Ember: "You like true animal books."

**Card 6. If you liked this, try that**
> You can recommend by comparing: "You liked this book. Try that one. Both have the thing you liked."

Worked example: *Dot liked Fern's story because Fern is new.*
1. What did Dot like? Someone new.
2. Which other card has someone new? Bea Finds the Way.
3. "Try Bea. Both are about someone new."

**Card 7. Both, but**
> To compare two books, find one way they are alike and one way they differ. Say it with "both" and "but".

Worked example: *Swallows Come Home and The Busy Hive.*
1. Alike: both are fact books about animals.
2. Different: one is about birds, one about bees.
3. "Both are fact books about animals, but one is about birds and one about bees."

**Card 8. Same topic, different theme**
> A topic is what a story is about, like being new. A theme is what the story says about it. Two stories can share a topic.

Worked example: *Fern and Bea.*
1. Topic of both: being new.
2. Fern's story says something about it.
3. Bea's story says something different.

**Card 9. Three questions about a story's idea**
> To see how an author treats an idea, ask: What is the problem? What does the character do? How does it end?

Worked example: *Pip and the Longest Nap.*
1. Problem: a short nap that turns out long.
2. What Pip does: wakes up and sees snow on the window.
3. Where the sample page ends: "That was a long one."

**Card 10. Read wide**
> Reading wide means trying many kinds: stories, poems, plays, true books, and books from other times and places.

Worked example: *A winter list.*
1. A story: Heidi.
2. A poem book: A Child's Garden of Verses.
3. A play, a true book, and one from far away make it wide.

**Card 11. A stretch book**
> A stretch book is a bit harder than you usually read: older words, more pages, or a new kind. Reading it with someone is fine.

Worked example: *Twelfth Night.*
1. Its words are over 400 years old.
2. It's a play, which may be a new kind.
3. Read it with a grown-up, a scene at a time.

**Card 12. Taste, with a reason**
> Your reading taste is the kind of book you like. Say it with a reason: what you like, and where a book has it.

Worked example: *Dot's taste.*
1. Dot likes books about animals that fly.
2. Swallows Come Home has swallows.
3. "I like it because the swallows fly so far."

## Benches

### (a) Book Talk (every depth; spoken; nothing stored)

**Header:** Talk about a book with a grown-up or someone at home. Take turns.

**Choose a book:** Any book you both know. Or pick one from the shelf.

**Pass the lantern:** Tap to pass it. The lantern shows whose turn it is. It counts nothing and remembers nothing.
- Lantern on your side: Your turn.
- Lantern on their side: Their turn. Listen to the end.

**Talk starters (tap one to see it):**
- My favorite part was ... because ...
- I wondered why ...
- A character I'd like to meet is ... because ...
- I see it a different way. In the book, ...
- You said ... Yes, and ...

**Listening card:** When they finish, say back their idea in a few words. Then add yours.

**Grown-up line:** Nothing here is recorded or saved. Let your child finish before you speak, and give a reason from the book when you disagree.

**Slots:**

### Set A. Book talk (Sprout, Seedling)

**A1 (Sprout, guided).** Steps shown first: 1. Dot is talking. 2. Has she finished? 3. What do good listeners do?
- Prompt: Dot is telling everyone about her book. What do you do?
- Choices: Talk over her so you get a turn (`talk-over`) · Listen until she finishes ✔ · Start reading a different book (`tune-out`)
- Feedback: Talk over: Then nobody hears either of you. Wait for your turn. · Different book: Dot is talking to you. Listen first.
- Hint: How do you know when it's your turn?
- Explanation: In book talk, one person speaks at a time. Listening comes first.

**A2 (Sprout).** Shows Pip's book card and its sample page.
- Prompt: Pip says his favorite part is the snow. What could you say next?
- Choices: I liked the snow part too. It was a surprise. ✔ · Snow is boring. That part is boring too, Pip. (`dismiss`) · Is it nearly supper time? I'm very hungry. (`off-topic`)
- Feedback: Boring: You can disagree kindly, with a reason from the book. · Supper: That's a new topic. Stay with the book.
- Hint: Which answer talks about Pip's part?
- Explanation: Saying something about his part, then adding yours, keeps the talk going.

**A3 (Seedling).** Shows Bea's story.
- Prompt: Bramble thinks Bea's story ends sadly. You disagree. What do you say?
- Choices: You're wrong, Bramble. Everyone knows it ends happily. (`no-reason`) · I'll just say I agree with you, so we don't argue. (`hide-view`) · I see it differently. Bea finds the post office. ✔
- Feedback: Wrong: "Everyone knows" isn't a reason. Point to the story. · Agree: Your view counts too. Say it kindly, with a reason.
- Hint: Which answer points to something in the story?
- Explanation: A different view is welcome. Give it kindly, with a part of the story.

**A4 (Seedling).** Shows Fern's story.

- Hint: Which reply starts with Dot's idea and adds to it?
- Explanation: Building means adding to the last idea: "Yes, and..."

- Grown-up guide: Listen to the end. Say back what your child said. Then add your own idea with a reason. There is no right answer and nothing is saved.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Book talk:** talking about a book with others, taking turns.
- **Recommend:** to tell someone a book suits them, and why.
- **Reason:** what makes you think something. A book reason points to the book.
- **Kind of book (genre):** a group of books that work alike, such as stories, poems, plays or fact books.
- **Novel:** a long story told in chapters.
- **Play:** a story written to be acted, with the words each person says.
- **Poem:** writing in short lines, often with rhythm or rhyme.
- **Fact book (true book):** a book about real things.
- **Fable:** a short story, often with animals, that has a message.
- **Character:** a person or animal in a story.
- **Setting:** where and when a story happens.
- **Compare:** to look for how two things are alike and how they differ.
- **Topic:** what a story is about, in a word or two.
- **Theme:** what a story says about its topic.
- **Stretch book:** a book a bit harder than you usually read.
- **Taste:** the kinds of books you like to read.
- **Read wide:** to read many kinds of books, from many times and places.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** taking turns and listening in talk about books; building on someone's idea and disagreeing kindly with a reason; recommending a book to fit a reader, with a reason from the book; comparing two books with "both" and "but"; comparing how two stories treat one topic (their problem, what the character does, the ending, and what each says); and, for older readers, choosing widely across kinds, times and places, with stretch books and a sense of their own taste.

**What it never records:** how much your child reads. School resources often suggest reading logs and records. This page leaves them out on purpose: a reading life belongs to the reader. Book talk is spoken, and nothing about it is saved. The winter list is not saved at all; write it down if you want to keep it.

**About the old books:** every old book named here is in the public domain in its original language: its author died long ago and it was first published long ago. For *Heidi* (German) and La Fontaine's *Fables* (French), any library translation will do; a modern translation may still be under copyright. Old books carry the attitudes of their time. In *A Child's Garden of Verses*, "Foreign Children" talks down to children from other countries and uses old names for some peoples, and "Travel" uses a racial word of its time; the card points to five other poems to start with. *The Wind in the Willows* has a few words and attitudes of 1908. Shakespeare's language is over 400 years old, so many families start a play together, a scene at a time. Please look at any book before you hand it over.

**Where the Willowbank books come from:** the fact books, Pip's book, the dragon book and the baker's poem live on the Library Shelves; the fables on the Fable Shelf; the river folders in the Travelers' Archive. *Fern and the Lost Bell* and *Bea Finds the Way* were written for this page.

**Why no modern books:** this page names only books whose original text is free for anyone to read and copy, plus the Garden's own. Your library's own shelves hold many more, chosen by people who know them.
