# Then, Now and Next: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 260](../specs/260-then-now-and-next.md). Use as written, after review. `{guide}` is never gendered. Pronouns: Pip he/him; Dot, the miller, Bramble, Nuria she/her; Hob he/him; the librarian they/them. Sources: [audit](../source-audits/260-then-now-and-next.md). Nothing is scored, timed or locked.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. So next time we'll know (Act II, a garden across the seasons)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** The librarian opens a new weather book. Pip grabs the pen first. He writes two lines very fast.

**Short:**
> Pip writes "Yesterday it rains" and "Tomorrow it rained." Dot can't tell which day was wet. The verb has to match the day.

**Longer:**
> The new book lies open on the library table. *Willowbank Weather*, says the cover, in the librarian's neat hand.
>
> "Me first," says Pip. He writes fast and turns the book around.
>
> *Yesterday it rains. Tomorrow it rained.*
>
> Dot reads it twice. "So which day was wet?"
>
> "Yesterday," says Pip. "Obviously."
>
> "It says tomorrow too."
>
> Pip looks at the page. The page looks back.
>
> "The day word says when," says {guide}. "The verb has to agree with it."
>
> Pip crosses out and writes again. *Yesterday it rained. Today it is sunny. Hob says it will rain tomorrow.*
>
> "Now I'd know," says Dot. "Next time."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> Most English verbs add an ending to show the past: rain, rained. English has no ending for the future. It puts a helper word in front instead: will rain.

### Scene 2. Pip's goed (Act II, a garden across the seasons)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** Pip writes about the windy night. Every verb ends in -ed. Some of them shouldn't.

**Short:**
> Pip writes "The wind blowed and the hen runned." Some verbs don't take -ed. Blew and ran are their past forms.

**Longer:**
> Pip reads his entry aloud, proudly.
>
> *Last night the wind blowed. A hen runned under the cart and hided. I eated two breakfasts.*
>
> "Two?" says Dot.
>
> "It was windy. Wind makes you hungry."
>
> "Blew," says {guide}. "Ran. Hid."
>
> "But walked works. Jumped works." Pip frowns at the page. "Why do some verbs get -ed and some don't?"
>
> "We say some verbs so often," says the librarian, "that they kept their old past forms."
>
> Pip fixes the lines. Then he stops at the last one.
>
> "Ate," says Dot. "And that's still two breakfasts."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> Long ago, the past of help was holp. Over hundreds of years it became helped. The verbs people use most, like go and be, kept their odd forms.

### Scene 3. Still raining (Act II, a garden across the seasons)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** Rain starts after lunch and doesn't stop. Pip falls asleep on the library cushions. Thunder wakes him.

**Short:**
> It was raining all afternoon. Pip was sleeping when the thunder cracked. Was raining and was sleeping show things that kept going.

**Longer:**
> Rain taps on the library roof after lunch. It is still tapping at three.
>
> Pip curls up on the cushions to wait. He means to wait with his eyes open.
>
> CRACK.
>
> Pip sits up so fast a cushion flies. "What happened?"
>
> "You were sleeping," says Dot, "when the thunder cracked."
>
> "I was resting."
>
> "You were snoring."
>
> Dot writes in the log: *It was raining all afternoon. Pip was sleeping when the thunder cracked.*
>
> "Why not just *it rained*?" asks Pip.
>
> "It rained is fine," says {guide}. "Was raining shows it kept going. Like your snoring."
>
> "Resting," says Pip.
>
> Outside, the rain is still falling. Dot writes that too: *It is raining now.*

### Scene 4. Has the brook come up? (Spring, Flood Boards and Planting)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** The miller and Hob slide the spring boards into the millrace. Pip runs to the flood post to look.

**Short:**
> The brook has risen to the first mark. It is high right now. Last spring it rose to the second mark. That spring is over.

**Longer:**
> The boards go in with a wet thunk. The miller wipes her hands.
>
> Pip is already at the flood post, nose to the painted marks.
>
> "Did it rise?" he shouts.
>
> "It has risen," says the miller. "See? The water is at the first mark."
>
> "When did it rise?"
>
> "Over the week. And it's still up there now."
>
> Hob points to a higher mark. "Last spring it rose to that one."
>
> "So which is it," says Pip, "rose or has risen?"
>
> "Last spring is over and done," says {guide}. "Rose. This week's water is still here. Has risen."
>
> Pip writes both in the log. Then he adds a line of his own: *The boards have gone in.*
>
> "They have," says the miller.

### Scene 5. A letter upstream (after the Act II ending; Act III, letters up and down the river)

Opens only after page 2 comes free.

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** The old Keeper's ledger lies beside the town's new log. At the bottom it says *Ask upstream.* Dot starts a letter to Nuria.

**Short:**
> Dot writes to Nuria about the old Keeper's ledger. One verb slips out of its time. The others change because the time changes.

**Longer:**
> The librarian lays the old Keeper's ledger beside the new weather log. Two hands, two books, one brook.
>
> At the bottom of the ledger: *Ask upstream.*
>
> "Then we ask," says Dot, and starts a letter to Nuria.
>
> *The old Keeper's ledger lists the floods at our mill. Back then, the floods come closer together. We have started a weather log. We will send you a page each month.*
>
> "You keep switching," says Pip. "Lists. Have started. Will send."
>
> "Three times, so three tenses," says Bramble, reading over Dot's shoulder. "That part's fine."
>
> Bramble is right. Dot changes one word only. The old floods are over: *came*.
>
> Two weeks later, Nuria's reply arrives. *Aquí ha llovido toda la semana.* Underneath, in English: *Here it has rained all week.*

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> Spanish can show the future with an ending: llueve (it rains), llovió (it rained), lloverá (it will rain). English uses the helper word will instead.

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer; worked-example steps appear one tap at a time. Every card is **A true thing about language**; the example sentences are **A story from Willowbank**.

**Card 1. Verbs tell when**
> A verb's form tells when something happens. It rained: then. It is raining: now. It will rain: next. This is called tense.

Worked example: *Written on Tuesday morning.*
1. Monday is before Tuesday: then. *It rained on Monday.*
2. Tuesday is the writing day: now. *The sun is out.*
3. Wednesday is after: next. *It will rain on Wednesday.*

**Card 2. Day words are clues**
> Yesterday, last night and last week point to then. Right now and at the moment point to now. Tomorrow and next week point to next.

Worked example: *Tomorrow the boards ___ in.*
1. Find the day word: tomorrow.
2. Tomorrow is next.
3. *Tomorrow the boards will go in.*

**Card 3. Most verbs add -ed**
> To show the past, most verbs add -ed: rain, rained; walk, walked; splash, splashed. These are called regular verbs.

Worked example: *Pip ___ in the puddle yesterday. (jump)*
1. Yesterday is then.
2. Jump is regular.
3. *Pip jumped in the puddle yesterday.*

Small line under the card: Spellings like stopped and hurried live at Word Garden's endings bed.

**Card 4. Odd ones out**
> Some verbs make the past a different way: go, went; run, ran; sit, sat; tell, told. These are called irregular verbs. You already know many.

Worked example: *The hen ___ under the cart. (hide)*
1. Try hide plus -ed: hided. Say it aloud.
2. It sounds wrong. The past of hide is hid.
3. *The hen hid under the cart.*

**Card 5. Odd families**
> Many odd verbs come in families: blow, blew; grow, grew. Sing, sang; drink, drank. Families help you remember, but not every look-alike joins: glow, glowed.

Worked example: *The wind ___ all night. (blow)*
1. Blow is in the grow family.
2. Grow, grew. So blow, blew.
3. *The wind blew all night.*

**Card 6. Still going**
> To show something going on for a while, use a form of be plus an -ing verb. It is raining. It was raining. It will be raining.

Worked example: *Look outside. Rain is falling right now.*
1. It is now, and still going.
2. Is plus raining.
3. *It is raining.*

**Card 7. Every day, or right now?**
> The plain present tells what usually happens: the miller opens the mill at six. Is opening tells what is happening right now.

Worked example: *These days, Dot ___ the post every morning. (sort)*
1. Every morning is a habit. These days means it still happens.
2. A habit that still happens takes the plain present.
3. *These days, Dot sorts the post every morning.*

**Card 8. A long action and a short one**
> When a short event cuts into a longer one, the longer one usually uses was or were plus -ing. The short one uses the plain past.

Worked example: *Pip ___ when the thunder ___. (sleep, crack)*
1. Which went on for a while? Sleeping.
2. Which happened in a moment? The crack.
3. *Pip was sleeping when the thunder cracked.*

**Card 9. Has happened, and matters now**
> Has or have plus a word like rained or gone tells that something happened before now, and it matters now. It has rained. The paths are still muddy.

Worked example: *The rain is over, but the paths are still muddy.*
1. The rain is over.
2. Its mud is here now.
3. *It has rained.*

**Card 10. Rained, or has rained?**
> Named, finished time, like yesterday or last spring? Use the plain past: it rained. A time reaching up to now, like so far? Use has rained.

Worked example: *The brook ___ last spring. This week it ___, and it's still high. (rise)*
1. Last spring is finished: *rose*.
2. This week reaches up to now: *has risen*.

**Card 11. The has form**
> Some odd verbs take a different form after has or have. Went, has gone. Rose, has risen. Blew, has blown. Saw, has seen.

Worked example: *The miller ___ to market. She isn't back yet. (go)*
1. She isn't back, so it matters now. Use has.
2. After has, go becomes gone.
3. *The miller has gone to market.*

**Card 12. Hold it steady**
> In one log entry about one time, keep the verbs in one tense. Change the tense when the time changes.

Worked example: *Last night the wind blew. The gate banged. A bucket rolls away.*
1. All three happened last night.
2. Rolls is for now. It slipped.
3. *A bucket rolled away.*

**Card 13. When the time really changes**
> A paragraph can move through time: last spring, this week, tomorrow. Each move needs its own tense. That kind of change is right.

Worked example: *Last spring the brook rose to the second mark. Now it is at the first. Tomorrow we will check again.*
1. Last spring: rose.
2. Now: is.
3. Tomorrow: will check. Three times, three tenses.

**Card 14. Which came first? (Keeper's Desk)**
> Had plus a has form, like gone, can show the earlier of two past events. The boards had gone in before the water came.

Worked example: *The boards went in. Then the water came.*
1. Which came first? The boards.
2. Use had for the earlier one.
3. *The boards had gone in before the water came.*

**Card 15. If and when (Keeper's Desk)**
> After if or when, use the plain present for a future event. If it rains tomorrow, the brook will rise. Not: if it will rain.

Worked example: *___ tomorrow, we will check the post. (rain)*
1. The rain would come tomorrow: next.
2. After if, use the plain present.
3. *If it rains tomorrow, we will check the post.*

## Benches

### (a) The Log Desk (Sprout, Seedling)

**Header:** Each entry says when it was written. Pick the verb that fits its time.

**On a pick:** the Log Line shows where that verb puts the weather: a dot, a long bar or an arrow.

**Sprout page (A story from Willowbank), written on Tuesday morning; tiles: rained · rains · will rain**
- Yesterday it ___ all day. (rained)
- Hob says it ___ tomorrow. (will rain)
- These days, it ___ a lot every spring. (rains)

**Seedling page (A story from Willowbank), written on Thursday at noon; tiles: rained · is raining · will rain · has rained; use each tile once**
- Look out the window. It ___ right now. (is raining)
- Last Monday it ___ all night. (rained)
- Since Monday it ___ twice. (has rained)
- Hob says it ___ again next Saturday. (will rain)

**Log Desk feedback:**
- Past clue, future tile: Yesterday is before the entry. Will rain is for later.
- Future clue, past tile: Tomorrow hasn't happened yet. Rained is for before.
- Habit clue, other tile: These days means it still happens every spring. Use the plain present: rains.
- Past clue, present tile: Yesterday is before the entry. Use the past: rained.
- Right now, past tile: It is still falling. Use is raining for right now.
- So far, past tile: So far reaches up to now. Use has rained.
- Last Monday, has rained tile: Last Monday is a finished time. Use rained.

### (b) Still Going (Seedling, Sapling)

**Header:** Build the verb: a form of be, then an -ing word.

**Builder (tiles: am · is · are · was · were · will be):**
- Right now the wind ___ blowing. (is)
- At three o'clock yesterday, rain ___ falling. (was)
- All afternoon tomorrow, we ___ planting. (will be)

**Every day, or right now?** Put each card on its side.
- The brook runs past the mill. (every day)
- The brook is running fast today. (right now)
- Dot sorts the post each morning. (every day)
- Dot is sorting a big parcel now. (right now)

**Cut in:** Tap the long action, then the short one that cut in.
- Pip was sleeping · the thunder cracked
- Dot was writing · the lamp went out

**Still Going feedback:**
- Missing helper: An -ing verb needs a helper: is, was or will be.
- Was for now: Was is for before. This is happening now.
- Habit on the right-now side: Each morning means usually. The plain present tells that.
- Short action picked as long: A crack takes a moment. Which one went on for a while?

### (c) Has It Rained? (Sapling)

**Header:** Is the time named and finished? Or does it reach up to now?

**Clue trays:** Put each clue on the rained tray or the has rained tray.
- yesterday (rained) · last spring (rained) · two days ago (rained)
- so far (has rained) · since Monday (has rained) · up to now (has rained)

**The has shelf:** Match each past to its has form.
- went · gone
- rose · risen
- blew · blown
- saw · seen
- fell · fallen
- grew · grown

**Where is she now?** Read: *The miller has gone to market.* Tap what we know.

**Has It Rained feedback:**
- Finished time on the has tray: That time is named and over. Use the plain past.
- Up-to-now clue on the rained tray: That clue reaches up to now. Use has.
- Wrong shelf pair: Say it after has: it has ___. Which sounds right?

### (d) The Odd Shelf (Sprout, Seedling)

**Header:** Some verbs don't take -ed. Say each one aloud, then pick its past.

**Mend Pip's entry (Sprout):** *Last night the wind blowed. A hen runned under the cart and hided. I eated two breakfasts.*
- blowed → blew
- runned → ran
- hided → hid
- eated → ate

**Families (Seedling):** Put each past on its family shelf.
- Grow family: blew · grew · threw · flew · knew
- Sing family: sang · rang · drank · swam · began
- Thought family: thought · brought · caught · taught · bought
- Same family: put · cut · let · hit · set

**Odd Shelf feedback:**
- An -ed form picked: Say it in a sentence about yesterday. Does it sound right?
- Same family: Put stays put. Yesterday Pip put his boots by the door.
- Thought family: These end in -ought or -aught, and they all sound alike.

### (e) Hold It Steady (Sapling, Keeper's Desk)

**Header:** Read the entry. Tap any verb that slips out of its time. If nothing slips, tap **No slips**.

**Entry 1 (Sapling, A story from Willowbank):** *Last night the storm came back. The wind howled. The gate banged. A bucket rolls down the lane. Pip chased it.* (one slip: rolls)

**Entry 2 (Sapling):** *Last spring the brook rose to the second mark. This week it has risen to the first. Tomorrow we will check again.* (no slips)

**Entry 3 (Sapling):** *These days Dot opens the post every morning. She sorted the letters. She hangs up her hat.* (one slip: sorted)

**A letter upstream (Keeper's Desk; opens after page 2 comes free):** Dot's letter to Nuria, one line at a time.
- *The old Keeper's ledger lists the floods at our mill.* (steady: the ledger lists them now)
- *Back then, the floods come closer together.* (slip: came)
- *We have started a weather log.* (steady: it matters now)
- *We will send you a page each month.* (steady: next)
- Then choose one line to add: *The brook had risen before the old Keeper wrote each number.* or *The brook has risen before the old Keeper wrote each number.* (had)

**Hold It Steady feedback:**
- Slip missed: Check each verb against the time word. Which one says a different time?
- Right verb tapped as a slip: That verb fits its own time word.
- No slips tapped when one slips: Look again at one verb. Does it match last night?
- Slip tapped in Entry 2: The time moves in each sentence, so the tense moves too.
- Lists tapped as a slip: The ledger still lists the floods today. Lists fits.

## Problem sets

Every item has the "I'm stuck" ladder. Choices appear in the order written; ✔ marks the right one. Tags in backticks name the misconception a wrong choice tests. Every entry and sentence is **A story from Willowbank**.

### Set A. Then, now, next (Sprout, Seedling)

- Explanation: Go, went, has gone.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Verb:** a word for an action or a state, like rain, run or is.
- **Tense:** the form of a verb that shows when: then, now or next.
- **Past tense:** a verb form for then: rained, ran.
- **Present tense:** a verb form for now, or what usually happens: is raining, rains.
- **Future:** what comes next. English uses will: will rain.
- **Regular verb:** a verb that adds -ed for the past: walk, walked.
- **Irregular verb:** a verb with its own past form: go, went.
- **Progressive:** be plus an -ing verb, for something going on: is raining. Also called continuous.
- **Present perfect:** has or have plus a past participle: has rained, has gone. It happened before now and matters now.
- **Past participle:** the form after has or have, like gone, risen or rained.
- **Habit:** something that usually happens, again and again.
- **Log:** a book where you write down what happens each day.
- **Ledger:** an old record book, written by hand. (Shown only after page 2 comes free.)

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** past, present and future; the progressive (be plus -ing) for actions in progress at any time; the plain present for habits; the present perfect against the simple past; common irregular past forms and past participles; holding one tense steady and changing it only when the time changes; and, at Keeper's Desk, *had* for the earlier of two past events and the present after *if* or *when* for a future condition.

**About the names:** US school standards call *will walk* the simple future tense, and this pack uses that school name. The English national curriculum glossary notes that English marks only present and past by changing the verb, so *will* shows future time with a helper. Both descriptions are correct about how English works. "Progressive" and "continuous" name the same form. UK teaching often says "past simple" for simple past.

**About "goed":** forms like *goed* and *runned* show that a child has learned the -ed pattern and is applying it everywhere. Research on children's speech found these forms fairly uncommon and usually temporary. A gentle recast ("Yes, the hen ran!") works better than a correction drill.

**About American usage:** in American speech, *I just ate* and *Did you eat yet?* are common and accepted, where British English prefers the present perfect. The items here avoid *just*, *already* and *yet*, and key *has rained* only on words like *so far* and *since*, or on a result still here now.

**Home and regional forms:** some families and regions say *I seen it* or *it done rained*. This pack teaches the forms used in written logs and letters. It never marks a child's own speech wrong.

**What it does not claim:** the Log Line is a picture, not a full grammar. Every log entry, the flood post marks and the old Keeper's ledger are made up. Nothing here forecasts real weather. Modal verbs for how sure we are (*might*, *must*) are taught in Clauses and Joins; passive and subjunctive forms in The Gazette's Grammar; -ed spelling changes in One Seed, Many Words.
