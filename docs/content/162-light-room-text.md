## The Sun rule card

Shown on Bend and Split, the rainbow scene and the Sort tray's Sun card. Never a quiz answer screen.

> **The Sun rule.** Never look at the Sun. Not with sunglasses. Not through clouds. Never through binoculars, a telescope or a lens. It can hurt your eyes for good. To see the Sun, a grown-up can help you make its picture on paper with a pinhole, with your back to the Sun.

**Short version (read aloud):** Never look at the Sun. A grown-up can show you its picture on paper.

## Story scenes (A story from Willowbank)

### Scene 1. The lamp in the wings (Act II, chapter 4: the first show)

**Short:** The stage lamp is stuck on its hook. Dot needs light at center stage. The baker brings a tin tray. Can you bend the light's path?

**Longer:** It is the night of the first show. The Story Loft is full, and Pip has eaten two of the props. The only stage lamp hangs on a hook in the wings, and the hook will not move. Its beam lands on the prop cupboard. "Center stage," says Dot, tapping her list. "Curtain call. I can't bow in the dark. Well, I can. Nobody will know." The baker arrives with a tin tray, polished for the occasion. The miller hands over her mirror, which she says is lucky. {guide} looks at the lamp, then the cupboard, then the star chalked on the floor where Dot will stand. "Light goes in straight lines," says {guide}. "Mirrors can turn it. Shall we try?" Bramble, in the front row, says she could do it with one ribbon. Nobody asks how.

**Keeper's note (How light works):** A mirror set slantwise turns a beam through a corner. Between mirrors, the beam goes straight. Two mirrors in a tube make a periscope. Johannes Hevelius described one in 1647 and called it a polemoscope.

### Scene 2. Bramble and the cat's eyes (Act II side scene)

**Short:** In the lamplight, the miller's cat's eyes glow green. "She shines them to see in the dark," says Bramble. Is she right?

**Longer:** After the show, the lamps go down one by one. Under the bench, two green circles shine. "The cat," says the miller. "She sees in the dark by shining her eyes," says Bramble, very sure. "Like little lanterns." Pip hides behind Dot. {guide} switches the lamp off. The green circles vanish. Lamp on: there they are again. "Lanterns stay lit when you switch the lamp off," says {guide}. "Hers don't." Bramble thinks about this for a long time. "Then where does the green come from?" That is a good question, and the Eye Path board can answer it.

**Keeper's note (How light works):** Cats have a shiny layer at the back of each eye, behind the part that senses light. It bounces light back through, giving the eye a second chance to catch it. Some of that light comes back out, which is the glow. Cats see well in dim light. In total dark, they see nothing either.

### Scene 3. After the long rain (Act VI, chapter 4)

**Short:** The rain stops. The low Sun comes out behind the town. Over Willow Brook, a rainbow. Pip asks where it ends.

**Longer:** The rain has gone up the valley. The evening Sun slides out low behind the bakery, and every wet roof shines. Over Willow Brook, in front of everyone, a rainbow stands up out of the reeds. Pip wants to know where it ends, because he has heard there is food. "It moves when you move," says Wren Halloway. "Everyone sees their own." Dot checks: the Sun is behind them, the rain is in front. Bramble's magpie flies straight through the rainbow and comes out the other side, unimpressed. "Don't look at the Sun to check it's there," says {guide}. "Look at your shadow. Your head's shadow points to the middle of the bow."

**Keeper's note (How light works):** A main rainbow sits about 42 degrees from the point opposite the Sun. That point is where your head's shadow falls. When the Sun is low, the bow stands high. When the Sun is higher than about 42 degrees, the bow is below the horizon, so you see none from flat ground.

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer. Worked examples reveal one step per tap.

**T1. Dark means no light.** To see anything, light must reach your eyes. In a shut room with no light, there is nothing to see, however long you wait.
- Worked example, the shut cupboard. Step 1: The door is shut. No light gets in. Step 2: Wait. Still nothing. Step 3: Open the door. Light gets in, and the jars appear.

**T2. Sources and reflectors.** A light source makes its own light: the Sun, a flame, a lamp. Most things don't. We see them because light bounces off them.
- Worked example, lantern or spoon? Step 1: Put both in the dark box. Step 2: Light the lantern. It glows. Step 3: The spoon shines only because the lantern's light bounces off it.

**T3. Light comes into your eyes.** You see the apple when lamp light bounces off it into your eye. Nothing comes out of your eyes.
- Worked example. Step 1: Lamp to apple. Step 2: Apple to eye. Step 3: Now you can see the apple.

**T4. Everything reflects some light.** A mirror bounces light neatly, so you see a picture in it. Paper and wood scatter light every way, so you see the paper itself.
- Worked example. Step 1: Shine the beam on the mirror: one bright spot on the wall. Step 2: Shine it on paper: a soft glow everywhere.

**T5. Light goes in straight lines.** Light passes three holes in cards only when the holes are in a line. That is also why a shadow keeps the card's outline.
- Worked example. Step 1: Holes lined up: light gets through. Step 2: Slide one card. Step 3: The light stops at that card.

**T6. Mirrors turn a beam.** A mirror set slantwise turns a beam through a corner. The beam still goes straight before and after the mirror.
- Worked example. Step 1: The beam goes right. Step 2: It hits a slanted mirror. Step 3: It turns and goes up, straight again.

**T7. Angle in, angle out (Sapling).** Draw the normal: a line at right angles to the mirror. The beam leaves the normal at the same angle it arrived.
- Worked example. Step 1: Draw the dashed normal where the beam hits. Step 2: The beam comes in 40 degrees from it. Step 3: It leaves 40 degrees on the other side.

**T8. Light changes direction at water.** Light is slower in water and glass. Crossing in or out at a slant, it changes direction. The straw only looks broken.
- Worked example. Step 1: Light leaves the underwater straw. Step 2: At the surface it changes direction. Step 3: Your eye sees the straw in the wrong place.

**T9. White light holds colors.** A prism spreads white light into colors. They were in the light already. Raindrops do the same, making rainbows when the Sun is behind you.
- Worked example. Step 1: White beam in. Step 2: The prism turns each color a different amount. Step 3: A band of colors comes out, red to violet.

**T10. The eye is a chain.** Light goes through the pupil. The cornea and lens focus it on the retina. The optic nerve carries signals to the brain, which makes the picture.
- Worked example. Step 1: Pupil, the opening. Step 2: Lens, the focuser. Step 3: Retina, the light catcher. Step 4: Optic nerve to the brain.

## History cards (A true thing from history)

**Ibn al-Haytham.** About a thousand years ago, Ibn al-Haytham wrote a *Book of Optics* in Cairo. Many people then thought eyes sent out rays to see. He argued light comes into the eye. One reason: very bright light harms the eye. Historians are not sure when he wrote it: perhaps about 1011 to 1021, perhaps about 1028 to 1038.

**Newton's prisms.** From 1666, Isaac Newton split sunlight with prisms. He let one color through a second prism. It bent again, but stayed the same color. The prism had not made the color. He published the full story in *Opticks* in 1704.

**Newton's seven colors.** At first Newton spoke of endless shades in between. Later he named seven, adding orange and indigo, partly to match a musical scale. The real band of colors has no lines in it.

**The lighthouse lens.** On 25 July 1823, a new lens by Augustin Fresnel was lit at the Cordouan lighthouse, on the coast of France. Its stepped glass gathered the lamp's light into a beam ships could see far out.

**The rainbow globe.** About 700 years ago, Theodoric of Freiberg shone sunlight into a glass globe full of water, like a giant raindrop, and saw rainbow colors. In 1637 René Descartes worked out the bow's angle.

## Key terms

- **Light source:** something that makes its own light, like the Sun, a flame or a lamp.
- **Reflect:** to bounce light off. Every surface reflects some light.
- **Mirror:** a smooth shiny surface that reflects light neatly, so you see a picture.
- **Scatter:** to send light off every which way, as paper and wood do.
- **Ray:** a line with an arrow, showing which way light travels. It's a drawing, not a piece of light.
- **Normal:** a line at right angles to a mirror. Angles are measured from it.
- **Periscope:** a tube with two mirrors for seeing over things.
- **Refraction:** light changing direction as it passes into or out of water or glass.
- **Lens:** curved glass that bends light toward one spot.
- **Prism:** a block of glass that spreads white light into colors.
- **Spectrum:** the band of colors from white light.
- **Pupil:** the opening in the eye that lets light in.
- **Iris:** the colored ring that makes the pupil bigger or smaller.
- **Retina:** the back of the eye, which catches light and sends signals.
- **Optic nerve:** carries signals from the eye to the brain.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches.** That we see when light enters our eyes, never because eyes send something out; sources versus reflectors (the Moon reflects); light traveling in straight lines, steered by mirrors and periscopes; at older depth, the law of reflection measured from the normal; a simple picture of refraction (the straw, a lens) and of white light splitting into colors; a generic eye from pupil to brain; and the Sun rule.

**What it does not claim.** Benches are simplified pictures, not measurements. The eye card is a generic drawing. Nothing asks about your child's eyes or sight, and nothing here is eye-health advice; questions about a child's vision belong with an eye-care professional. Selective color absorption, filters, virtual images and the wave model are not taught.

**The Sun rule** is a fixed safety rule, not something the game demonstrates. Ordinary sunglasses are not safe for looking at the Sun, and eye damage from sunlight can happen without pain, so the rule never depends on discomfort. Eye protection and skin protection (shade, hats, sunscreen) are different needs.

**Off-screen, optional, grown-up-led:**
- *Dark den:* a blanket over a table and a flashlight. Check the space is safe, stay close, and keep it short.
- *Holes in cards:* a grown-up makes one hole in each of three index cards; line them up on a table with a flashlight.
- *Pinhole Sun picture:* a pinhole in card held over white paper, your backs to the Sun, looking only at the paper. Never look through the pinhole at the Sun. Adult-led every time.
- *Straw in a glass:* a clear glass of water and a straw. Mop spills.
