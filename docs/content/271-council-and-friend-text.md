Companion to [spec 271](../specs/271-council-and-friend.md). Use as written, after review. `{guide}` is never gendered. Pronouns: Pip he/him; Dot, Nuria, Wren Halloway she/her; the librarian they/them; Puff takes no pronouns. Sources: [audit](../source-audits/271-council-and-friend.md). ## Story scenes

### Scene 1. Two letters on Dot's counter (Act III ch. 4, letters up and down the river)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** The reed seeds from Vadoalto have arrived. Two letters must go up the river: one to Nuria, and one to Vadoalto's town council.

**Short:**
> One piece of news, two readers. Nuria gets a friendly letter. The town council gets a formal one. Same news, different words.

**Longer:**
> Dot spreads two sheets of paper on the counter. "Same news, two readers," she says. "The reed seeds came. And Willowbank would like a copy of Vadoalto's flood post readings from the storm."
>
> Pip grabs the pencil. "Hi council! Seeds came. I tried one. Not tasty. Send the numbers, okay?"
>
> "That's a fine letter," says Dot, "for Nuria."
>
> Pip reads it again. "What's wrong with it for the council?"
>
> "Nothing's wrong with it. The council doesn't know you. They'll expect 'Dear Members of the Council' and a clear request."
>
> {guide} reads over his shoulder. "Maybe leave out the part where you ate a seed."
>
> "It was one seed," says Pip.
>
> Dot takes out her brass pen. "Two letters. Two voices. Both of them ours."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> Spanish letters show formality too. To one person, Spanish has two ways to say "you": *tú* for a friend and *usted* for formal talk. A letter to Nuria says *tú*. *(fluent reader)*

### Scene 3. The librarian's reading (any time; a library reading hour)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** The librarian reads aloud from an old book set in Yorkshire, in the north of England. A character says "nowt". Pip wants to know what nowt is.

**Short:**
> In *The Secret Garden*, Martha speaks the English of Yorkshire. "Nowt" means nothing. Reading it aloud helps you hear her voice.

**Longer:**
> The librarian opens *The Secret Garden*, a book from 1911. "Martha is talking to Mary," they say, and read:
>
> ‘Nowt o’ th’ soart’ means ‘nothin’-of-the-sort,’
>
> "Nowt?" says Pip.
>
> "Nothing," says the librarian. "Martha speaks Yorkshire, the English of the place where she grew up."
>
> "Is she saying it wrong?"
>
> "She's saying it the Yorkshire way. It has its own words and its own rules. Later in the book, Mary tries to learn it."
>
> "Why is it spelled like that?"
>
> "The writer wanted you to hear Martha's voice. Read it aloud and you will."
>
> Pip tries. "Nowt o' th' soart." He grins. "That's quicker."
>
> "Martha says so herself," says the librarian. "She says the long way takes so long to say."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> A dialect is the English of a group of people, often from one place. Yorkshire English has its own words, like *nowt* for nothing. Standard English is one dialect among many. It is the one most books, notices and formal letters use.

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer; worked-example steps appear one tap at a time. All cards are **A true thing about language**.

**Card 1. Formal and informal**
> Informal language is for people you know well. Formal language is for people you don't know well, or for serious jobs. Neither one is better.

Worked example: *Two greetings.*
1. To a friend: Hi Nuria!
2. To a town council: Dear Members of the Council,
3. Same job, saying hello. Different readers.

**Card 2. One formal word for two**
> Formal writing often swaps a two-word verb for one word. Find out becomes discover. Ask for becomes request. Go in becomes enter.

Worked example: *Dot's council line.*
1. Friendly: We'd like to ask for a copy.
2. Swap ask for with request.
3. Formal: We would like to request a copy.

**Card 3. Keep the meaning**
> A formal swap must keep the meaning. Request asks politely. Require says something must happen. Pick the word that says what you mean.

Worked example: *Two formal words.*
1. We request a copy. (We are asking.)
2. We require a copy. (We are telling them they must.)
3. Willowbank is asking, so request fits.

**Card 4. Letter forms**
> Many people say "we was" or "I done it". Most letters, books and notices write "we were" and "I did it". Both are English. Choose for your reader.

Worked example: *Pip's news, two ways.*
1. Pip says: We was up all night.
2. For the council, find the verb: was.
3. Most letters write: We were up all night.

**Card 5. Formal is not the same as standard**
> Formal or informal depends on your listener. Standard forms are word forms, like "we were" and "I did". You can chat in standard forms. Standard English has no special accent.

Worked example: *Three lines about the storm.*
1. We were soaked! Informal, standard forms.
2. We was soaked! Informal, another dialect's forms.
3. We were thoroughly soaked. Formal, standard forms.

**Card 6. Dialect and register**
> A dialect is how a group of people speak, often in one place. A register is how anyone speaks for one kind of job, like a letter or a chat.

Worked example: *Martha and Dot.*
1. Martha says nowt: Yorkshire dialect.
2. Dot writes "Dear Members of the Council": a formal letter register.
3. Everyone has a dialect, and everyone switches registers.

**Card 7. Dialect in stories**
> Writers sometimes spell a dialect the way it sounds, so you hear a character's voice. Read it aloud. Then ask what it means.

Worked example: *A line from Martha.*
1. Read aloud: "Nowt o' th' soart."
2. Martha explains it: "nothin'-of-the-sort".
3. Nowt means nothing. Th' is the.

**Card 8. Talk and writing**
> Chat often has contractions, slang and quick tags like "isn't it?" Formal writing usually has whole sentences, fewer contractions and no slang.

Worked example: *Pip's note and Dot's letter.*
1. Pip: c u at the mill, ok?
2. Find the chat parts: c u, ok.
3. Dot: Please meet us at the mill.

**Card 9. Who's listening?**
> Good speakers change their words for their listeners. A friend gets a joke. A meeting gets a clear opening, one point at a time, and a thank-you.

Worked example: *Telling Puff and telling the meeting.*
1. To Puff: Guess what? The seeds came!
2. To the meeting: Good evening, everyone.
3. Then: The reed seeds have arrived from Vadoalto.

**Card 10. A short speech**
> A short speech has an opening, one or two points with a reason, and a closing. Keep to the point. Look up at your listeners.

Worked example: *Pip's welcome for the traders.*
1. Opening: Good morning, and welcome to Willowbank.
2. Point: Our market is open until noon, so there's time to look around.
3. Closing: Thank you for coming.

**Card 11. Words for many subjects**
> Some words turn up in lots of subjects: estimate, evidence, predict, conclude. Others belong to one subject, like floodplain. Learn a word, then use it yourself.

Worked example: *A word from a letter.*
1. The council writes: We hope these readings are sufficient.
2. Clue: Will they be enough?
3. Sufficient means enough. Try it in a sentence of your own.

**Card 12. Notice your own choices**
> Good speakers and writers notice how they change their words. Ask: who is it for? What did I change? What would I try next time?

Worked example: *Dot looks at her two letters.*
1. Who for? Nuria, then the council.
2. What changed? The greeting, some words and the ending.
3. Next time: shorter sentences for the council.

### (d) Talk or Text? (Keeper's Desk)

**Header:** Is it more usual in a chat, or in formal writing? Some features fit both.

**Feature cards and trays (chat · formal writing · both):**
- "isn't it?" on the end: chat. *A quick check with the listener.*
- c u, lol: chat. *Text-message spellings.*

### (f) The Word Bank (Keeper's Desk)

**Header:** Words from this pack. Tap one to see what it means and how it is used.

**Word cards:**
- estimate: a careful guess using what you know. *Wren estimated the boxes she hadn't counted.*
- evidence: what you can point to that shows something is true. *The readings are evidence.*
- predict: say what you think will happen. *Nuria predicted the brook would rise.*
- conclude: decide something after looking at the evidence. *What can we conclude from the readings?*
- indicate: point to, or show. *The readings indicate a fast rise.*
- sufficient: enough. *We hope these readings are sufficient.*
- floodplain (a river word): flat land beside a river that floods when the river is high.

**Use it yourself:** Pick one word. Say a sentence with it to a grown-up. *(Spoken; nothing stored.)*

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Formal:** careful language for people you don't know well, or for serious jobs.
- **Informal:** relaxed language for people you know well.
- **Audience:** the people who read or hear what you say.
- **Dialect:** the way a group of people speak, often in one place, with its own words and forms.
- **Register:** the way anyone speaks or writes for one kind of job, like a chat or a formal letter.
- **Standard English:** the dialect most books, notices and formal letters use. It has no special accent.
- **Accent:** how words sound when someone says them.
- **Verb:** a doing or being word, like ran, did or were.
- **Contraction:** two words squeezed into one with an apostrophe, like didn't.
- **Slang:** very informal words, often new, used among friends.
- **Speech:** a short talk you give to a group.
- **Floodplain:** flat land beside a river that floods when the river is high.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** formal and informal language for a stated reader; formal word swaps that keep the meaning; standard verb forms for formal letters; dialect and register, with a public-domain novel; spoken and written language; adapting speech to listeners; a short spoken speech; academic and subject words; and noticing one's own choices.

**About home and regional English:** many children speak a dialect at home that uses forms like "we was" or "I done it". These forms follow their own rules and are not mistakes. This pack teaches standard forms as a choice for a formal reader, and never checks or marks how a child speaks. England's grammar glossary makes the same distinction: Standard English is a set of word forms, not an accent, and it can be casual as well as formal. Please don't correct your child's own speech during these benches.

**About the book:** *The Secret Garden* (1911) by Frances Hodgson Burnett is in the public domain. Lines are quoted exactly from the Project Gutenberg text, or marked "retold". The book ties Yorkshire speech to servants and cottagers, a class view of its time: the housekeeper, Mrs. Medlock, tells Martha not to speak it, and later speaks it herself “because there was no one to hear her”. This pack does not repeat the ban. It shows Mary loving Dickon's Yorkshire and learning it, and treats Yorkshire as a dialect with its own words and rules. If your child asks why Mrs. Medlock hides hers, that is a good question to talk about.

**Speaking and reflection:** speeches and "use it yourself" sentences are spoken to you, and reflection is talk. Nothing is recorded or stored: not the child's voice, words or which plan they spoke for. The low-fields speech asks for a plan's cost alongside it; the pack never says which plan is best.

**Terms:** in England these topics sit in Year 4 (standard verb forms), Year 6 (formal vocabulary) and key stage 3. The child text uses American terms and "Sincerely,"; British letters often close "Yours sincerely" or "Yours faithfully".

**Spanish:** Nuria's and the council's lines, and the *tú* and *usted* note, need a fluent reader before build.
