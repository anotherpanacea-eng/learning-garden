## Story scenes

### Scene 1. Two lambs at the ford (spring)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** Hob's white ewe has twins. One lamb is white. One is black as a boot. Pip has questions.

**Short:**
> Hob's white ewe has two lambs, one white, one black. Pip thinks the black one is lost. It isn't. Lambs can differ from their parents.

**Longer:**
> Two lambs wobble beside Hob's white ewe. One is white. One is black from nose to tail.
>
> "That one's in the wrong field," says Pip. "White sheep have white lambs."
>
> "Born right here at dawn," says Hob. "I watched."
>
> Pip squints. "But it doesn't match."
>
> "Same kind, though," says {guide}. "Four legs, woolly coat, same ears. It's a lamb, through and through."
>
> The black lamb butts its mother for milk. She doesn't seem to mind the color.
>
> "Every spring there's a surprise," says Hob. "Young take after their parents. Never a perfect copy."
>
> Pip writes in his notebook: LAMBS: SAME KIND. NOT SAME.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about living things):**
> In most white sheep breeds, white wins over black. A white sheep can carry black hidden. If both parents carry it, a black lamb can be born, though not often.

### Scene 2. Jelly in the millpond (spring)

**Hook:** Pip finds gray jelly with black dots in the millpond. "Fish soup," he says. The miller says, "Wait and see."

**Short:**
> Pip thinks the jelly is fish soup. It is frogspawn. The dots become tadpoles, then grow legs and become little frogs.

**Longer:**
> The jelly sits at the edge of the millpond, full of black dots.
>
> "Fish soup," says Pip, hopefully.
>
> "Frogspawn," says the miller. "Leave it be and come back."
>
> In two weeks the dots wriggle free. Tiny tadpoles, all tail, nibble green slime.
>
> "Fish," says Pip. "I knew it."
>
> Weeks later the tadpoles have back legs. Then front legs. Their tails shrink.
>
> One morning a froglet no bigger than a fingernail climbs onto a lily pad and blinks at Pip.
>
> "You were a tadpole," he tells it. "You've changed your whole shape."
>
> The froglet hops away. It has places to be.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about living things):**
> Common frogs live high in the Pyrenees, breeding in lakes up to about 2,500 meters. They spawn when the ice melts: early April near 1,500 meters, June near 2,330 meters. Warmth sets the timing.

### Scene 3. Five kittens (Act II, the weekly market)

**Hook:** The miller brings a basket to the flour stall. Five kittens are asleep in it, in a heap.

**Short:**
> The miller's cat has five kittens. They are all cats, and each is different. Dot names each one by what makes it different.

**Longer:**
> The basket wakes up. Ten ears, five tails, five very small yawns.
>
> Dot lines them up on a flour sack. "This one has a white chin. This one's all stripes. This one has one ginger paw."
>
> "Five kittens from one cat," says {guide}. "And no two the same."
>
> "They're all cat, though," says Dot. "Whiskers, paws, that nose."
>
> She names them by their differences: Chin, Stripes, Ginger Paw, Smudge and Plain. Plain looks offended.
>
> "Fine," says Dot. "Plain has the longest tail."

### Scene 4. Bramble's beans (summer, Market Fair)

**Hook:** Bramble's beans grow on the sunny wall. Pip's grow in the shed. Both say they have better seeds.

**Short:**
> Bramble's beans are short and green. Pip's are tall and pale. Same seed packet. The light made the difference.

**Longer:**
> Bramble sets her pot on the market table. Bushy, dark green, covered in leaves.
>
> "Better seeds," she says.
>
> Pip sets his pot beside it. His bean is twice as tall, thin and pale.
>
> "Taller," says Pip. "So mine are better seeds."
>
> The orchard keeper turns both pots round. "You both took seeds from my packet. Same seeds."
>
> "Then why are they different?" says Bramble.
>
> "Where did they grow?"
>
> "The sunny wall," says Bramble. "The shed," says Pip.
>
> "There's your difference," says the orchard keeper. "The light. The seeds were the same. And tall isn't better. Pip's bean was stretching for light."
>
> Bramble looks pleased, then less pleased. Her plant is healthier. Her seeds were no better.

### Scene 5. The moth on the mill wall (Act IV, Keeper's Desk)

**Hook:** A speckled moth rests on the mill's pale stone. You almost miss it. Bramble says moths change color to hide.

**Short:**
> Bramble says moths change color to hide. The librarian brings a real case from the archive. Moths didn't change. Which moths survived did.

**Longer:**
> The moth is gray and speckled, flat against the pale stone.
>
> "Moths turn the color of whatever they sit on," says Bramble. "Everyone knows that."
>
> "Some animals can change color," says the librarian. "But this kind can't. A moth keeps its wing color all its life." They set a folder on the desk. *The Peppered Moth: a true case.*
>
> "When soot blackened the trees, the dark moths were the hidden ones," says the librarian. "Birds found more pale ones. So more dark moths lived to lay eggs."
>
> "So the moths did change," says Bramble.
>
> "The mix changed," says the librarian. "Not one moth."
>
> Bramble frowns, then writes that down.

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer; worked-example steps appear one tap at a time. Cards 1-3 are **A true thing about living things**.

**Card 1. One ring, many shapes**
> Living things start, grow, can make young, and one day their life ends. Their young start the ring again. That is a life cycle.

Worked example: *A broad bean.*
1. Start: a seed in the soil.
2. Grow: a seedling, then a tall plant with flowers.
3. Make young: pods full of new seeds.
4. Life ends: the plant dries up once its pods ripen. Its seeds start the ring again.

**Card 2. Change shape, or grow bigger?**
> Some young look like small adults and grow bigger. Others change their whole body shape. That big change is called metamorphosis.

Worked example: *A chick and a tadpole.*
1. A chick has a beak, wings and legs, like a hen. It grows bigger.
2. A tadpole has a tail and no legs. A frog has legs and no tail.
3. Chick: grows bigger. Tadpole: changes shape.

**Card 3. The frog's ring**
> Frogspawn hatches into tadpoles that live underwater. Back legs grow, then front legs. The tail shrinks. A froglet climbs out.

Worked example: *Order the millpond pictures.*
1. Jelly with dots comes first: frogspawn.
2. A tadpole with back legs only comes before one with four legs.
3. A froglet still has a stub of tail. Then a frog.

**Card 4. Young are the same kind**
> Young plants and animals are the same kind as their parents. They share many features, like ear shape or leaf shape.

Worked example: *Which adult goes with the lamb?*
1. Look at the lamb: woolly coat, hooves, long face.
2. Look for an adult with the same features.
3. The sheep. Same kind.

**Card 5. Never an exact copy**
> Young are never exact copies of their parents. Young from one litter differ from each other too. That is variation.

Worked example: *The five kittens.*
1. All have whiskers, paws and tails: all cats.
2. Chin has a white chin. Stripes is all stripes.
3. Same kind, each one different.

**Card 6. A spread is real**
> Measure many of one kind and the numbers spread out. Most sit near the middle. A spread is real variation. Nobody made a mistake.

Worked example: *Five pods from one row.*
1. Measure: 16, 17, 17, 18, 19 cm.
2. Most are near 17.
3. One is 19. Measured again, still 19. It's just a longer pod.

**Card 7. Born with, or picked up?**
> Some features come from parents: born with. Some come from where it lives: picked up. Many come from both.

Worked example: *Hob's sheep.*
1. Black wool: from its parents. Born with.
2. A short fleece after shearing: Hob did that. Picked up.
3. A big, strong lamb: parents and good grass. Both.

**Card 8. Picked up is not passed on**
> A feature picked up during life does not pass to young. A shorn ewe's lamb is born woolly.

Worked example: *Hob paints a blue spot on a ewe.*
1. The spot came from paint, not parents.
2. Her lamb arrives in spring.
3. No blue spot. Only born-with features pass on.

**Card 9. Fit for the place**
> An adaptation is a born-with feature that helps a living thing survive where it lives. A good fit in one place can be a bad fit elsewhere.

Worked example: *A frog's webbed back feet.*
1. Problem: the frog must swim fast in the pond.
2. Feature: skin between its toes pushes water.
3. On dry rock, webbed feet don't help it hide or run.

**Card 10. Over many generations (Keeper's Desk)**
> Over many generations, the born-with features of a group of living things change. That is evolution. Natural selection is one cause. There is lots of evidence for it.

Worked example: *Where the evidence comes from.*
1. Fossils show living things long ago were different (the fossil corner, `#living/fossils`).
2. Living things today share features from shared ancestors.
3. Some changes were watched happening, like the peppered moth.

### (f) The Moth on the Mill Wall (Keeper's Desk)

**Bird's-eye Bark header (A made-up model):** Twenty model moths rest on pale bark. Tap the eight a bird would notice first.

**After tapping:** On pale bark, dark moths are easy to spot. Now watch the model.

**Model run, pale bark (A made-up model):**
1. Start: 10 pale moths, 10 dark moths.
2. Birds find 2 pale and 6 dark.
3. Left: 8 pale, 4 dark.
4. Each moth left leaves two young like itself.
5. Next generation: 16 pale, 8 dark.

**Switch to sooty bark:** Same start. Birds find 6 pale and 2 dark. Next generation: 8 pale, 16 dark.

**Model line:** A made-up model with simple rules. Real moths have more young, and food and space limit their numbers.

**The real file (A true thing from history):**
- **1848:** A dark peppered moth is recorded in Manchester, England. The dark form had been rare.
- **1895:** About 98 of every 100 peppered moths in Manchester are dark. Soot has blackened the trees and killed the pale lichens.
- **1953 and 1955:** Bernard Kettlewell releases moths in a sooty wood and a clean one. Birds take more of the moths that stand out.
- **Critics' questions:** Too many moths at once? Released in daylight? Placed on trunks, where they rarely rest?
- **1956 onward:** Clean-air laws cut the soot. Bark grows pale lichen again. Dark moths become rare.
- **The 2000s:** Michael Majerus answers the critics with a new test: 4,864 moths over six years, mostly on branches. Birds still take more dark ones.
- **2016:** Scientists find the change in one gene that makes a moth dark. They estimate it began about 1819.

**Four conditions (tick each that the moth case shows):**
- Moths vary: some pale, some dark.
- Young get their color from their parents.
- More moths hatch than can survive.
- Better-hidden moths leave more young.

**Two stories:** Bramble: "Each moth turned itself dark." The file: "More dark moths survived and had young." Which evidence tells them apart?

## Problem sets

### Set A. Four rings (Sprout, Seedling)

- Hint: What did Pip find in the pond first?

**A2 (Seedling, order).** Put the frog's ring in order.

- Answer: frogspawn, tadpole, tadpole with back legs, froglet, frog.

- Explanation: Back legs, then front legs, then the tail shrinks.

**A3 (Seedling).**
- Prompt: Which young one changes its whole body shape?

- Feedback: Lamb: A lamb grows bigger. Its body plan stays the same. · Chick: A chick already has wings, beak and legs.
- Hint: Which young one looks least like its parent?
- Explanation: A tadpole changes shape into a frog. That is metamorphosis.

**A4 (Seedling).**

### Set E. The moth on the mill wall (Keeper's Desk)

- Hint: First find how many dark moths are left.
- Explanation: No moth changed color. The share of dark moths fell.

- Prompt: From 1848 to 1895, how many years passed?

- Follow-up: Peppered moths have one generation a year. About how many generations? About 47.
- Hint: Subtract the earlier year from the later one.
- Explanation: In about 47 generations, dark moths went from rare to about 98 in every 100 in Manchester.

**E3 (number).**

- Hint: Share 4,864 into six equal years.
- Explanation: Hundreds of moths a year made his test far stronger than a handful.

**E4 (3-choice).**
- Prompt: Bramble says each moth turned itself dark. Which evidence answers her?
- Choices: Moths can't change, so the population didn't change (`nothing-changed`) · Each moth kept its color; the dark share rose ✔ · Soot stained the pale moths dark (`soot-stains`)
- Feedback: Didn't change: The mix did change: rare dark moths became most moths. · Soot: Dark moths have dark wings even in clean air.
- Hint: Did one moth change, or the mix of moths?
- Explanation: Each moth kept its color. More dark ones survived to have young.

**E5 (true / not true).**
- "Natural selection needs living things to vary." True.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Life cycle:** the ring of a living thing's life: start, grow, make young, life ends.
- **Offspring:** the young of a plant or animal.
- **Generation:** parents are one generation; their young are the next.
- **Metamorphosis:** a big change of body shape as an animal grows, like tadpole to frog.
- **Mammal:** an animal with hair or fur that feeds its young on milk, like a sheep.
- **Amphibian:** an animal that starts life in water and later lives on land too, like a frog.
- **Inherited:** passed from parents to young. Born with.
- **Trait:** a feature, like wool color or pod length.
- **Variation:** the differences between living things of the same kind.
- **Environment:** the place and conditions where something lives: light, food, weather, soil.
- **Breed:** a kind of farm animal that people chose for certain traits.
- **Adaptation:** a born-with feature that helps a living thing survive in its place.
- **Natural selection:** better-fitted living things leave more young, so their traits spread.
- **Evolution:** change in the born-with features of a group of living things over many generations.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** life cycles of a plant, a mammal, a bird and an amphibian compared on one backbone; offspring resemble parents but vary; inherited, environmental and mixed traits, with picked-up traits not passed on; adaptation as fit to a place; and, at Keeper's Desk, natural selection in the peppered moth, with evolution taught as science with evidence.

**Why there are no human examples:** many school lessons ask children which parent they look like. This pack never does. Children in adoptive, foster, kinship, donor-conceived and blended families can find that question painful, and a child's body and family are private. Plants, Hob's sheep, the miller's kittens and the moths teach the same science. Please keep conversations on those too.

**What it does not claim:** pictures and numbers are simplified. The pod lengths, the 120 and 75 apples, the moth model, *Lantern Red* and every Willowbank animal are invented. No genes or DNA are taught. Insect life cycles are taught at the Life Stages corner and the Bee Meadow, not here. Nobody in Willowbank dies; the "life ends" stage appears only in real-science cards.

**About the peppered moth:** Kettlewell's 1950s experiments were criticized for their methods. Majerus's six-year redesign (published 2012) answered the critics and supported bird predation: science checking itself.

**Safety:** both off-screen activities are optional and adult-led; see the cards above. Nothing is eaten or tasted.
