### Sounding the millpond (with Hob Pennick)

**Hook:** The old Keeper measured the brook with a marked post. Hob shows you a sailor's trick.

**Short:**
> Hob ties a stone to a knotted string. You lower it into the millpond until it goes slack. Count the knots. That's how deep.

**Longer:**
> "The old Keeper read the flood post," says Hob Pennick. "But you can't put a post in the middle of a pond." He ties a stone to a string knotted every hand's width.
>
> You lower the stone from the mill jetty. The string pulls tight, then suddenly goes loose.
>
> "Felt that? It's hit bottom." Hob pinches the string at the water. "Now count the knots."
>
> Pip counts. He loses count. You count again.
>
> "Sailors did this for the whole sea, once," says Hob. "With a much longer string."

**Keeper's note:**
> Sailors measured depth this way for centuries, with a weighted line marked in fathoms. HMS Challenger did it in the deep ocean in the 1870s. Today ships time an echo instead.

## D1. Who lives where? (the tray)

**Teach (A true thing about the ocean):** Sea animals need food, a safe place, and the right water. A rabbit needs the same kinds of things.

**Worked example (tap to reveal):** A clownfish.
1. Food: it eats tiny animals and bits of algae.
2. A safe place: it hides in an anemone's tentacles.
3. The right water: warm, shallow sea with oxygen in it.
4. All three met. It can live here.

**Teach 2:** Fish need oxygen too. They take it from the water with their gills.

**Teach 3:** Different parts of the sea suit different animals. A warm reef fish would be too cold in the deep.

**Contrast card:** Rabbit: grass, a burrow, air. Clownfish: tiny food, an anemone, warm sea water. Same kinds of needs.

**Tray text:**
- **Pick an animal:** clownfish · shore crab · anglerfish · sea turtle
- **Give it:** Food · A safe place · The right water (three cards each)
- **All three given:** It's settled in. It has what it needs.
- **Wrong water:** This water is too cold for me. Where else could I go?
- **Wrong food or place:** I can't use this. Try another card.

**Guided (Sprout):** Give the crab what it needs.
Steps shown: 1. Pick food: small bits on the shore. 2. Pick a safe place: under a rock. 3. Pick water: cool sea water.

## D2. The reef

**Teach:** Coral is an animal. A reef is built by tiny animals called polyps.

**Worked example (zoom):**
1. A whole reef, busy with fish.
2. Zoom in: one branch of coral.
3. Zoom again: one polyp. It has a mouth and tentacles.
4. A mouth means it eats. It is an animal.

**Teach 2:** Each polyp builds a hard cup around itself. Millions of cups, over many years, make a reef.

**Teach 3:** Tiny algae live inside many corals. They use sunlight to make food and share it.

**Teach 4:** So reef corals need clear, sunny, shallow water.

**Contrast card:** A snail is alive. Its shell is hard. A polyp is alive. Its cup is hard.

**Teach 5, variety:** Reefs cover a tiny part of the sea floor, but hold a huge variety of life. Count the kinds you spot.

**Spot-the-variety scene labels:** coral · clownfish · sea turtle · parrotfish · octopus · sea star · reef shark · sponge
**Bare floor scene (contrast):** sand · one sea cucumber
**After both scenes:** Lots of kinds on the reef. Very few on the bare sand.

**Teach 6, the nickname:** People call reefs "rainforests of the sea." Lots of kinds of life live there, and the reef gives them homes.

**Keeper's note:** Reefs cover less than 1% of the ocean floor. They are often said to hold about a quarter of known sea species. That is a rough estimate of unclear origin.

## D3. Not a bath (Profile table)

**Teach:** The sea floor is not flat. It has wide plains, mountains, a long ridge and deep trenches.

**Worked example (Drain the sea):**
1. Pip's guess: a flat line. Tap Drain the sea.
2. Near the shore: a shallow shelf, then a slope down.
3. Far out: wide flat plains, with mountains rising from them.
4. In the middle: a long ridge. Near the edge: a deep, narrow trench.

**Feature labels (tap to name):**
- **Shelf:** the shallow sea floor near land.
- **Slope:** where the shelf drops away.
- **Plain:** wide, flat and very deep.
- **Seamount:** a mountain under the sea.
- **Ridge:** a long underwater mountain range, with a valley down its middle.
- **Trench:** a very deep, narrow valley.
**Land cousins card:** Seamount, like a mountain. Trench, like a deep valley. Ridge, like a mountain range.

**Teach 2:** The mid-ocean ridge is the longest mountain range on Earth. Most of it is underwater.

**Teach 3 (Seedling):** The deepest place we know is the Challenger Deep. It's about 10,935 meters down.

**Drop Everest in (optional):** Mount Everest is about 8,849 meters tall. Put it in the Challenger Deep.
**After dropping:** Its peak is still about 2,100 meters underwater.

**Always shown:** A simplified picture. Up and down are to scale: 1 cm for every 1,000 m. Side to side is squeezed.
**Depths note:** Different ships measure slightly different depths. That's why we say "about."

**Tharp step (Sapling):** Here are five depths across the ridge. Plot them. Do it again for the next line.
**After three lines:** Look. A dip in the middle, every time. Marie Tharp noticed that too.

## D4. Lights in the dark (Dark water)

**Recall card (from the depth zones):** Below about 1,000 meters, no sunlight reaches. That's the midnight zone.

**Teach:** Many deep animals make their own light. This is called bioluminescence.

**Worked example (tap the dark):**
1. It's black. Tap somewhere.
2. A small light switches on. An anglerfish's lure.
3. The light comes from inside the lure.
4. So the fish made it. No sunlight is down here.

**Teach 2:** Living light has jobs: to catch food, find a mate, or startle a hunter.

**Teach 3:** An anglerfish's lure glows because of tiny bacteria living inside it.

**Light found lines:**
- An anglerfish. Its lure dangles in front of its mouth.
- A jelly, glowing blue when something bumps it.
- A lanternfish, with rows of little lights along its belly.
**Job question:** What is this light for?
**Job choices:** Catch food · Find a mate · Startle a hunter
**After choosing:** Good thinking. Scientists think that's one of its jobs.

**Teach 4, the deep is hard:** It's dark, cold, and food is scarce. The water presses hard from every side.

**Match it (challenge to answer):**
- No light → Make your own, or have huge eyes
- Not much food → A big mouth and a stretchy stomach
- Heavy water pressing → A soft body, mostly water

**Teach 5, pressure (Sapling):** Every 10 meters down adds about one more "air's worth" of pushing. At 1,000 meters, that's about 100 times.

**Teach 6:** Deep animals aren't crushed. Their bodies are mostly water, which hardly squashes, with little or no air inside.

**Follow the snow:**
**Teach 7:** Bits of dead plankton and waste sink from the top. This "marine snow" feeds many deep animals.
**Snow line:** Some flakes fall for weeks before they reach the floor.
**Snow end:** A sea cucumber on the floor eats the snow. Bacteria break down the rest.
**After the snow:** So most deep animals still depend on sunlight. It just reaches them as food, very slowly.

**The vent (Sapling):**
**Teach 8:** In 1977, scientists in a small submarine found hot springs on the deep floor. Animals crowded round them.
**Teach 9:** There, bacteria make food from chemicals in the water. Snails graze on them. Tube worms keep bacteria inside their bodies.
**Teach 10:** That means some life doesn't depend on sunlight at all.
**Two chains (build both):**
- Sunlight chain: phytoplankton (uses sunlight) · zooplankton · fish
- Vent chain: bacteria (use chemicals) · vent snail · crab
**Arrow reminder:** The arrow means "is eaten by." It points to the eater.
**After both chains:** Two different starts. One uses sunlight. One uses chemicals.
**Keeper's note:** Making food from chemicals is called chemosynthesis. The food comes from the chemicals, not from the heat. Light from animals and food from chemicals are two separate ideas.

## D5. Ripples (Ripple board)

**Always shown:** A made-up model. Real reefs change in many ways at once.

**Teach:** An ecosystem is the living things in a place, plus the water, light and warmth around them.

**Worked example (follow one change):**
1. Change card: muddy water washes onto the reef.
2. Less light gets through.
3. The coral's algae make less food. The coral grows less.
4. Fewer hiding places. There may be fewer small fish.

**Teach 2:** One change can ripple, step by step. Keep asking, "And then?"

**Teach 3:** Decomposers, like bacteria, break down dead things. That puts goodness back into the water.

**Board cards:** sunlight · warm water · currents · coral · coral's algae · small fish · reef shark · decomposers
**Change cards:** Muddy water from land · Cooler water · Fewer small fish
**And then? prompt:** What may happen next? Tap the next card.
**After two steps:** You followed it two steps. Changes can spread further than you'd think.
**Small and big:** A small change may not last. A big or long change can change the reef a lot.

**Land to sea:**
**Teach 4:** Most harm to the sea starts on land. Rain carries litter, mud and chemicals down rivers.
**Strip labels:** hill farm · town · drain · river · river mouth · reef
**Teach 5:** Plastic lasts a long time. It breaks into tiny pieces that animals can swallow.
**Teach 6:** Fishing too much can leave too few fish. Oil spills are rarer, but harmful.

**People help:**
**Teach 7:** People are doing a lot to help. Place each helper on the strip.
**Helper cards:**
- Trees planted by the river hold the mud in.
- Waste water is cleaned before it reaches the river.
- Volunteers clear litter from beaches and riverbanks.
- Some areas are protected, so fish can recover.
- Fishing limits leave enough fish to grow.
- Reef scientists grow young corals and replant them.
**After placing:** Help happens all along the way, from the hills to the reef.

**Keeper's note:** People often say about 80% of ocean pollution comes from land. That's an old, rough estimate. Scientists agree "most" is right, and are working on better numbers.

## How we know (A true thing from history)

**Card 1. HMS Challenger (1872 to 1876):** A British ship sailed around the world measuring the sea. To find the depth, sailors lowered a weighted line and measured it.
**Card 1, more:** In 1875, between Guam and Palau, they measured about 8,184 meters. It was their deepest. The place is named after the ship.
**Card 1, Keeper's note:** They found thousands of animals new to science (sources say about 4,000 to 4,700).

**Card 2. Echo sounding:** Later, ships timed sound instead. The sound goes down, bounces off the floor, and comes back.
**Card 2, the rule:** Depth = how fast sound goes × the time there and back ÷ 2.
**Card 2, model note:** We use 1,500 meters per second. Real sound speed changes a little with the water.

**Card 3. Marie Tharp (1950s):** Marie Tharp turned thousands of depth measurements into maps of the sea floor.
**Card 3, more:** She noticed a valley running along the top of the Atlantic's ridge. Her partner, Bruce Heezen, doubted it at first.
**Card 3, more (Sapling):** Women weren't allowed on the research ships then. She drew the maps from his and others' measurements.
**Card 3, Keeper's note:** Earthquake records lined up with her valley. People still tell the story of her discovery in slightly different ways. Her maps helped scientists accept that the sea floor spreads apart (see the Question Bench).

**Card 4. Trieste (1960):** On 23 January 1960, Jacques Piccard and Don Walsh rode the Trieste to the bottom of the Challenger Deep.
**Card 4, more:** About 5 hours down, 20 minutes at the bottom, about 3½ hours up (Walsh's own figures).

**Card 5. Alvin (1977):** Scientists in the submarine Alvin found hot springs on the deep floor, crowded with tube worms and giant clams.

**Card 6. How much have we seen? (2025):** By 2025, ships had mapped just over a quarter of the floor in detail. People have seen far less than 1% of the deep floor.
**Card 6, Keeper's note:** A 2025 study estimated less than 0.001% of the deep floor has been seen. That's an area about the size of Rhode Island.

### Sounding bench text

**Bench title:** The Sounding Bench
**Always shown:** A simplified picture. Real sound speed changes with temperature, saltiness and depth.
**Line station:** Let out line (500 m) · Reel in
**When it touches:** The line went slack. The weight has touched bottom.
**Slack card (Sapling):** Long lines drift with currents. It's hard to feel the bottom. Old depths were often off.
**Echo station:** Ping · Read the stopwatch
**Echo steps:** 1. Time there and back: {t} seconds. 2. Distance sound went: 1,500 × {t}. 3. Halve it for the depth.

### Tools and jobs

**Teach:** Light and radio don't travel far through water. Sound does.
- **Sonar ship:** maps the floor's shape with sound.
- **Satellite:** sees the whole sea surface, and gives a rough floor map.
- **Submersible:** takes people down to look.
- **ROV:** a robot on a cable. Cameras and arms, no people aboard.
- **Diver:** shallow water only.

### Checking Bramble's claim

**Three questions card:**
1. Is our detailed map of the Moon more complete? Yes, so far.
2. Have people seen much of the deep floor? Very little.
3. Do scientists understand how the deep works? A great deal, with lots still to learn.
**After the card:** So Bramble's line is partly right. But it mixes up mapping, seeing and understanding.
**Bramble's line:** "Fine. 'We've seen more of the Moon's face than the sea floor.' Print that."
**Librarian:** "Much better."

### Set A. Sounding and scale

**A1 (Sprout, order).** Put these in order, from shallow to deep.
Cards: rock pool · shelf · plain · trench.
Answer: rock pool, shelf, plain, trench.
Hint: Start at the shore. Walk out to sea.
Explanation: The sea gets deeper as you go out, but the very deepest places are narrow trenches.
Stuck example: Order a puddle, a bath and a pond.

**A2 (Seedling).** If the sea drained away, what would the floor look like?
A. Flat sand all the way across
**B. Plains, mountains and deep trenches**
C. One deep bowl, deepest in the middle
Misconceptions: A, flat sea floor; C, deepest part is in the middle.
Feedback A: That's Pip's guess. Drain the sea and look again.
Feedback B: Yes. A hidden landscape, like the land.
Feedback C: Ridges are shallower. The deepest trenches are mostly near the edges.
Hint: Look at the profile after you tap Drain the sea.
Explanation: The floor has wide plains, seamounts, a long ridge and narrow trenches.

**A3 (Seedling, guided number).** The echo comes back after 2 seconds. How deep is the floor?
Steps shown: 1. Sound goes 1,500 m each second. 2. In 2 seconds: 1,500 × 2 = 3,000 m. 3. That's there and back. Halve it.
Answer: 1,500 m.
Hint: The sound went down and back up.
Explanation: 3,000 m is the whole trip, so the floor is half that: 1,500 m.

**A4 (Seedling, number).** The echo takes 6 seconds. How deep is the floor?
Answer: 4,500 m (1,500 × 6 = 9,000; half is 4,500).
Hint: Multiply first, then halve.
Explanation: Sound traveled 9,000 m there and back, so the floor is 4,500 m down.
Stuck example: 4 seconds: 1,500 × 4 = 6,000, half is 3,000 m. Now try 6.

**A5 (Sapling).** The echo takes 8 seconds. How deep is the floor?
**A. 6,000 m**
B. 12,000 m
C. 3,000 m
Misconceptions: B, forgot to halve; C, halved twice.
Feedback A: Yes. 12,000 m there and back, so 6,000 m down.
Feedback B: That's the whole trip. The sound came back up too.
Feedback C: You halved twice. Halve just once.
Hint: Work out the whole trip first.
Explanation: 1,500 × 8 = 12,000 m there and back; half is 6,000 m.

**A6 (Sapling, number).** Setup: the Challenger Deep is about 10,935 m deep. Everest is about 8,849 m tall.
Prompt: How much water would be above Everest's peak?
Answer: 2,086 m, about 2 km.
Steps: 10,935 − 8,849 = 2,086.
Hint: Take the mountain away from the depth.
Explanation: Everest would vanish with about 2 km of water on top.

**A7 (Sapling).** Setup: in 1875 Challenger's line read about 8,184 m. Today's figure nearby is about 10,935 m.
Prompt: Why was the old reading smaller?
A. The trench floor sank a long way deeper after 1875.
B. Fathoms are a smaller unit, so the number shrank.
**C. Lines drift, and the deepest spot is easy to miss.**
Misconceptions: A, the floor changes fast; B, a unit change makes a depth smaller.
Feedback A: Trenches change very slowly. A century is far too short.
Feedback B: Their fathoms were turned into meters already. Units don't change the depth.
Feedback C: Yes. Old lines were hard to read, and they may not have been over the deepest point.
Number follow-up: How much less was the 1875 reading? Answer: 2,751 m (10,935 − 8,184).
Hint: Think about lowering a string thousands of meters.
Explanation: Rope soundings were brave but rough. Echo sounding and later dives found the deeper floor.

**A8 (Keeper's Desk, number).** How long would an echo take from 10,935 m down?
Answer: about 14.6 seconds.
Steps: 1. There and back: 2 × 10,935 = 21,870 m. 2. 21,870 ÷ 1,500 = 14.58 s.
Hint: This time, work backward from the depth.
Explanation: Double the depth, then divide by the speed. Real sound speed varies, so this is an estimate.

**A9 (Keeper's Desk, number).** Setup: Trieste took about 5 hours down, 20 minutes at the bottom, 3½ hours up.
Prompt: How long was the whole trip?
Answer: 8 hours 50 minutes (300 + 20 + 210 = 530 minutes).
Hint: Change everything to minutes first.
Explanation: 530 minutes is 8 hours and 50 minutes, about 9 hours, as Don Walsh remembered it.

**C2 (Sapling).** What does an ecosystem include?

## Key terms

- **Bioluminescence:** light made by a living thing.
- **Chemosynthesis:** making food from chemicals, without sunlight.
- **Coral polyp:** a tiny animal that builds a hard cup. Many make a reef.
- **Decomposer:** a living thing that breaks down dead things.
- **Echo sounding:** finding depth by timing sound there and back.
- **Ecosystem:** living things plus the water, light and warmth around them.
- **Marine snow:** bits of dead things and waste sinking to the deep.
- **Pressure:** how hard something pushes. Water pushes harder the deeper you go.
- **Ridge:** a long underwater mountain range.
- **ROV:** a robot sub on a cable, with no people aboard.
- **Sounding:** measuring how deep water is.
- **Trench:** a very deep, narrow valley in the sea floor.
- **Vent:** a hot spring on the deep sea floor.
