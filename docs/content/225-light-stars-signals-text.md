Companion to [spec 225](../specs/225-light-stars-signals.md). Use as written. `{guide}` is the chosen companion and `{courier}` the explorer's courier; the explorer is "you". US spelling, no em-dashes. Facts marked [check] stay out of the build until the source audit clears them.

Not with sunglasses. Not through clouds. Never through binoculars, a telescope or a lens. It can hurt your eyes for good. To see the Sun, a grown-up can help you make its picture on paper with a pinhole, with your back to the Sun.* Lens line, after it on the Ray Bench: *Never use a lens to gather sunlight.*

## Story scenes

Each scene is an optional job. None of them decides anything for the town.

### Scene 1. The lantern in the tower (Act I, chapter 4: mend the village)

**Short:** The storm cracked the tower lantern. You and Dot mend it. Pip flashes it at the bakery. The baker waves back. But what did Pip mean?

**Longer:** The storm had cracked a pane in the post office tower, and rain had got into the old signal lantern. Dot fitted new glass. You polished the mirror behind the flame.

"Let me try it," said Pip. He opened and shut the lantern's shutter, very fast, at the bakery across the lane.

The baker looked up, and waved a floury hand.

"He understood!" said Pip.

"Understood what?" asked {guide}.

Pip thought. "Hello? Or maybe 'bread'? I meant 'bread'."

"Then you need a code," said Dot. "A code you both agreed before you started. Otherwise a flash is just a flash."

Nobody in town remembered who last used the lantern, or what their flashes meant.

**Keeper's note (A true thing from history):** In 1794 France opened a line of towers from Paris to Lille. Each tower had moving wooden arms. Each arm position stood for a sign in an agreed code book.

### Scene 2. Where did the hunter go? (Act II, chapter 2: a garden across the seasons)

**Short:** On Lantern Night, Pip saw three bright stars in a row over the bridge. Now it is summer, and they are gone. "Did they fly away?" asks Pip.

**Longer:** The librarian kept a star page in the almanac, next to the weather page. "On Lantern Night," they said, "the hunter stood over the footbridge. Three stars in a row for his belt."

"I remember," said Pip. "I waved at him."

It was summer now. The garden beds were full of beans. Pip had looked every clear night and found no belt at all.

"Did he fly away?" asked Pip. "Is he cross with me?"

"He's still there," said {guide}. "We're the ones who moved."

Pip looked down at his paws. "I haven't moved. I've been sitting here all evening."

"Bigger than that," said {guide}. "Let's go and spin the Sky Wheel."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** Stars do not change places in a few months. Earth does. As Earth goes around the Sun, the night side of Earth faces a different part of space.

### Scene 3. The picture is upside down (Act II, chapter 4: the first show)

**Short:** The Story Loft has an old magic lantern. Bramble slides in her painted dragon. It appears on the sheet, big and upside down. Everyone laughs, except Bramble.

**Longer:** For the first show, the librarian found a magic lantern in a cupboard: a box with a lamp inside and a fat glass lens at the front. Bramble had painted a dragon on a glass slide, very carefully, with its wings up.

She slid it in. The lamp shone. On the sheet across the loft appeared a huge dragon, upside down, with its wings pointing at the floor.

The audience laughed. Ember laughed loudest. Bramble did not laugh.

"The lens flipped it," said {guide}. "It flips every picture it throws."

"Then I'll paint it upside down," said Bramble, and the magpie fetched her brushes. "On purpose. As if I meant it."

"And if you put a hand over half the lens?" asked Pip. "Do we get half a dragon?"

"Let's find out on the Ray Bench," said {guide}.

**Keeper's note (A true thing from history):** In about 1659 the Dutch scientist Christiaan Huygens drew a skeleton for a lantern show. It was meant to be thrown big by a lamp and lenses. It is the oldest known plan for a magic lantern.

### Scene 4. A radio through the wall (Act III, chapter 4: letters up and down the river)

**Short:** Nuria writes. Her grandmother's radio played inside a stone house. But lamp light can't shine through a wall. Why does one get through?

**Longer:** {courier} brought a short note from Vadoalto, tucked inside the usual letter.

*¡Hola desde Vadoalto!* (Hello from Vadoalto!) My grandmother lived in a big city by the sea. She had a radio in her stone house. The walls were thick as a cow. The radio still played. But when she signaled to her brother with a lamp, he had to come to the window. Light won't go through a wall, but radio will. Why? Also, her radio went quiet inside a metal elevator. Was it shy?

Dot read it twice. "Stone lets one through and stops the other," she said. "And metal stops the radio."

"So it's not the wall," said {guide}. "Or not only the wall. It's the wall and the wave together."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** Radio waves and light are the same kind of thing, in different sizes. A stone wall lets most radio through but stops light. A metal box stops both.

### Scene 5. Old stars and young stars (Act V, path 3: a sky-watching night)

**Short:** On the mill roof, Ember finds her dragon star: a red one in the hunter's shoulder. "It's near the end of its life," says {guide}. "And it's very big."

**Longer:** Hollis's telescope stood on its brass legs, pointing south. Half the village sat on blankets. The miller held the ladder and did not look down.

"There," said Ember. "My dragon star. It's red."

"That one is very big, and near the end of its life," said {guide}. "It has swollen up. One day it will blow apart."

"Tonight?" asked Pip, holding his tail.

"Probably not for a very long time. Nobody knows the day."

"And the little fuzzy patch under the belt?" asked Dot.

"A dusty cloud of gas. New stars are being born in it."

Pip looked from one to the other. "So the sky has babies and grandparents in it at the same time."

"All at once," said {guide}. "That's how we know a star's whole life. We see lots of stars, at every age."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** The red star is Betelgeuse, a red supergiant in Orion. It is expected to end as a supernova. Below the belt lies the Orion Nebula, where new stars are forming.

### Scene 6. A lamp from the mill (Act VI, chapter 3: the long rain)

**Short:** The rain won't stop. The miller wants to signal the bridge from the mill. "Rain hides a quick flash," says Dot. "We need a rain code."

**Longer:** On the second day of the long rain, the miller came up the tower ladder, dripping. "When the boards go in, the bridge should know," she said. "And if the water nears the top mark."

"The tower lamp can see the mill," said Dot. "We'll put a lamp at each end."

"Quick flashes, then," said Pip. "Short, long, short."

Dot shook her head and her hat dripped. "In this rain, a short flash is gone before you see it. Only the long ones show."

"So we need a code where the long flashes alone still say everything," said {guide}. "Even if it's slower."

They wrote the code on two cards, one for the mill and one for the bridge.

The runner's warning still came down the river path. {courier} still carried it on to Léon. The lamps just helped Willowbank talk to itself.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** A good code fits its job. A quick code can fail in noise like rain. A slower code with bigger differences can still get through.

## Teach cards

Each worked example reveals one step per tap.

**T1. A message needs an agreed code** (A true thing about science). A signal is a flash, a sound or a sign. It only carries a message if sender and reader agreed the code first.
Worked example: (1) Pip flashes the lamp. (2) The baker has no code. (3) No message arrives. (4) With a shared card, it would.

**T2. Two different things are enough.** Any two things you can tell apart can make a code: lamp on and off, short and long, up and down.
Worked example: (1) Short flash, long flash. (2) Put them in patterns. (3) Short then long can mean "Come help."

**T3. Many codes, one message.** The same message can go by flag, drum or lamp. The message stays the same. Only the code changes.
Worked example: (1) Message: "All safe." (2) Flag code: flag up. (3) Lamp code: one long flash. (4) Same message.

**T4. Compare codes for the job.** No code is best for every job. Ask: Is it quick? Does it work in rain? Is it easy to learn?
Worked example: (1) Code A is quick. (2) Rain hides its short flashes. (3) Code B is slower. (4) It still works in rain.

**T5. Star patterns** (A true thing about science). People join bright stars into pictures called constellations. The stars are not really joined. They are at very different distances.
Worked example: (1) Find three bright stars in a row. (2) That is the hunter's belt. (3) The hunter is Orion.

**T6. Some patterns never set.** From Willowbank, the Big Dipper and Cassiopeia circle the North Star. They are up every clear night of the year.
Worked example: (1) Find the Big Dipper. (2) Follow the two stars at the end of its bowl. (3) There is the North Star. (4) Cassiopeia is across from it.

**T7. One spin each day.** Earth spins once a day. That is why stars rise in the east and set in the west during one night.
Worked example: (1) Evening: Orion is low in the east. (2) Earth spins. (3) Later, Orion is high in the south.

**T8. One trip around the Sun each year.** At night we face away from the Sun. As Earth moves around the Sun, "away" points at different stars.
Worked example: (1) December midnight: away from the Sun is Orion. (2) June: Earth is across its path. (3) Now the Sun sits in front of Orion.

**T9. Hidden, not gone.** A winter star pattern is still there in summer. It is up in the daytime sky, and sunlight hides it.
Worked example: (1) In June, Orion is up by day. (2) The bright sky hides it. (3) By winter, it is back at night.

**T10. A star is born** (A true thing about science). A star begins in a nebula: a cold, dusty cloud of gas. Gravity pulls the cloud tighter and tighter until the middle is hot enough to shine.
Worked example: (1) A cold cloud. (2) Gravity squeezes it. (3) The middle gets very hot. (4) A new star shines.

**T11. Stars are not on fire.** A star shines by fusion. In its middle, hydrogen is squeezed into helium. That gives out energy. There is no fire and no burning.
Worked example: (1) Hydrogen in the middle. (2) Squeezed together. (3) It becomes helium. (4) Energy streams out as light.

**T12. Mass decides the ending.** A star like the Sun swells, puffs off its outside and leaves a small white dwarf. A much heavier star explodes as a supernova.
Worked example: (1) Same start: a cloud. (2) Sun-like: red giant, then white dwarf. (3) Much heavier: supergiant, then supernova.

**T13. Heavy stars live fast.** Heavier stars use their fuel much faster. A star ten times heavier than the Sun lives about 20 million years. The Sun lives about 10 billion.
Worked example: (1) 10 billion ÷ 20 million. (2) = 500. (3) The Sun's life is about 500 times longer.

**T14. Mirror pictures** (A true thing about science). A flat mirror makes a picture that seems to be behind the glass. It sits behind the glass at the object's own distance. Same size, the right way up.
Worked example: (1) Dot stands 2 steps from the mirror. (2) Light bounces off at equal angles. (3) Trace the bounced rays back. (4) They meet 2 steps behind.

**T15. A lens bends light.** Light bends when it goes into glass at a slant. A lens is curved, so it bends rays from one point back together again.
Worked example: (1) A ray goes in level. (2) The lens bends it. (3) It passes through the focus, F, on the far side.

**T16. Two rays find the picture.** Every point of a lamp sends light everywhere. To find its picture, draw just two easy rays from one point and see where they cross.
Worked example: (1) Ray 1: level, then through F. (2) Ray 2: straight through the center. (3) Where they cross is the picture of the tip.

**T17. Real or virtual.** If the rays really meet, the picture is real: you can catch it on a sheet. If they only seem to meet, it is virtual: no sheet can catch it.
Worked example: (1) Lantern picture: rays meet on the sheet. Real. (2) Mirror picture: rays only seem to meet behind it. Virtual.

**T18. One family of light** (A true thing about science). Radio, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays are all one family. They differ in frequency: how many waves pass each second.
Worked example: (1) Radio: few waves each second, long waves. (2) Visible light: many more. (3) Gamma rays: the most.

**T19. All the same speed.** In empty space, every member of the family travels at the same speed: about 300,000 km (186,000 miles) each second.
Worked example: (1) The Sun is about 150 million km away. (2) 150,000,000 ÷ 300,000 = 500. (3) 500 seconds is 8 minutes 20 seconds, for every rung.

**T20. More energy, more care.** Higher frequency means more energy in each bit. Ultraviolet and higher can harm living cells. Harm depends on the kind of light, its strength and how long it shines.
Worked example: (1) Radio: low energy in each bit. (2) Visible: medium. (3) Ultraviolet and up: enough to harm cells. (4) Very bright light of other kinds can harm too.

**T21. Three things can happen.** When a wave meets a material, some bounces off (reflects), some soaks in (absorbs) and some goes through (transmits). Absorbed energy warms the material.
Worked example: (1) Sunlight hits black cloth. (2) Most soaks in. (3) The cloth warms.

**T22. The material and the wave together.** The same material treats different waves differently. Glass lets light through but stops warm-object infrared. Stone lets radio through but stops light.
Worked example: (1) Stone and light: blocked. (2) Stone and radio: mostly through. (3) So "blocks" depends on the wave too.

**T23. Color is what bounces back.** A red apple reflects red light and absorbs the other colors. Under blue light there is no red to reflect, so it looks dark.
Worked example: (1) White light has all colors. (2) The apple keeps all but red. (3) Red reaches the eye. (4) The apple looks red.

**T24. The first elements** (Keeper's Desk; A true thing about science). In its first minutes, the universe made nearly nothing but hydrogen and helium, plus a little lithium. Every other element came later.
Worked example: (1) 100 kg of early gas. (2) About 3 parts hydrogen to 1 part helium. (3) About 75 kg hydrogen, 25 kg helium.

**T25. Stars build up to iron.** Stars fuse light elements into heavier ones: helium, carbon, oxygen and on up to iron. Each step up to iron gives out energy.
Worked example: (1) Hydrogen to helium. (2) Helium to carbon. (3) In heavy stars: oxygen, silicon. (4) Then iron.

**T26. The iron wall.** Fusing iron takes in energy instead of giving it out. A heavy star's middle can't hold up against gravity any more. It collapses and explodes.
Worked example: (1) The middle fills with iron. (2) No more energy out. (3) It collapses. (4) Supernova.

**T27. Scattered and used again.** Dying stars and supernovas throw their elements into space. New stars and planets form from that dust. Much of the gold probably comes from colliding neutron stars.
Worked example: (1) A star makes iron. (2) A supernova scatters it. (3) A new cloud forms. (4) A planet forms with iron in it.

## Activity (e): Light Ladder

**The ladder.** Opening: Put the family in order, from fewest waves each second to most.
Rungs: radio · microwaves · infrared · visible light · ultraviolet · X-rays · gamma rays.
Guided first item (1 step shown): Visible light sits between infrared and ultraviolet. Place the rest.
Rung cards (A true thing about science, one use and one care each):
- **Radio:** music, news and ship-to-shore talk. Low energy.
- **Microwaves:** heat food in an oven; carry phone and Wi-Fi signals.
- **Infrared:** warm things give it out; TV remotes use it.
- **Visible light:** the thin slice our eyes can see. A rainbow is all of it, spread out.
- **Ultraviolet:** comes from the Sun. Too much harms living cells.
- **X-rays:** pass through soft parts, stopped by bone. Workers stand behind screens.
- **Gamma rays:** the most energy of all. Come from the hottest, wildest places in space.
Speed line: Every rung travels through empty space at the same speed.
Scale line: The real waves range from longer than a house to smaller than an atom. No drawing can show this to scale.
Sun line: The Sun gives out light from all seven rungs. Radiation is natural, not only made by people.

**Window Test.** Opening: Pick a material and a wave. Predict, then test.
Materials: **Window glass** · **Stone wall** · **Metal sheet** · **Black cloth**.
Waves: **Radio** · **Warm-object infrared** · **Visible light**.
Prediction buttons: **Mostly bounces off** · **Mostly soaks in** · **Mostly goes through**.
Fate bar: 10 tokens of energy. Bounced + soaked in + through = 10, always.
Soaked-in line: Soaked-in energy isn't lost. It warms the material.
Cell results (A made-up model; the biggest share is the true part):
- Glass, visible light: mostly goes through. A little bounces off.
- Glass, warm infrared: mostly soaks in. Glass warms.
- Glass, radio: mostly goes through.
- Stone wall, visible light: none goes through. Some bounces off, so you can see the wall. The rest soaks in.
- Stone wall, warm infrared: none goes through.
- Stone wall, radio: most goes through, a bit weaker.
- Metal sheet, visible light: mostly bounces off. That's why it shines.
- Metal sheet, warm infrared: mostly bounces off.
- Metal sheet, radio: mostly bounces off. Radio goes quiet in a metal cabin.
- Black cloth, visible light: mostly soaks in. The cloth warms.
- Black cloth, radio: mostly goes through.
- Black cloth, warm infrared: not on this bench.
X-ray puzzle card (Keeper's Desk): Radio has long waves and goes through stone. Visible light has short waves and stops at stone. X-rays have even shorter waves and go through a fish's soft parts. So is it only about size?
Color card: A red apple under white light, then under blue light. Predict, then switch the lamp.
Model line (A made-up model): The token shares are rounded and made up. Only the biggest share in each cell comes from real tests. Thickness matters too.

## History cards

**Chappe's towers (A true thing from history).** In 1794 France opened a line of tall towers from Paris to Lille. Wooden arms on each tower made signs from a code book. Each tower copied the signs of the one before it.

**Morse's first long message (A true thing from history).** On 24 May 1844 Samuel Morse sent a message by electric wire from Washington to Baltimore. It arrived as raised dots and dashes on a paper strip. Alfred Vail read it at the other end.

**Drums that talk (A true thing from history).** In Yorùbá country in West Africa, drummers play talking drums. Yorùbá words rise and fall in pitch. The drum copies those rises and falls, so listeners can hear words.

**Braille's dots (A true thing from history).** In France in 1824, Louis Braille, aged about 15, made a reading code of raised dots. Each cell has six dot places. He published it in 1829.

**Matariki (A true thing from history).** Māori in Aotearoa New Zealand watch for Matariki, the star cluster also called the Pleiades. When it rises before dawn in midwinter, it marks the new year. Since 2022 it has been a public holiday.

**The picture through a hole (A true thing from history).** Old Chinese writings linked to the thinker Mozi, from more than 2,000 years ago, describe light through a small hole making an upside-down picture. Light goes in straight lines, so the picture flips.

**Huygens's lantern (A true thing from history).** About 1659 Christiaan Huygens sketched a skeleton for a lantern show, thrown big by lenses. Soon traveling showmen were carrying magic lanterns across Europe.

**Beyond the violet (A true thing from history).** In 1800 William Herschel found invisible rays past red: infrared. In 1801 Johann Ritter looked past violet. A chemical darkened there, where no light showed. He had found ultraviolet.

**A bell rung through walls (A true thing from history).** In 1895, in Calcutta (now Kolkata), India, Jagadish Chandra Bose sent very short radio waves through walls. They made a bell ring on the other side.

**Hertz and Röntgen (A true thing from history).** In the late 1880s Heinrich Hertz made radio waves and caught them in his lab. In 1895 Wilhelm Röntgen found X-rays. Both turned out to be light's family.

**Stars as factories (Keeper's note: A true thing from history).** In 1957 four scientists, Margaret and Geoffrey Burbidge, William Fowler and Fred Hoyle, showed how stars could build most elements. Cecilia Payne had shown in 1925 that stars are mostly hydrogen and helium (spec 172).

**Gold from a collision (Keeper's note: A true thing from history).** On 17 August 2017, detectors felt ripples in space from two neutron stars colliding. Telescopes then saw the glow. Its light showed heavy elements had just been made there. Gold was probably among them.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Signal:** a flash, sound or sign sent to someone.
- **Code:** an agreed set of patterns that stand for messages.
- **Message:** what you mean to tell someone.
- **Beat:** one tick of time, used to count flashes.
- **Constellation:** a pattern of stars that people named.
- **Orbit:** a path around something, like Earth's yearly trip around the Sun.
- **Spin:** turning around once, like Earth each day.
- **Nebula:** a dusty cloud of gas in space. Stars are born in some.
- **Galaxy:** a huge group of billions of stars. Not a cloud.
- **Gravity:** the pull that draws things together.
- **Supergiant:** a very heavy star, swollen near the end of its life.
- **North Star:** a star that stays almost still in the north sky while others circle it.
- **Cell:** one of the tiny building blocks of living things.
- **Infrared:** invisible light just past red. Warm things give it out.
- **Ultraviolet:** invisible light just past violet.
- **Telegraph:** a way to send coded messages far, by towers or by wire.
- **Magic lantern:** an old projector: a lamp and a lens that throw a picture on a sheet.
- **Fusion:** squeezing tiny particles together so they join and give out energy.
- **Hydrogen, helium:** the two lightest elements. Most stars are made of them.
- **Red giant:** a star like the Sun, swollen near the end of its life.
- **White dwarf:** the small, hot leftover core of a star like the Sun.
- **Supernova:** the huge explosion of a very heavy star.
- **Neutron star:** a tiny, very heavy leftover core of an exploded star.
- **Mass:** how much stuff something has.
- **Ray:** a line showing the path light takes.
- **Reflect:** bounce off.
- **Refract:** bend when going into a new material at a slant.
- **Lens:** curved glass that bends light rays.
- **Focus (F):** the point where level rays meet after a lens.
- **Image (picture):** where light from an object meets, or seems to meet.
- **Real image:** a picture where rays truly meet. A sheet can catch it.
- **Virtual image:** a picture where rays only seem to meet. No sheet catches it.
- **Frequency:** how many waves pass each second.
- **Wavelength:** the length of one wave.
- **Radiation:** energy carried by light's family, from radio to gamma rays.
- **Spectrum:** the whole family of light, in order.
- **Absorb:** soak in.
- **Transmit:** go through.
- **Element:** a basic substance, like hydrogen, carbon or iron.

## Off-screen invitations (grown-up-led)

1. **Flashlight code.** A grown-up and a child each hold a flashlight in one room. Agree a code for three messages. One sends, the other reads. Then try without agreeing first. (Never shine a light into anyone's eyes.)
2. **Catch a picture.** Indoors, a grown-up holds a magnifying glass between a lit lamp and a sheet of paper across a dim room. Move the paper until the lamp's picture is sharp. Which way up is it? (A lamp only. Never the Sun or a sunny window.)
3. **Find the belt.** On a clear winter evening, a grown-up leads the way outside, on the ground, somewhere safe and dark. Look for three stars in a row. (Never on a roof. Dress warmly. Nothing is written down or recorded.)

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** codes as agreed patterns, and comparing codes for a job; recognizing star patterns, and why some are seasonal (Earth's orbit, not its spin); a star's life, with mass deciding its ending; ray diagrams for a flat mirror and a converging lens, and real versus virtual images; light's family from radio to gamma rays, one speed, and how materials reflect, absorb or transmit each one; and, at the Keeper's Desk, where the elements were made.

**What it does not claim:** the benches are made-up models with stated rules. The rain rule, the token shares, the four-month sky wheel and the perfect lens are simplified pictures, not measurements. **Sun and skin:** Never look at the Sun. Not with sunglasses. Not through clouds. Never through binoculars, a telescope or a lens. It can hurt your eyes for good. To see the Sun, a grown-up can help you make its picture on paper with a pinhole, with your back to the Sun. Ultraviolet light from the Sun can harm skin and eyes; ordinary sun care is a family decision and is not taught on screen.
