## Arriving

Hook (A story from Willowbank): The miller's back room is full of muddy jars, odd springs and a broken speaker. Things change here. Let's find out how.

Doors:

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. The leaky barrel (Act I, chapter 4: mend the village)

**Short:** Hail holed the miller's rain barrel on its ledge in three places. Muddy water squirts out. "The bottom one goes furthest," says Pip. "Why?"

**Longer:** The storm had been hard on the mill. Hail had punched three holes down the side of the miller's rain barrel, up on its stone ledge: one high, one in the middle, one low. Muddy water squirted out of all three.

"The bottom one goes furthest," said Pip, standing well back from it.

"There's more water pressing down above it," said {guide}. "And look: it squirts sideways. Water pushes every way, not just down."

The miller scooped a jug of the brown water. "I can't wash flour sacks in mud," she said. "Can we get the water clean again?"

"We could sieve it," said Pip.

{guide} looked at the sieve, then at the tiny grains of sand. "The holes might be a bit big."

"Then we'll need a finer idea," said the miller, and plugged the top hole with her thumb.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** Water adds about one atmosphere of pressure for every 10 meters of depth. That is why dams are thickest at the bottom.

### Scene 2. Pip and the market scale (Act II, chapter 1: the weekly market)

**Short:** Pip swings on the apple stall's spring scale. Wheee! Now the empty scale says something is in it. "I didn't break it," says Pip. "I lengthened it."

**Longer:** The orchard keeper weighed apples on a hanging spring scale. The hook bobbed up and down. Pip could not resist. He grabbed the hook and swung.

The spring stretched, and stretched, and Pip dropped off with a thump.

The orchard keeper looked at her empty scale. The pointer did not go back to zero. "It thinks there's an apple in it," she said. "There isn't."

"I didn't break it," said Pip. "I lengthened it."

"You stretched it past its limit," said {guide}. "A spring springs back, but only up to a point. Past that, it stays a little longer for good."

Dot peered at the coils. "So how much can a spring take?"

"Let's test one properly," said {guide}. "Small steps. Write everything down."

**Keeper's note (A true thing from history):** In 1676 Robert Hooke hid his spring rule in a scrambled Latin puzzle. In 1678 he gave the answer: as the stretch, so the force.

### Scene 3. The crackling speaker (Act II, chapter 4: the first show)

**Short:** The Story Loft's speaker crackles before the show. The librarian opens it: a paper cone, a coil of wire and a magnet. "How does this make sound?"

**Longer:** An hour before the show, the Story Loft's speaker gave a crackle and went quiet. The librarian unscrewed the back. Inside was a paper cone, a small coil of wire stuck to it, and a heavy ring magnet.

"No music box," said Dot. "Where does the sound come from?"

"The cone," said the librarian. "When it moves in and out, it pushes the air. But what moves the cone?"

{guide} touched the loose wire. "Current goes through the coil. The coil sits in the magnet's field. A wire with current in a magnet's field gets a push."

"Toward the magnet?" asked Pip.

"Sideways," said {guide}. "That's the odd part. Let's try it with one straight wire first."

The librarian found the loose wire and pressed it back. The speaker hummed. The ducks outside went quiet to listen.

**Keeper's note (A true thing from history):** In 1821 Michael Faraday made a wire carrying current circle around a magnet. It is often called the first electric motor.

### Scene 4. The wall that never cracked (Act III, chapter 3: the route by the high ford)

**Short:** On the old path, {courier} finds a stone ring, black inside, and a little hut of clay and straw. Its walls have stood for years.

**Longer:** {courier} led the way. The path to the high ford was faint, but here and there a faded ribbon hung on a branch. Beside one ribbon stood a round stone tower, open at the top and black inside.

"A lime kiln," said Bramble, who had come along to see her grandmother's ribbons. "My grandmother said they burned stone in it."

"You can't burn stone," said Pip.

"You can heat some stones until they break into new stuff," said {guide}. "That's a reaction. A grown-up job, with a very hot fire."

Behind the kiln was a little hut. Its walls were clay with bits of straw sticking out. They had no cracks at all.

Bramble tugged a straw. It held. "Clay alone cracks when it dries," she said. "The straw holds it together."

"Two materials," said {guide}, "doing a job neither could do alone."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** Heating limestone makes a new white solid, quicklime, and carbon dioxide gas. Builders mixed lime with sand and water to make mortar, the paste between stones.

### Scene 5. A smudged letter (Act III, chapter 4: letters up and down the river)

**Short:** Léon's letter arrives damp, as always. His black-ink name has run into blue, red and yellow. "Black ink isn't black?" says Dot.

**Longer:** Léon's letter arrived damp, as usual. Dot unfolded it carefully. Where the water had soaked the paper, his black signature had spread into soft rings of blue, red and yellow.

"Black ink isn't black?" said Dot.

"It's a mixture," said {guide}. "Three dyes. The water carried each one a different distance."

Léon's letter was about salt. *In the hills near us there is a town with a salty spring,* he wrote. *People let the water go into the air, and salt crystals stay behind. My aunt says all salt comes from the sea. I told her it also comes from hills.*

"Is all salt the eating kind?" asked Pip, hopefully.

"In science, 'salt' is a whole family," said {guide}. "Most of them you would never eat. Let's meet some."

**Keeper's note (A true thing from history):** Salies-de-Béarn, in the French Pyrenees foothills, grew up around a salt spring. Its salty water is still dried in big pans to make salt.

## Teach cards

Each worked example reveals one step per tap.

**T1. Pure or mixed?** In science, pure means one substance only. A mixture has two or more, not joined. Pure water boils at one temperature. Salty water boils higher.
Worked example: (1) Muddy water: sand and water. (2) Two substances. (3) A mixture.

**T2. Pick the method from the difference.** A sieve sorts bits by size. A filter catches bits that haven't dissolved. Dissolved salt slips straight through a filter.
Worked example: (1) Muddy water. (2) Sand is not dissolved. (3) Filter it. (4) Clean water drips through.

**T3. Which part do you want?** To keep dissolved salt, evaporate the water. To keep the water, distil: boil it, cool the steam back to water, and collect it.
Worked example: (1) Salty water. (2) Want the water? (3) Boil, then cool the steam. (4) Pure water drips out; salt stays.

**T4. Chromatography.** Put an ink spot above the water line on paper. Water climbs and carries each dye a different distance. Each spot is one dye.
Worked example: (1) Black ink spot. (2) Water climbs the paper. (3) Blue, red and yellow spots. (4) At least three dyes.

**T5. A reaction makes new stuff.** In a reaction, new substances form. Atoms are only rearranged, so the total mass stays the same, gases included.
Worked example: (1) Magnesium + oxygen. (2) → magnesium oxide. (3) 2.4 g + 1.6 g = 4.0 g.

**T6. Burning and gaining oxygen.** Combustion: a fuel burns in oxygen, giving heat and light. Oxidation: something gains oxygen. Rusting is slow oxidation. Burning is fast oxidation.
Worked example: (1) Magnesium burns brightly. (2) It gains oxygen. (3) Combustion and oxidation, both.

**T7. Breaking up and swapping.** Thermal decomposition: heat breaks one substance into new ones. Displacement: a more reactive metal pushes a less reactive one out.
Worked example: (1) Iron nail in blue copper sulfate. (2) Iron is above copper on the ladder. (3) Iron sulfate + copper.

**T8. Three ways an acid reacts.** Acid + metal → salt + hydrogen. Acid + metal oxide → salt + water. Acid + alkali → salt + water too. Neutral is pH 7.
Worked example: (1) Hydrochloric acid + magnesium. (2) A metal, so: salt + hydrogen. (3) Magnesium chloride + hydrogen.

**T9. Naming a salt.** The metal gives the first word. The acid gives the second: hydrochloric makes chloride, sulfuric makes sulfate, nitric makes nitrate.
Worked example: (1) Sulfuric acid + copper oxide. (2) First word: copper. (3) Second word: sulfate. (4) Copper sulfate + water.

**T10. Ceramics.** Fired clay, brick and china are ceramics. They are hard and heat-proof, but brittle: they snap instead of bending. Brittle doesn't mean weak.
Worked example: (1) An oven tile. (2) It must take heat. (3) Ceramic: yes. (4) Don't drop it.

**T11. Polymers.** Polymers are made of very long chains. Some come from living things: rubber, wool, cotton. Some are made: nylon, polythene. Makers tune how they behave.
Worked example: (1) Cling film and a tough rope. (2) Both polymers. (3) Very different jobs.

**T12. Composites.** A composite joins two materials so the whole does what neither part can. Straw in clay stops cracking. Steel in concrete takes the bending.
Worked example: (1) Clay alone: cracks drying. (2) Straw alone: flops. (3) Cob: holds its shape, no cracks.

**T13. Spring back or stay?** An elastic change springs back. A permanent change stays. Past its elastic limit, even a spring stays stretched.
Worked example: (1) Stretch a rubber band: back. (2) Bend a paperclip: stays. (3) Pip's swing: the scale stayed long.

**T14. Force in newtons.** Force is measured in newtons (N), with a newton meter. Mass is in kilograms. On Earth, 100 g weighs about 1 N.
Worked example: (1) A 300 g bag. (2) Each 100 g: about 1 N. (3) About 3 N.

**T15. Extension, not length.** Extension is how much longer the spring got: length now take away length at rest. Each newton adds the same extension, up to a limit.
Worked example: (1) At rest: 10.0 cm. (2) With 2 N: 13.9 cm. (3) 13.9 − 10.0 = 3.9 cm.

**T16. A fair force test** (Keeper's Desk). Change one thing, measure one thing, keep the rest the same. Repeat. Draw one best-fit line through the scatter.
Worked example: (1) Change: the force. (2) Measure: the extension. (3) Keep: the same spring. (4) Repeat on unloading.

**T17. Hot things grow.** When a solid warms, its particles jiggle more and move a little apart. The particles don't grow. A heated ring's hole grows too.
Worked example: (1) Cold rail: particles close. (2) Hot rail: same particles, more space. (3) The rail is longer.

**T18. Pressure pushes every way.** Water and air push on every surface, every way. Deeper water pushes harder, because more water presses down from above.
Worked example: (1) Three holes in a barrel. (2) Bottom: most water above. (3) Bottom jet goes furthest.

**T19. The wire jump.** A current makes magnetism (Sparks and Switches). Put a wire with current across a magnet's field: it is pushed sideways, not toward a pole.
Worked example: (1) Current toward you. (2) North pole on the left. (3) The wire jumps up.

**T20. Flip it.** Reverse the current, or swap the magnets: the push flips. Reverse both: the push is back to the start. Motors and speakers use this push.
Worked example: (1) Start: up. (2) Current reversed: down. (3) Magnets swapped too: up.

## Activity (e): Spring Bench

**Bend or bounce** (Sprout). Opening: Squeeze or bend it. Will it spring back?
Buttons: **Springs back** / **Stays**.

**Spring test.** Opening: Add one newton at a time. Write down the extension.
Labels: **Force (N)** · **Length (cm)** · **Extension (cm)**.
Guided first load (3 steps): (1) Load 2 N. (2) Read the ruler: 13.9 cm. (3) 13.9 − 10.0 = 3.9 cm.
Length-entered reply: That's the whole length. Take away 10.0 cm.
Graph line: Draw one straight line. Let points sit on both sides.
Join-the-dots reply: Real readings wobble. One line through the middle shows the pattern.
Bend line (shown when the 6 N point lands off the line): The line bends after 5 N. The rule stops.
Unload line (after 7 N): Back to 0 N. Length 12.0 cm, so the extension is 2.0 cm. It went past its elastic limit.

**Pull Cart** (Keeper's Desk). Opening: Predict the speed gain. Then pull.
Buttons: mass **2 kg** / **4 kg**; pull **2 N** / **4 N** / **6 N**; **Pull from rest**.
Readout: Predicted {p} m/s each second. Measured {m} m/s each second.
Gap line: Friction pushes back 0.4 N. The push left over is smaller.
Model line (A made-up model): Tidy made-up numbers. Real carts and springs wobble more.

## History cards

**Mary's bath (A true thing from history).** Mary the Jewess worked in Alexandria, Egypt, between about 1,700 and 2,000 years ago. Later writers credit her with a still and a warm water bath. We know her only through them.

**Tsvet's chalk column (A true thing from history).** In the early 1900s Mikhail Tsvet poured a green extract of leaves through a tube of chalk powder. Bands of yellow, orange and green appeared. He named it chromatography, from Greek words for color and writing.

**The salt spring (A true thing from history).** At Salies-de-Béarn, salty spring water is dried in big pans. The water goes into the air; salt stays.

**The salt spring boar (A story people tell).** People there tell of a wounded wild boar found coated in salt by a marsh. They say that is how the spring was found.

**Shen Kuo's bitter spring (A true thing from history).** Around 1088, Shen Kuo wrote about a bitter spring in China. Heating its water left crystals. He wrote that an iron pan used to heat them turned to copper. Today we know iron swapped places with copper and coated the pan.

**Lavoisier's sealed tin (A true thing from history).** In 1774 Antoine Lavoisier heated tin in a sealed glass vessel. Some of the tin changed to a black powder. The whole vessel weighed the same. Nothing was lost.

**The first rubber (A true thing from history).** By about 3,600 years ago, people in Mexico and Central America made rubber. They mixed tree sap with morning glory juice. The juice made it bouncier.

**The composite bow (A true thing from history).** Riders of the Asian grasslands made bows of wood, horn and sinew glued together. Horn takes the squeeze. Sinew takes the pull.

**Monier's flowerpots (A true thing from history).** Joseph Monier was a gardener in Paris. His concrete pots cracked. He set iron mesh inside them, and in 1867 he patented the idea.

**Kwolek's fiber (A true thing from history).** In 1964-65, chemist Stephanie Kwolek made a new polymer at DuPont. Spun into fiber, it became Kevlar: light, strong and used in protective vests.

**Hooke's puzzle (A true thing from history).** In 1676 Robert Hooke published his spring rule as a scrambled puzzle. Two years later he gave the answer: as the stretch, so the force.

**Faraday's turning wire (A true thing from history).** On 3 September 1821, in London, Michael Faraday made a wire carrying current circle around a magnet. It is often called the first electric motor.

**The paper cone speaker (A true thing from history).** In 1925, engineers Chester Rice and Edward Kellogg described a speaker with a paper cone driven by a coil. Most speakers still work this way.

### Set 1. The clean-up (Sprout 1.1, Seedling 1.2, Sapling 1.3-1.5, Keeper's Desk 1.6)

**1.1 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: The miller wants clean water from muddy water. Which tool?
- A. A sieve *(misconception: any separating tool works)*
- B. Filter paper *(right)*
- C. A dish in the sun *(misconception: evaporate-keeps-liquid)*
Hint: Sand grains are tiny. Which tool catches tiny bits?
Feedback: A: The sand slips through the holes. B: Yes. The sand stays; clean water drips through. C: The water would go into the air.
Explanation: Sand hasn't dissolved, so filter paper catches it.

**1.2 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Salty water goes through filter paper. What drips out?
- A. Water that is still salty *(right)*
- B. Clean water, with the salt left on the paper *(misconception: filter-catches-dissolved)*
- C. Nothing at all, because the paper soaks it up *(misconception: filters stop liquids)*
Hint: Is the salt dissolved?
Feedback: A: Yes. Dissolved salt slips through. B: Dissolved salt is too tiny to catch. C: Water passes through filter paper.
Explanation: A filter only catches bits that haven't dissolved.

**1.3 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: You want the water back from salty water. What do you do?
- A. Leave it in an open dish *(misconception: evaporate-keeps-liquid)*
- B. Filter it twice *(misconception: filter-catches-dissolved)*
- C. Distil it: boil, cool the steam, collect *(right)*
Hint: Which part do you want to keep?
Feedback: A: The water escapes into the air. B: Salt passes through every time. C: Yes. The steam turns back to pure water.
Explanation: Distilling keeps the liquid. Evaporating keeps the solid.

**1.4 (Sapling, order the cards)**
Prompt: Sand, salt and water. Put the steps in order to get dry salt.
Cards: Collect the salt crystals · Filter out the sand · Let the salty water evaporate
Answer: Filter out the sand → Let the salty water evaporate → Collect the salt crystals.
Misconception tested: one-step-enough.
Hint: What has to come out first?
Explanation: Filter the sand first. Then evaporate the water to leave the salt.

**1.5 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: A black ink spot spreads into blue, red and yellow. What does it show?
- A. The paper added the colors *(misconception: paper-adds-color)*
- B. The ink is a mixture of at least three dyes *(right)*
- C. The ink is one pure substance *(misconception: one color means one substance)*
Hint: Where were the colors before the water came?
Feedback: A: Plain paper stays white. B: Yes. Each spot is a dye. C: One substance would make one spot.
Explanation: The water carried each dye a different distance.

**1.6 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Given: at sea level, pure water boils at 100 °C.
Prompt: Which jar holds pure water?
- A. Jar 1: boils at 100 °C and stays there *(right)*
- B. Jar 2: boils at 102 °C, then climbs *(misconception: higher-boil-purer)*
- C. Neither: you can only tell by tasting *(misconception: tasting tests purity)*
Hint: A pure substance boils at one sharp temperature.
Feedback: A: Yes. One sharp boiling point. B: A rising boiling point means a mixture. C: Never taste. The thermometer tells you.
Explanation: Jar 2 has something dissolved in it, so it boils higher and over a range.

### Set 2. The reaction shelf (Sapling 2.1-2.3, Keeper's Desk 2.4-2.5)

**2.1 (Sapling, sort)**
Prompt: Sort each change into its family.
Cards: a nail rusting · green copper carbonate heated, turning black · an iron nail in copper sulfate gets a copper coat · natural gas burning on a stove
Answer: nail → oxidation; copper carbonate → thermal decomposition; iron nail in copper sulfate → displacement; gas → combustion (oxidation also accepted).
Misconception tested: sorting by looks, not evidence.
Hint: Did it gain oxygen, break apart, swap, or burn?
Explanation: Gaining oxygen is oxidation. Heat breaking one thing apart is decomposition. A swap is displacement. Burning is combustion.

**2.2 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Magnesium burns brightly and makes magnesium oxide. Which family?
- A. Only combustion *(misconception: categories-exclusive)*
- B. Combustion and oxidation *(right)*
- C. Thermal decomposition *(misconception: heat-means-decomposition)*
Hint: Did it burn? Did it gain oxygen?
Feedback: A: It also gained oxygen. B: Yes. Burning is fast oxidation. C: Nothing broke apart. Oxygen joined on.
Explanation: A reaction can sit in two families. Burning magnesium does.

**2.3 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Ice is heated and melts. Is that thermal decomposition?
- A. Yes, because heat changed it *(misconception: heat-means-decomposition)*
- B. Yes, because it broke into water *(misconception: changing state is breaking up)*
- C. No, it is still water *(right)*
Hint: Is there a new substance?
Feedback: A: Heat can change things without a reaction. B: Ice and water are the same substance. C: Yes. No new substance, no reaction.
Explanation: Melting changes state only. Decomposition makes new substances.

**2.4 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Given: the ladder, top to bottom: magnesium, zinc, iron, copper, silver.
Prompt: Copper goes into magnesium sulfate. What happens?
- A. Nothing happens *(right)*
- B. Copper pushes the magnesium out *(misconception: any-metal-swaps)*
- C. The copper dissolves away *(misconception: metals vanish in solutions)*
Hint: Which metal is higher?
Feedback: A: Yes. Copper is lower, so no swap. B: Only a higher metal pushes a lower one out. C: Copper stays as it was.
Explanation: Displacement goes one way: a more reactive metal pushes out a less reactive one.

**2.5 (Keeper's Desk, number with steps)**
Given: 2.4 g of magnesium burns. It makes 4.0 g of magnesium oxide.
Prompt: How much oxygen joined the magnesium?
Answer: 1.6 g.
Steps: (1) After: 4.0 g. (2) Before, magnesium only: 2.4 g. (3) 4.0 − 2.4 = 1.6 g.
Misconception tested: burning-destroys-mass (expecting less than 2.4 g).
Hint: The extra mass came from the air.
Explanation: Mass is kept. The oxide weighs more because oxygen joined.

### Set 3. The salt maker (Sapling 3.1-3.3, Keeper's Desk 3.4-3.5)

**3.1 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Acid + metal makes a salt and what else?
- A. Water *(misconception: same-products-every-acid)*
- B. Nothing else *(misconception: the salt is the only product)*
- C. Hydrogen *(right)*
Hint: Remember the squeaky pop.
Feedback: A: Water comes with alkalis and oxides. B: There's a gas too. C: Yes. Salt + hydrogen.
Explanation: Acid + metal → salt + hydrogen.

**3.2 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Hydrochloric acid + sodium hydroxide makes what?
- A. Sodium chloride + water *(right)*
- B. Sodium chloride + hydrogen *(misconception: same-products-every-acid)*
- C. Nothing; they cancel out *(misconception: neutral-means-nothing-left)*
Hint: Sodium hydroxide is an alkali.
Feedback: A: Yes. Acid + alkali → salt + water. B: Hydrogen comes from metals. C: A salt and water are left.
Explanation: Neutralizing makes a salt and water.

**3.3 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Sulfuric acid + zinc. Which salt forms?
- A. Zinc sulfide *(misconception: ending-mix-up)*
- B. Zinc chloride *(misconception: every salt is a chloride)*
- C. Zinc sulfate *(right)*
Hint: Sulfuric acid gives which ending?
Feedback: A: Sulfuric makes sulfate. B: Chloride comes from hydrochloric acid. C: Yes. Zinc, then sulfate.
Explanation: The metal gives the first word. The acid gives the second.

**3.4 (Keeper's Desk, build the equation)**
Prompt: Build the word equation for copper oxide and sulfuric acid.
Tiles: copper oxide · sulfuric acid · copper sulfate · copper chloride · water · hydrogen · +  · →
Answer: copper oxide + sulfuric acid → copper sulfate + water.
Steps: (1) A metal oxide: salt + water. (2) First word: copper. (3) Second word: sulfate.
Misconception tested: same-products-every-acid (choosing hydrogen); ending-mix-up.
Hint: Is copper oxide a metal or a metal oxide?
Explanation: Acid + metal oxide → salt + water. The salt is copper sulfate.

**3.5 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: A neutral solution's water evaporates. What is left in the dish?
- A. Nothing, because neutral means empty *(misconception: neutral-means-nothing-left)*
- B. Crystals of a salt *(right)*
- C. Table salt, whatever acid you used *(misconception: salt-is-table-salt)*
Hint: Where did the salt go when it dissolved?
Feedback: A: Neutral means balanced, not empty. B: Yes. The salt was there all along. C: Table salt is one salt of many.
Explanation: Evaporating shows the salt made by the reaction. Never taste it.

### Set 4. The right stuff (Sprout 4.1, Seedling 4.2-4.3, Sapling 4.4, Keeper's Desk 4.5)

**4.1 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which is best for a dish that goes in a hot oven?
- A. A plastic dish *(misconception: any material works for any job)*
- B. A clay dish *(right)*
- C. A wax dish *(misconception: hard when cold means hard when hot)*
Hint: Which one won't soften in the heat?
Feedback: A: Many plastics soften in an oven. B: Yes. Fired clay takes the heat. C: Wax melts in the heat.
Explanation: Ceramics like fired clay stay hard when hot.

**4.2 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Clay tiles are hard and heat-proof. Why do they shatter when dropped?
- A. They are brittle: they snap, not bend *(right)*
- B. They are soft and weak inside *(misconception: brittle-means-weak)*
- C. The fall made them very hot *(misconception: breaking needs heat)*
Hint: Can a tile bend a little before it breaks?
Feedback: A: Yes. Hard, but brittle. B: Tiles are hard and strong when squashed. C: A short fall barely warms them.
Explanation: Brittle means it snaps instead of bending. It doesn't mean weak.

**4.3 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which is true about polymers?
- A. They are all made in factories *(misconception: all-polymers-made)*
- B. They are all weak and flimsy *(misconception: plastic is flimsy)*
- C. Some come from living things, like rubber and wool *(right)*
Hint: Think of a sheep and a rubber tree.
Feedback: A: Rubber and wool are natural polymers. B: Nylon rope is strong enough to tow a cart. C: Yes. Natural or made, both count.
Explanation: Polymers are long chains, from living things or factories.

**4.4 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Why mix straw into clay for a hut wall?
- A. Clay holds the shape; straw stops it cracking *(right)*
- B. It's just a mixture; the straw does nothing *(misconception: composite-is-just-mixture)*
- C. The straw makes it harder than stone *(misconception: a composite beats everything)*
Hint: What does clay alone do as it dries?
Feedback: A: Yes. Each part does a job. B: Without straw, the clay cracks. C: It isn't harder. It just doesn't crack.
Explanation: A composite combines what each part does well.

**4.5 (Keeper's Desk, say why, to a grown-up)**
Prompt: A bike frame is carbon fiber in resin. Why not just one?
Answer to listen for: the fibers are strong and stiff when pulled but floppy alone; the resin holds them in shape; together the frame is stiff and light.
Misconception tested: composite-is-just-mixture ("it's just stronger").
Hint: What does each part do the other can't?
Explanation: Neither part alone is stiff and light. Together they are.

### Set 5. Springs and pulls (Sprout 5.1, Seedling 5.2, Sapling 5.3-5.6, Keeper's Desk 5.7-5.8)

**5.1 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which one keeps its new shape after you bend it?
- A. A rubber band *(misconception: all bending stays)*
- B. A sponge *(misconception: soft things stay squashed)*
- C. A paperclip *(right)*
Hint: Which one doesn't spring back?
Feedback: A: A rubber band springs back. B: A sponge springs back. C: Yes. It stays bent.
Explanation: Rubber bands and sponges are elastic. A bent paperclip stays bent.

**5.2 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: After Pip's swing, the scale's spring stays longer. Why?
- A. It went past its elastic limit *(right)*
- B. Every stretch lasts forever *(misconception: all-stretch-permanent)*
- C. It is tired and will rest back *(misconception: springs-rest-back)*
Hint: Small stretches spring back. What about big ones?
Feedback: A: Yes. Past the limit, it stays. B: Small stretches spring back. C: Resting won't fix it.
Explanation: A spring is elastic only up to its elastic limit.

**5.3 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: A 500 g bag hangs on a newton meter. About what does it read?
- A. 500 N *(misconception: mass-is-force)*
- B. About 5 N *(right)*
- C. About 0.5 N *(misconception: decimal slip)*
Hint: On Earth, 100 g weighs about 1 N.
Feedback: A: That's the grams, not newtons. B: Yes. Five lots of 100 g. C: Check: five lots of 100 g.
Explanation: 500 g is five lots of 100 g, so about 5 N (4.9 N).

**5.4 (Sapling, number with steps)**
Given: the spring is 10 cm long at rest. With a load, it is 16 cm.
Prompt: What is the extension?
Answer: 6 cm.
Steps: (1) Length now: 16 cm. (2) At rest: 10 cm. (3) 16 − 10 = 6 cm.
Misconception tested: total-length-not-extension (answering 16).
Hint: How much longer did it get?
Explanation: Extension is the extra length, not the whole length.

**5.5 (Sapling, number with steps)**
Given: 1 N gives 2 cm of extension. 2 N gives 4 cm.
Prompt: Below the limit, what extension will 4 N give?
Answer: 8 cm.
Steps: (1) Each newton adds 2 cm. (2) 4 × 2 = 8 cm.
Misconception tested: "the length doubles each newton" instead of the same extension added each newton.
Hint: How much does each extra newton add?
Explanation: The same extension is added for every newton, up to the limit.

**5.6 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Your points wobble a little. How do you draw the line?
- A. Join every dot *(misconception: join-the-dots)*
- B. Through the first and last dots only *(misconception: two points decide the line)*
- C. One straight line, dots on both sides *(right)*
Hint: Real readings have small errors.
Feedback: A: Joining dots copies the wobbles. B: Use all the dots, not two. C: Yes. A best-fit line.
Explanation: A best-fit line averages the wobbles and shows the pattern.

**5.7 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Given: 0 to 6 N gave 0.0, 2.1, 3.9, 6.1, 8.0, 10.0, 13.5 cm.
Prompt: Where does the straight-line pattern stop?
- A. After 5 N *(right)*
- B. It never stops *(misconception: proportional-forever)*
- C. After 1 N *(misconception: small wobbles are the limit)*
Hint: Look for a jump much bigger than 2 cm.
Feedback: A: Yes. 10.0 to 13.5 is a 3.5 cm jump. B: The last jump is much bigger. C: Those are small wobbles.
Explanation: Up to 5 N, each newton adds about 2 cm. Then the line bends.

**5.8 (Keeper's Desk, number with steps)**
Given: a 2 kg cart starts from rest. You pull with 6 N.
Prompt: Predict the speed gain each second, ignoring friction.
Answer: 3 m/s each second.
Steps: (1) Force ÷ mass. (2) 6 ÷ 2 = 3. (3) 3 m/s faster each second.
Misconception tested: force sets a speed, not a change in speed.
Hint: Divide the force by the mass.
Explanation: The bench measures 2.8. Friction pushes back 0.4 N, so the push left over is 5.6 N.

### Set 6. Heat, depth and the wire jump (Sprout 6.1, Sapling 6.2-6.3 and 6.6, Keeper's Desk 6.4-6.5 and 6.7)

**6.1 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: The barrel stands on a high ledge. Which jet goes furthest?
- A. The top hole *(misconception: pressure-only-down, top pushes hardest)*
- B. They all go the same *(misconception: pressure is the same everywhere)*
- C. The bottom hole *(right)*
Hint: Which hole has the most water above it?
Feedback: A: Little water presses above the top hole. B: Watch again. They differ. C: Yes. Deepest water pushes hardest.
Explanation: Deeper water pushes harder.

**6.2 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: A metal rail warms up and gets longer. What do its particles do?
- A. Each particle gets bigger *(misconception: particles-grow)*
- B. They jiggle more and move apart *(right)*
- C. New particles are made *(misconception: heat makes matter)*
Hint: Do particles change size?
Feedback: A: Particles keep their size. B: Yes. More space, same particles. C: No new particles appear.
Explanation: Expansion is more space between the same particles.

**6.3 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: A metal ring is heated. What happens to the hole?
- A. It shrinks *(misconception: hole-shrinks)*
- B. It gets bigger *(right)*
- C. It stays the same *(misconception: only the metal changes)*
Hint: Every bit of the ring moves apart from its neighbors.
Feedback: A: The metal doesn't grow inward. B: Yes. Everything spreads out. C: The whole ring grows, hole too.
Explanation: Every particle moves apart, so the ring and its hole both grow.

**6.4 (Keeper's Desk, number with steps)**
Given: steel grows about 12 millionths of its length for each 1 °C.
Prompt: A 50 m steel rail warms by 40 °C. How much longer?
Answer: 0.024 m, which is 2.4 cm.
Steps: (1) 50 × 40 = 2,000. (2) 2,000 × 0.000012 = 0.024 m. (3) 0.024 m = 2.4 cm.
Misconception tested: expecting no measurable change (gaps as decoration).
Hint: Multiply length, warming and the growth per degree.
Explanation: That's why rails and bridges need gaps.

**6.5 (Keeper's Desk, number with steps)**
Given: every 10 m of water adds about one atmosphere. Air adds one at the surface.
Prompt: A diver is 20 m down. About how many atmospheres in all?
Answer: about 3.
Steps: (1) 20 ÷ 10 = 2 from the water. (2) Plus 1 from the air. (3) 2 + 1 = 3.
Misconception tested: forgetting the air above the water (answering 2).
Hint: Don't forget the air pressing on the water.
Explanation: Water and air both press on the diver.

**6.6 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: The wire jumped up. You reverse the current. What now?
- A. It jumps down *(right)*
- B. It jumps toward the north pole *(misconception: pulled-to-a-pole)*
- C. It still jumps up *(misconception: current direction doesn't matter)*
Hint: One flip in, one flip out.
Feedback: A: Yes. The push flips. B: The push goes across the field, not to a pole. C: Reversing the current flips the push.
Explanation: Reverse the current and the push reverses.

**6.7 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: You reverse the current and swap the magnets. Which way now?
- A. Down, because you flipped things *(misconception: two-reversals-flip)*
- B. Toward a pole *(misconception: pulled-to-a-pole)*
- C. Up, as at the start *(right)*
Hint: What do two flips make?
Feedback: A: Two flips cancel out. B: The push is always across the field. C: Yes. Two flips, back to the start.
Explanation: Each reversal flips the push. Two reversals cancel.

## "I'm stuck" worked examples (step 2 of the ladder)

- **Filter or sieve:** Flour with lumps. Which tool? Start: are the lumps bigger than flour grains?
- **Which part:** Sugar water. You want the sugar. Start: the water can go into the air.
- **Order:** Pebbles, sand and water. Start: pour through the sieve first, then filter.
- **Chromatography:** A green spot splits into blue and yellow. How many dyes? Start: count the spots.
- **Families:** A copper pan turns black when heated in air. Start: did it gain oxygen?
- **Mass:** 10 g of metal becomes 14 g of oxide. Start: 14 − 10 = 4 g of oxygen joined.
- **Ladder:** Zinc into copper sulfate. Start: is zinc above copper?
- **Salts:** Nitric acid + magnesium oxide. Start: an oxide gives salt + water; nitric gives nitrate.
- **Materials:** A rope for a boat. Start: does it need to bend, or stay stiff?
- **Composite:** Steel bars in a concrete path. Start: what does concrete do when bent?
- **Spring back:** A ball of play dough squashed flat. Start: did it spring back?
- **Extension:** At rest 8 cm, now 11 cm. Start: 11 − 8 = 3 cm.
- **Newtons:** A 200 g apple bag. Start: two lots of 100 g, about 2 N.
- **Expansion:** A metal lid stuck on a jar. Start: warm metal moves apart a little.
- **Depth:** Two holes, one high, one low. Start: which has more water above?
- **Wire jump:** The wire jumped down. You swap the magnets. Start: one flip.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Pure substance:** one substance only, with nothing mixed in.
- **Mixture:** two or more substances together, not joined.
- **Dissolve:** to break up too small to see and spread through a liquid.
- **Filter:** paper that lets liquid through and catches bits that haven't dissolved.
- **Evaporate:** to change from liquid into gas, into the air.
- **Crystal:** a solid with flat faces, left when dissolved stuff comes back.
- **Distil:** to boil a liquid off, cool it back to liquid and collect it.
- **Condense:** to change from gas back into liquid.
- **Chromatography:** separating colors by letting a liquid carry them up paper.
- **Atom:** one of the tiny bits every substance is built from.
- **Reaction:** a change that makes new substances.
- **Oxygen:** a gas in the air that many things react with.
- **Hydrogen:** a very light gas. It pops near a flame.
- **Methane:** the main gas burned in gas stoves.
- **Limestone:** a rock made mostly of calcium carbonate.
- **Kiln:** a very hot oven for baking clay or heating stone.
- **Word equation:** a reaction written in words, like A + B → C.
- **Combustion:** when a fuel burns in oxygen, giving heat and light.
- **Oxidation:** a substance gaining oxygen. Rusting is slow oxidation.
- **Thermal decomposition:** heat breaking one substance into new ones.
- **Displacement:** a more reactive metal pushing a less reactive one out.
- **Reactive:** how readily something takes part in reactions.
- **Acid:** a substance with a pH below 7.
- **Alkali:** a substance that dissolves in water to give a pH above 7.
- **Neutral:** pH 7, neither acid nor alkali.
- **Neutralize:** to add an alkali to an acid (or the other way) until it is neutral.
- **Salt:** what an acid makes with a metal, alkali or oxide. Table salt is one.
- **Ceramic:** a hard, heat-proof material like fired clay, often brittle.
- **Brittle:** snaps instead of bending.
- **Polymer:** a material made of very long chains, natural or made.
- **Composite:** two materials joined so the whole does what neither can alone.
- **Resin:** a sticky liquid that sets hard, used to hold fibers.
- **Fiber:** a long, thin thread of material.
- **Patent:** an official paper saying an idea belongs to its inventor for a time.
- **Sinew:** tough cord from an animal's body that joins muscle to bone.
- **Reinforced concrete:** concrete with steel bars or mesh inside.
- **Elastic:** springs back to its shape.
- **Elastic limit:** the most a thing can stretch and still spring back.
- **Newton (N):** the unit of force.
- **Newton meter:** a spring tool that measures force in newtons.
- **Extension:** how much longer a spring got.
- **Best-fit line:** one line drawn through the middle of scattered points.
- **Expand:** to get bigger. **Contract:** to get smaller.
- **Pressure:** how hard something pushes on each bit of a surface.
- **Atmosphere (pressure):** about how hard the air pushes at sea level.
- **Current:** the flow of electricity around a circuit.
- **Magnetic field:** the space around a magnet where it pushes or pulls.
- **Motor effect:** the push on a wire with current in a magnet's field.
- **Motor:** a coil that turns when pushed by the motor effect.
- **Loudspeaker:** a cone moved in and out by the motor effect, making sound.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** choosing a separation method from what differs and which part is wanted; four families of reaction, with mass kept; how acids make salts, and how to name them; choosing ceramics, polymers and composites for a job; elastic and permanent change, expansion and fluid pressure; a fair spring test with extension and a best-fit line; and the push on a wire with current across a magnet's field.

**What it does not claim:** the benches are made-up models with stated rules. The spring readings, cart numbers, gaps and rig results are simplified pictures, not measurements. Symbol equations, moles, F = ke and why magnetic fields push are left for later. Nothing here is safety advice about chemicals, kilns, ovens or electrical devices. The pack teaches the investigation on screen; real lab work belongs with a teacher.

**Safety:** Acids, flames, heating and electricity in these benches stay on the screen. Off screen, a grown-up leads, and nothing from a bench is ever tasted.
