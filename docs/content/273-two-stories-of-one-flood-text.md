# Two Stories of One Flood: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 273](../specs/273-two-stories-of-one-flood.md). Use as written, after review. `{guide}` is never gendered. Pronouns: Pip, Hob and the baker he/him; Dot, the miller, the orchard keeper and Bramble she/her; the librarian and Hollis they/them; the magpie "it". Samuel Pepys (a real person) he/him. Sources: [audit](../source-audits/273-two-stories-of-one-flood.md). Nothing is scored, timed or locked.

**Hollis and page rule:** scenes 1 to 3, benches (a) to (f) in their standalone forms and sets A, B, D, E and F never name Hollis and read no page. Scene 4, scene 5, the Act IV versions of benches (b) and (c), and set C open only after page 4 comes free (opening of Act IV).

**Labels used throughout:**
- **A story from Willowbank** marks the town, its people and everything they write (fiction).
- **A true thing about language** marks teach cards about accounts, narrators and purpose.
- **A true thing from history** marks Samuel Pepys, the Great Fire of London and the *London Gazette* of 1666.

Quoted lines from Pepys keep the old spelling of Henry B. Wheatley's 1893 edition (Pepys wrote in shorthand, so the spelling is the editors'). Each sits beside a line in today's US English.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. The storm night, twice told (Act II, "so next time we'll know")

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** The town wants its own records now. In the mill's book, the miller finds the note she wrote on storm night. Dot brings her old notice.

**Short:**
> The miller wrote her note during the storm. Dot wrote her notice a week later. Both are about the same night. They tell it differently.

**Longer:**
> The miller turns the mill book around so everyone can see. Her writing goes uphill across the page.
>
> "I wrote that by lamplight," she says. "The cat was on my lap. I could hardly hold the pen."
>
> Dot unfolds her notice from the Post Office wall. It is neat and square.
>
> "I wasn't at the mill," says Dot. "I asked everyone what they saw, a week later, and wrote it all down."
>
> "Yours has the footbridge," says the miller. "I never saw the footbridge. I couldn't see past my own window."
>
> "Yours has the cat," says Dot. "Nobody told me about the cat."
>
> {guide} lays the two pages side by side. "Same night. Two windows on it."

**The miller's note** (A story from Willowbank; written in the mill book on storm night):
> Storm night. Writing by lamp at the mill. The brook is over the second flood board. I pulled the third board in at nine. Rain on the roof like gravel. The cat will not leave my lap. Water at the bottom step now, and rising. I can see no further than the window.

**Dot's notice** (A story from Willowbank; pinned up at the Post Office a week later):
> NOTICE. On the night of the storm, Willow Brook rose over the mill's flood boards. Water reached the mill steps. The footbridge was damaged. The low road was under water until morning. People say it was the worst storm in years. The Post Office is open again. Letters may be late. Dot, Postmaster.

### Scene 2. The missing ribbon (Act II ch. 3, Ember's birthday)

**Hook:** At Ember's birthday, the red ribbon vanishes from Bramble's gate. Three people tell what happened. Then the magpie tells its own version.

**Short:**
> A ribbon goes missing at Ember's birthday. Pip, Dot and the whole party each know a different part. The magpie knows the most, and says the least.

**Longer:**
> The café is full of cake and candles when Bramble stops at the door.
>
> "My ribbon," she says. "The red one. It was on the gate."
>
> Pip thinks the wind took it. Dot noticed that not a leaf was moving. Ember noticed nothing, because of the candles.
>
> The magpie sits on the café sign, looking at the sky in a very innocent way.
>
> "Shall we write down what everyone saw?" says {guide}.
>
> "Write down what I saw," says the magpie. "I have a version."
>
> Bramble folds her arms. "I'm sure you do."

**Pip tells it** (first person):
> I was guarding the cake. Well, looking at it. Something flashed red past the window. When I looked at the gate, Bramble's ribbon was gone. I blamed the wind. Then I had more cake.

**Dot's side** (third person, limited to Dot):
> Dot was hanging the birthday banner when she glanced at the gate. Its red ribbon was gone. She looked at the willow. Not a leaf was moving. So where had it gone?

**The whole party** (third person, all-knowing):
> Dot wondered where the ribbon had gone. Pip blamed the wind, and was secretly glad of the extra cake. Bramble hadn't noticed yet. And up in the willow, the magpie was tucking something red into its nest, feeling extremely clever.

**The magpie's version** (first person, unreliable):
> Let me tell you what really happened. I was nowhere near the gate. I was being helpful, as usual. I did not untie any ribbon. The knot was very tight, anyway, and it took ages. Later I found a ribbon in my nest. Ribbons blow about, you know. Nobody felt any wind, but ribbons are like that. I kept it safe. You're welcome.

**Ending line:** Bramble holds out her hand. Slowly, the magpie gives back the ribbon.

### Scene 3. Pip and the scratching (spring, Flood Boards and Planting)

**Hook:** Pip offers to sleep at the mill and guard the new flood boards. In the morning, he has a story to tell.

**Short:**
> Pip hears scratching at the mill door all night. He is sure it is a monster. The clues say something else.

**Pip's story** (first person; Pip believes every word):
> Something scratched at the mill door. Scratch, scratch, scratch. A monster, I was sure of it. I bravely hid under the flour sacks. It went on for ages. In the morning there were little paw prints in the spilled flour. The miller's cat was asleep by the door, with flour on its paws. I still say it was a monster.

### Scene 4. Page 4 and the archive column (Act IV ch. 1, after page 4 comes free)

**Hook:** Page 4 is dry. The librarian has already written about it for the *Willowbank Gazette*. Pip reads both, then reads them again.

**Short:**
> Hollis wrote page 4 in the years it happened. The librarian wrote about it much later. They tell the same years, with different things left in.

**Longer:**
> Pip reads page 4 out loud. Then the librarian hands him this week's *Gazette*, folded open at a column called *From the Archive*.
>
> "I wrote that from page 4," says the librarian. "I wasn't there. I wasn't even born."
>
> Pip reads it. "It's tidier than Hollis."
>
> "It is," says the librarian. "It explains what the Sponge was. Hollis didn't need to. They were standing in it."
>
> Bramble leans over the gate. "Hollis says the apples made them pleased. Your column doesn't."
>
> "No," says the librarian, after a moment. "It doesn't."

**From the Archive** (A story from Willowbank; the librarian's column, written from page 4):
> Long ago, Willowbank drained a wide marsh called the Sponge to plant new orchard fields. That autumn, the orchard had its best harvest ever. Five years later, the brook rose higher than ever before at the mill's flood post. Our old Keeper, Hollis, recorded both.

Page 4 itself is shown exactly as written in the [spec 120 companion](120-keeper-of-the-ways-text.md#page-4-the-year-of-the-fine-harvest), with all three reading lengths.

### Scene 5. The Gazette's two stories (Act IV ch. 3)

**Hook:** The *Gazette* prints two pieces side by side: "Just a bad storm" and "We made it worse." The whole town reads both.

**Short:**
> The Gazette prints two stories about the flood. The orchard keeper tells a third. Bramble spots something one of them left out.

**Longer:**
> The *Gazette* is pinned to the café wall. Two columns, two big headlines.
>
> The orchard keeper reads both, slowly. "Neither of them asked me," she says. So she tells it herself.
>
> Bramble reads "We made it worse" twice. Then she taps it.
>
> "This uses Hollis's page," she says. "But it leaves out the bit where Hollis asks, 'Wet years, or our doing?'"
>
> She is right. It does.
>
> "And 'Just a bad storm' never mentions the old map," says {guide}.
>
> Nobody says which story wins. Dot pins a blank sheet under the two columns, headed *What we still don't know*.

**Just a bad storm** (A story from Willowbank; a *Gazette* piece):
> The storm brought more rain in one night than anyone could remember. No marsh could have soaked up all that water. The orchard has fed Willowbank for as long as anyone can recall. Don't blame the apples for the weather.

**We made it worse** (A story from Willowbank; a *Gazette* piece):
> Hollis's own page says it. The Sponge was drained, and five years later came the highest flood at the post. The old map shows marsh where the water now sits. We changed the valley, and the brook noticed.

**The orchard keeper's account** (A story from Willowbank; told aloud):
> My grandmother told me about the year of the fine harvest. The whole town came to pick, she said, and there was cider till spring. Nobody talked about floods then. But I've watched the low corner myself. These last few springs it stays wet, and reeds have come up that nobody planted.

### Archive drawer. The Fire Diary (open any time; A true thing from history)

**Hook:** A real diary from London, 1666, and a page written today about the same fire. One writer was there. One wasn't.

**Short:**
> In 1666 a great fire burned much of London. Samuel Pepys wrote about it in his diary. A history page tells it too.

**Keeper's note (A true thing from history):**
> Samuel Pepys worked for the navy in London. He kept a diary from 1660 to 1669, in shorthand, a fast code-like writing. He wrote it for himself. It was first printed in 1825, long after he died.

**From Pepys's diary** (A true thing from history; short excerpts in old spelling, in diary order, with today's English beside each):

| Pepys wrote | In today's English |
| --- | --- |
| 2 September: "Jane called us up about three in the morning" | Jane, one of the maids, woke the house at about 3 a.m. to say there was a fire. |
| He looked, thought it was far off, "and so went to bed again and to sleep." | He went back to bed. |
| He was told "that it begun this morning in the King's baker's house in Pudding-lane" | Later, an officer at the Tower of London told him it began that morning in the king's baker's house on Pudding Lane. |
| "So I down to the water-side, and there got a boat and through bridge, and there saw a lamentable fire." | He took a boat on the river and saw how terrible the fire was. |
| "the poor pigeons, I perceive, were loth to leave their houses, but hovered about the windows and balconys" | The pigeons didn't want to leave their homes. They flew around the windows. |
| 4 September: "and I my Parmazan cheese, as well as my wine and some other things." | He buried his Parmesan cheese and his wine in a pit, to keep them safe. |

**A history page** (A true thing from history; newly written for Willowbank's archive):
> The Great Fire of London began early on Sunday, 2 September 1666, in a bakery on Pudding Lane. A strong east wind blew the flames from house to house. After a long, dry summer, the wooden buildings burned easily. The fire burned for about four days. It destroyed more than 13,000 houses and the old St Paul's Cathedral.

**The London Gazette (Keeper's Desk; A true thing from history):**
> The *London Gazette* was the government's newspaper. The fire burned its printing house, so it missed an issue. On 10 September it printed a report of the fire. It said the report was for "satisfying the minds" of the king's people.

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer; worked-example steps appear one tap at a time. All cards are **A true thing about language**.

**Card 1. Firsthand**
> A firsthand account is told by someone who was there. They saw, heard or did it themselves. Look for words like "I saw" or "we heard."

Worked example: *The miller's note.*
1. Where was she? At the mill.
2. When did she write? During the storm.
3. "I pulled the third board in." She did it herself. Firsthand.

**Card 2. Secondhand**
> A secondhand account is told by someone who wasn't there. They learned about it from other people, from records, or from research.

Worked example: *Dot's notice.*
1. Was Dot at the mill? No.
2. "People say it was the worst storm." She heard it from others.
3. Secondhand.

**Card 3. Was the writer there?**
> A diary or letter is often firsthand. A textbook is often secondhand. But that's only a clue. The real test: was this writer there?

Worked example: *Two newspapers.*
1. A reporter stood on the bridge and watched the water. Firsthand.
2. A reporter wrote it up next day from phone calls. Secondhand.
3. Both are news reports.

**Card 4. Close up or big picture**
> Firsthand accounts usually give close details and feelings. Secondhand accounts often give the big picture: totals, causes and what happened next. Each has a different focus.

Worked example: *The storm night.*
1. The miller: the cat, the lamp, rain like gravel. Close up.
2. Dot: the footbridge, the low road, letters late. Big picture.
3. Neither has everything.

**Card 5. Told, not seen**
> Firsthand writers also pass on what they were told. That part is secondhand, even inside a diary. Look for "he tells me" or "they say."

Worked example: *Pepys and Pudding Lane.*
1. Pepys saw the fire from a boat. Firsthand.
2. Someone told him where it began. Secondhand.
3. One diary, two kinds of telling.

**Card 6. Many accounts**
> People in different places notice different things. Look for facts every account agrees on. Then ask why each one adds something the others don't.

Worked example: *Four people, one storm.*
1. All four say heavy rain, at night.
2. Hob talks about sheep. The baker talks about bread.
3. Each saw their own corner, and cared about it.

**Card 7. Who is telling?**
> The narrator is the voice telling a story. A first-person narrator is in the story and says "I." They usually know only what they saw, heard and thought.

Worked example: *Pip's version.*
1. "I blamed the wind." Pip says "I."
2. Pip is the narrator, in the story.
3. He never saw the magpie, so he can't tell you.

**Card 8. Limited or all-knowing**
> A third-person narrator says "he," "she" or "they." A limited one follows one character's thoughts. An all-knowing, or omniscient, narrator can tell anyone's thoughts.

Worked example: *Dot's side and the whole party.*
1. Dot's side: only Dot's thoughts. Limited.
2. The whole party: Dot's, Pip's and the magpie's. All-knowing.
3. Only the all-knowing one shows the nest.

**Card 9. A narrator you can't trust**
> An unreliable narrator tells a story you can't fully trust. Some fib. Some are mistaken. Some don't understand what they see. Look for clues.

Worked example: *The magpie.*
1. It says, "I did not untie any ribbon."
2. Then it says the knot "took ages."
3. How would it know? A clue it isn't telling the truth.

**Card 10. You know more**
> Sometimes you know something a character doesn't. That is called dramatic irony. It can make a story funny, or full of suspense.

Worked example: *Pip and the scratching.*
1. Pip thinks a monster scratched the door.
2. You see the paw prints and the floury cat.
3. You know more than Pip. That's why it's funny.

**Card 11. Purpose and audience**
> A text's purpose is its job, such as to record, inform, entertain or persuade. Its audience is who it's for. Both shape the words a writer picks.

Worked example: *The baker's sign.*
1. Job: get people to come in. Persuade.
2. Audience: hungry neighbors.
3. So: short, cheerful, "Come in!"

**Card 12. Context**
> Context is when, where and why a text was written. Knowing it helps you read it. A private diary isn't written like a newspaper.

Worked example: *Pepys's diary.*
1. When: 1666, day by day.
2. Why: for himself, in shorthand.
3. So he writes about his cheese. A newspaper wouldn't.

### (d) Who Is Telling?

**Header:** One missing ribbon, three tellings. Who is the narrator, and what can they know?

**Step 1.** Read Pip's, Dot's and the whole party's versions. For each, choose: *first person* · *third person, limited* · *third person, all-knowing*.

**Step 2. What can they know?** Tap each fact the narrator knows.
- The ribbon was gone: all three.
- Not a leaf was moving: Dot's side and the whole party.
- Pip was glad of the extra cake: Pip's version and the whole party.
- The magpie took it: only the whole party.

**Step 3. Switch the window.** Pick the line the magpie would say, telling it as "I."
- "I tucked something red into my nest." (fits)
- "Dot wondered where it had gone." (that's Dot's thought, not the magpie's)

### (e) The Magpie's Version (Keeper's Desk)

**Header:** The magpie tells it its own way. Find the clues that you can't fully trust it.

**Clue hunt:** tap any line that doesn't fit with another line, or with what others saw.
- "I did not untie any ribbon." + "The knot was very tight... it took ages." *How would it know about the knot?*
- "Ribbons blow about." + "Nobody felt any wind." *It argues against itself.*
- "I was nowhere near the gate." + the whole party's version. *Another account puts it at the nest with the ribbon.*

**Two kinds of untrustworthy (sort two narrators):**
- Fibs on purpose: the magpie.
- Honest but mistaken: Pip and the scratching.

**Why tell it this way?** *You can see what the magpie won't admit. That gap is the joke.* Card: When you know something a character doesn't, like Pip and the cat, that's dramatic irony.

### (f) Who Is It For?

**Header:** Four texts about the same storm. Match each one to its job and its audience.

**The texts (A story from Willowbank):**
- **The mill book:** Storm night. Brook over the second board. Third board in at nine.
- **Dot's notice:** The Post Office is open again. Letters may be late.
- **The library's picture-book page:** Splash! went the brook. Up came the water, over the boards and onto the steps. The cat said, "Not on my paws."
- **The baker's sign:** OPEN AGAIN after the storm! Warm bread, dry floor. Come in!

**Jobs:** record · inform · entertain · persuade
**Audiences:** the next miller · the whole town · small children · hungry neighbors

**Feedback (one line each):**
- Mill book: *Short notes and exact times. It's a record for whoever keeps the mill next.*
- Notice: *Plain facts and what to expect. It informs the whole town.*
- Picture-book page: *Sound words and a joke. It entertains small children.*
- Sign: *Exclamation marks and "Come in!" It persuades hungry neighbors.*

**Act III version (ch. 4, letters):** add Dot's letter to Nuria. "Dear Nuria, you asked about our storm. The brook came right up to the mill steps! The miller stayed up all night with her cat. We're all fine. Write soon! Dot." Job: share news with a friend. Audience: a friend far away.

**Keeper's Desk: context.** Pepys's diary (for himself, in shorthand, day by day), the *London Gazette* (for the king's people, eight days after the fire began) and today's history page (for learners). Match each to when, who for and why.

## Problem sets

Every item has the "I'm stuck" ladder. Choices appear in the order written; ✔ marks the right one. Tags in backticks name the misconception a wrong choice tests. Set C opens only after page 4 comes free; the other sets never name Hollis.

### Set A. Was you there? (Sprout, Seedling)

**A1 (Sprout, guided).** Steps shown first: 1. Where was the miller? 2. When did she write? 3. Did she see it herself?
- Prompt: The miller wrote her note at the mill, during the storm. What kind is it?
- Choices: Firsthand ✔ · Secondhand (`later-means-secondhand`) · Neither, it's only a note (`form-decides`)
- Feedback: Secondhand: She was right there, writing as it happened. · Neither: Notes can be firsthand. Was she there?
- Hint: Did the miller see the water herself?
- Explanation: She was there and wrote what she saw. Firsthand.

**A2 (Sprout).**
- Prompt: Dot wrote her notice a week later, from what people told her. Which kind?
- Choices: Firsthand (`told-is-seen`) · Secondhand ✔ · A made-up story (`secondhand-is-false`)
- Feedback: Firsthand: Dot wasn't at the mill. She asked others. · Made up: Secondhand isn't made up. Dot asked people who were there.
- Hint: Was Dot at the mill that night?
- Explanation: Dot learned it from others. Secondhand, and still useful.

**A3 (Seedling, sort).** Put each card in a tray: firsthand or secondhand.
- Cards: a diary by someone who saw the fire, written that day · a textbook page by a writer born years later, who used old records · a letter from someone who saw the flood · a news report by a writer who wasn't there, written from phone calls · a biography of a sailor, written from her letters by someone born later · an autobiography about the writer's own life
- Answer: firsthand: the diary by someone who saw the fire, the letter from someone who saw the flood, the autobiography. Secondhand: textbook page, news report from phone calls, biography.
- Hint: For each one, ask: was this writer there?
- Explanation: Each card says whether its writer saw it. The kind of paper alone doesn't decide.

**A4 (Seedling).**
- Prompt: Which words show the writer was there?
- Choices: "People say the water rose." (`told-is-seen`) · "I watched the water rise." ✔ · "Records show the water rose." (`told-is-seen`)
- Feedback: People say: That's what others told the writer. · Records show: That's from records, not the writer's own eyes.
- Hint: Look for "I" and a seeing word.
- Explanation: "I watched" means the writer saw it. Firsthand.

**A5 (Seedling).**
- Prompt: The miller writes, "I can see no further than the window." What does that show?
- Choices: Her note can't tell us about the footbridge ✔ · Her note must be wrong (`limit-means-wrong`) · She could see the whole town from the mill window (`firsthand-is-whole-story`)
- Feedback: Wrong: A limit isn't a mistake. She tells what she could see. · Whole town: She says she couldn't see past the window.
- Hint: What could she see from inside the mill?
- Explanation: Being there shows you one corner. Not everything.

### Set B. Two accounts, one event (Seedling, Sapling)

- Feedback: Footbridge: That's in Dot's notice. The miller couldn't see it. · Post Office: That's Dot's news, not the miller's.
- Hint: Which details come from inside the mill?
- Explanation: Close details, like the cat, come from being there.

**B2 (Seedling).**
- Prompt: What does Dot's notice have that the miller's note doesn't?
- Choices: The low road under water ✔ · Rain on the roof like gravel (`swap-accounts`) · The third flood board (`swap-accounts`)
- Feedback: Gravel: That's the miller's. She heard it on her roof. · Third board: The miller pulled it in herself.
- Hint: Which details needed someone outside the mill?

**B3 (Sapling).**
- Prompt: Why might Dot's notice leave out feelings?

- Hint: Read the last two lines of the notice.
- Explanation: A notice for the town keeps to useful facts. That's its focus.

**B4 (Seedling, true / not true).** Read each and choose True or Not true.
- "Pepys's diary tells us about the pigeons." True.
- "The history page gives a number of houses destroyed." True.
- "Pepys counted all 13,000 houses himself." Not true (`firsthand-is-whole-story`): one person saw one part.
- Hint: Who could count a whole city?
- Explanation: Firsthand gives close detail. Secondhand gathers the totals.

**B5 (Sapling).**
- Prompt: Pepys was told where the fire began. Is that part firsthand?
- Choices: Yes, it's in his diary (`told-is-seen`) · Yes, he was in London (`firsthand-is-whole-story`) · No, someone told him ✔
- Feedback: In his diary: A diary can hold things he heard. · In London: Being in London isn't seeing Pudding Lane.
- Hint: Did Pepys see it begin, or hear about it?

## Glossary

- **account:** a telling of what happened.
- **firsthand:** told by someone who was there.
- **secondhand:** told by someone who wasn't there.
- **focus:** what a text pays most attention to.
- **narrator:** the voice telling a story.
- **first person:** told by an "I" inside the story.
- **third person:** told with "he," "she" or "they."
- **limited:** follows one character's thoughts.
- **all-knowing (omniscient):** can tell any character's thoughts.
- **unreliable narrator:** a teller you can't fully trust.
- **dramatic irony:** when you know more than a character.
- **purpose:** a text's job.
- **audience:** who a text is for.
- **context:** when, where and why something was written.
- **diary:** a private daily record.
- **autobiography:** a life story told by the person who lived it.
- **biography:** a life story told by someone else.
- **shorthand:** fast writing with signs for sounds and words.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** firsthand and secondhand accounts (what each focuses on and leaves out), comparing several accounts of one event, the narrator's point of view (first person, third person limited, all-knowing), unreliable narrators (fibbing and honestly mistaken), dramatic irony, and how purpose, audience and context shape a text.

**Why the town's question stays open:** in Act IV the pack compares the *Gazette*'s two pieces for what they show and leave out. It never says which is right. The town's plan is decided at the meeting, by the explorer, in another activity.

**The real history:** Pepys's lines are from his diary of September 1666, which is out of copyright. The history page was written new for this pack. No deaths are described.

**Talk prompts** are said to you and never stored. Nothing the child says or writes is recorded.

**The UK terms:** English schools may call this "point of view" and "narrative perspective"; the curriculum asks pupils to know "the purpose, audience for and context of the writing".
