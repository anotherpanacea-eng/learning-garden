## Story scenes

### Scene 1. Three crates or six? (Act VI ch. 2, manifest)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** Dot writes the first manifest for the cart to Vadoalto. Pip loads one crate for every bit between commas.

**Short:**
> Dot's list has only commas. Pip loads six crates, and one says TWO BOXES. Dot adds a colon and semicolons. Now the list says three loads.

**Longer:**
> Dot writes the manifest at the café, between two honey jars.
>
> *The cart carries apples, six baskets, honey cake, two boxes, seeds, one tin.*
>
> Pip reads it and gets to work. Soon there are six crates by the cart. One is labeled TWO BOXES. It is empty.
>
> "Two boxes of what?" says Pip.
>
> "Of honey cake," says Dot. "It says so."
>
> "It says honey cake. Then it says two boxes. Separately."
>
> Dot looks at her list for a long moment. Then she adds a colon and two semicolons.
>
> *The cart carries three loads: apples, six baskets; honey cake, two boxes; seeds, one tin.*
>
> "Three crates," says Pip. He sits on the empty one. "Commas aren't strong enough for big items."
>
> "Semicolons are," says Dot.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> When the items in a list have commas of their own, semicolons split the list. Writers do this with places: Paris, France; Valencia, Spain; and Toulouse, France.

### Scene 2. Three Ways, one page (Act VI ch. 2, manifest)

**Hook:** Dot's notes about the three Ways fill a whole page in one long lump. Bramble says nobody will read it. Bramble is right.

**Short:**
> Dot's notes about the Ways run on and on. Bramble draws a table, one row for each Way. Now anyone can compare them at a glance.

**Longer:**
> Dot's notes about the Ways fill a whole page. *The river path is fast it floods the bridge road is slow it is always dry the high ford is short...*
>
> Bramble reads three lines and puts it down. "Nobody will read that," she says. "Not even you."
>
> "It's all true," says Dot.
>
> "It's all true in one long lump." Bramble takes the pen. She draws lines across and lines down. Down the side, the three Ways. Along the top: *Good for*, *Bad for*, *Safe when?*
>
> In a minute the lump is a table.
>
> Pip runs his finger along the high ford's row. "Short. Wet feet. Summer only." He looks up. "I can see it all at once."
>
> Dot is quiet. Then she writes a heading at the top: *Our Three Ways.*
>
> "You're welcome," says Bramble.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> A table works when a reader wants to compare. Each row is one thing, each column is one question, and every row and column has a label.

### Scene 3. The book crate (Act VI ch. 2, manifest)

**Hook:** The librarian packs a crate of books for Vadoalto's library. Pip writes the label. Every title looks exactly the same.

**Short:**
> Pip's label mixes up a book and a poem inside it. The librarian shows him: book titles in italics, poem titles in quotation marks.

**Longer:**
> The librarian is sending books up the river. Vadoalto's library has asked for stories in English.
>
> Pip writes the label: The Wind in the Willows. The Swing. A Child's Garden of Verses.
>
> "Is The Swing a book?" asks Dot, reading over his shoulder.
>
> "It's a poem," says the librarian. "It lives inside A Child's Garden of Verses. Pip, may I?"
>
> They write the label again. The book titles lean over in italics. The poem stands up straight, between quotation marks.
>
> "Books lean," says Pip. "Poems get hugged."
>
> "Something like that," says the librarian. "A chapter gets quotation marks too. It lives inside a book."
>
> At the bottom they add a line for Vadoalto, in two languages.
>
> *Libros en inglés para Vadoalto.* Books in English for Vadoalto.

**Keeper's note (A true thing from history):**
> *The Wind in the Willows* is by Kenneth Grahame, from 1908. *A Child's Garden of Verses* is by Robert Louis Stevenson, from 1885. "The Swing" is one of its poems.

### Scene 0. The market list (market list version, any time)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** The baker writes a list for the miller. Pip carries it. Pip goes looking for four things, and one of them is "one dozen."

**Short:**
> The baker's list says flour, two sacks, eggs, one dozen. Pip comes back puzzled. Semicolons show where each item ends.

**Longer:**
> The baker's list says: *flour, two sacks, eggs, one dozen.*
>
> Pip runs to the mill. He comes back with flour, two sacks, some eggs, and a worried face.
>

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer; worked-example steps appear one tap at a time. Every card is **A true thing about language**. Cards 13 to 15 are Keeper's Desk.

**Card 1. A colon opens a list**
> A colon ( : ) can open a list. In most school writing, the words before it make a whole sentence. The list comes after.

Worked example: *We send three things up the river: apples, honey cake and seeds.* (Market list version: *The miller sells three things: flour, oats and bran.*)
1. Cover the list. Read what's left: "We send three things up the river."
2. That's a whole sentence, so a colon can open the list.
3. "We need" isn't a whole sentence. In a sentence like that, most writers leave the colon out.

**Card 2. Commas in a short list**
> Commas split a short list into items. Some writers put a comma before the last "and." Some don't. Both are fine. Pick one way and keep it.

Worked example: *apples, honey cake, and seeds* or *apples, honey cake and seeds*
1. Three items: apples, honey cake, seeds.
2. The first has a comma before "and." The second doesn't.
3. Both are fine. Keep one way in one piece of writing.

**Card 3. Semicolons split big items**
> When list items have commas of their own, semicolons ( ; ) split the items. Then a reader can tell where each item ends.

Worked example: *The cart carries three loads: apples, six baskets; honey cake, two boxes; and seeds, one tin.*
1. With commas only, a reader sees six bits.
2. Each load has a name and an amount, joined by a comma.
3. Semicolons go between the loads. Now there are three.

**Card 4. Bullet points**
> Bullet points put each item on its own line, so a reader can scan down. A whole-sentence lead-in with a colon often comes first.

Worked example: *Pack these before the cart leaves:* then apples · honey cake · seeds, one to a line.
1. The lead-in is a whole sentence, so it ends with a colon.
2. Each item gets its own line and a dot in front.
3. A reader's eye runs straight down the list.

**Card 5. Same all the way down**
> Style guides disagree about bullet points. Some want capitals, some don't. Some want end marks, some don't. So choose one way and keep every bullet the same.

Worked example: three bullets: *apples* · *Honey cake.* · *seeds*
1. Bullet two has a capital and a period. The others don't.
2. Pick one way: no capitals, no end marks.
3. Fix bullet two: *honey cake*. Now every bullet matches.

**Card 6. Bullets that match**
> Start every bullet the same kind of way. If one bullet starts with a verb, they all should. Whole-sentence bullets usually end with a period.

Worked example: *Count the crates.* · *Tie the lids.* · *The letters.*
1. "Count" and "Tie" are verbs: things to do.
2. "The letters." isn't a thing to do.
3. Change it to *Pack the letters.* Now all three match.

**Card 7. A semicolon between sentences**
> A semicolon can join two whole sentences that belong together. Each side must make sense on its own. A comma alone can't do this job.

Worked example: *The river path is fast; the bridge road is dry.*
1. Cover the right side. "The river path is fast." A whole sentence.
2. Cover the left side. "The bridge road is dry." A whole sentence.
3. Both stand alone, and they belong together. A semicolon can join them.

**Card 8. A colon between sentences**
> A colon can join two whole sentences when the second one explains the first. Read the colon as "here's why" or "here's what."

Worked example: *The bridge is closed: the miller is painting it.*
1. "The bridge is closed." Why?
2. "The miller is painting it." That's why.
3. The second explains the first, so a colon fits.

**Card 9. A dash for a sharp break**
> A dash ( – ) can join two whole sentences too. It makes a sharper break, so the second part lands harder. Dashes feel less formal.

Worked example: *We packed two honey cakes – Pip ate one.*
1. Two whole sentences: "We packed two honey cakes." "Pip ate one."
2. The dash makes a sudden stop before the surprise.
3. A period or semicolon would also be correct. The dash adds the jolt.

**Card 10. Titles of works**
> In most school writing, titles of books and newspapers go in italics (slanted letters). Titles of poems, chapters and songs go in quotation marks. By hand, underline instead of italics.

Worked example: *The Wind in the Willows* and "The River Bank"
1. *The Wind in the Willows* is a whole book: italics.
2. "The River Bank" is its first chapter: quotation marks.
3. A part inside a bigger work gets quotation marks.

**Card 11. Headings**
> A heading tells a reader what a part is about before they read it. Subheadings split a long page into parts a reader can find.

Worked example: *the manifest page* (market list version: *the market page*)
1. The title goes at the top: *The Weekly Exchange Manifest*.
2. Subheadings name each part: *What Goes Up*, *What Comes Down*, *Our Three Ways*.
3. Looking for the Ways? Jump to that subheading.
(Market list version: title *Market Day Plan*; subheadings *What the Baker Needs*, *What the Miller Sells*, *When the Cart Stops*. Step 3: Looking for the cart times? Jump to that subheading.)

**Card 12. Tables and columns**
> A table lines facts up in rows and columns, with a label on each, so a reader can compare. Two columns set two lists side by side.

Worked example: *the Ways table* (market list version: *the cart-stop table*)
1. One row for each Way. (Market: one row for each stop.)
2. One column for each question: good for, bad for, safe when? (Market: what's there, and when?)
3. Read across for one row. Read down to compare all three.

**Card 13. Commas that change the meaning (Keeper's Desk)**
> Commas around a "which" part mean it's true of every one. With no commas, a "that" part picks out only some.

Worked example: *the baskets in the rain*
1. *The baskets, which got wet, stay behind.* Every basket got wet. All stay.
2. *The baskets that got wet stay behind.* Only the wet ones stay.
3. Same baskets, different marks. The commas change who stays.

**Card 14. Commas between adjectives (Keeper's Desk)**
> Put a comma between two adjectives if "and" fits between them and you could swap them. If not, leave the comma out.

Worked example: *two mornings and a bridge*
1. *a cold, wet morning*: "cold and wet" works. "Wet, cold" works too. Comma.
2. *an old stone bridge*: "old and stone" sounds wrong. No comma.
3. Test with "and" first. Then try swapping.

**Card 15. Three dots (Keeper's Desk)**
> Three dots ( ... ) are called an ellipsis. In a quote, they show that some words were left out. In a story, they can show a pause.

Worked example: *the miller's notice*
1. The notice says: "The mill opens at eight. The flour is fresh today. Bring your own sacks."
2. Dot copies: "The mill opens at eight. ... Bring your own sacks."
3. The dots show that a sentence was left out.

**Header:** Put a mark in each gap, or leave it empty. Then tap **Load it**.

**Tiles:** colon ( : ) · semicolon ( ; ) · comma ( , ) · leave it empty

3. These come down the river [1] reed, four bundles [2] honey, two jars [3] and letters, one bag.

**Market list lines (A story from Willowbank):**
1. The miller sells three things [1] flour [2] oats [3] and bran.
2. The baker needs three loads [1] flour, two sacks [2] eggs, one dozen [3] and butter, one block.
3. The cart stops at three places [1] the mill, at nine [2] the bakery, at ten [3] and the café, at noon.
4. We need [1] flour, eggs and oats.

**What fits (shown after Load it):**
- Lines with a lead-in sentence: slot 1 takes a colon.
- Short lists (manifest 1, market 1): slot 2 takes a comma; slot 3 takes a comma or nothing. Both are fine.
- Lists of big items: slots 2 and 3 take semicolons.
- *We need* lines: slot 1 stays empty.

**Load it (Pip loads one crate per item, reading exactly the marks placed):**
- All fits: Pip loads one crate for each item. Every label makes sense.
- Commas in both slots 2 and 3 on manifest line 2 (the cart line): Pip loads six crates. One just says TWO BOXES. Two boxes of what?
- Commas where semicolons go on any other line: Pip can't tell where one load ends and the next begins.
- Slot 1 empty on a lead-in line: Pip reads the lead-in and the first item as one long item. Where does the list start?
- A semicolon in slot 1: A semicolon joins two whole sentences. The list isn't one.
- A colon after *We need*: Pip stops after "We need." Need what? The colon cut the sentence in half.
- Any other pattern: Pip loads it just as it stands.

**Spanish line (manifest line 5):** *manzanas* = apples, *carrizo* = reed, *miel* = honey. Each item is one Spanish name and one English name.

### (b) Joining Dot's Notes

**Header:** Dot's notes join two sentences. Choose a mark for the gap. Then tap **Read it**.

**Tiles:** period ( . ) · semicolon ( ; ) · colon ( : ) · dash ( – ) · comma ( , ) · nothing

**Notes (A story from Willowbank):**
1. The river path is fast [ ] the bridge road is dry.
2. The bridge is closed [ ] the miller is painting it.
3. We packed two honey cakes [ ] Pip ate one.
4. Because the river path floods [ ] we take the bridge road.
5. Dot has one rule [ ] letters always go first.

None of these notes reads a page, so both versions use them.

**Read it (what each mark tells a reader):**
- Period: Two separate sentences, a clean stop between them.
- Semicolon: Two equal ideas, closely linked.
- Colon: Here's why, or here's what.
- Dash: A sudden break. The second part lands hard.
- Comma (notes 1, 2, 3, 5): A comma alone can't join two whole sentences. Try a stronger mark.
- Nothing (notes 1, 2, 3, 5): The sentences crash together. A reader can't tell where one ends.
- Colon (notes 1 and 3): A colon promises "here's why." Does the second part explain the first?
- Period, semicolon, colon or dash (note 4): "Because the river path floods" can't stand alone. A comma fits here.
- Nothing (note 4): A "because" part that comes first usually takes a comma. It helps a reader.

### (e) Clear It Up (Keeper's Desk)

**Header:** Small marks, big changes. Pick the version that says what the writer means.

**Which baskets?** Picture: four baskets, two of them wet. Which sentence fits the picture?
- *The baskets, which got wet, stay behind.* This says every basket got wet.
- *The baskets that got wet stay behind.* Fits: only the two wet ones stay.

**Two adjectives:** Tap where a comma belongs, if anywhere.
- *a cold wet morning*: comma after *cold*. "Cold and wet" works.
- *an old stone bridge*: no comma. "Old and stone" sounds wrong.
- *a long muddy path*: comma after *long*. "Long and muddy" works.

**Three dots:** Dot shortens a notice. Tap the words her dots stand for.
- Notice: "The bakery opens at seven. Honey cake is on Fridays. Please bring a basket."

## Problem sets

Every item has the "I'm stuck" ladder. Choices appear in the order written; ✔ marks the right one. Tags in backticks name the misconception a wrong choice tests. Items marked *(manifest)* appear only in the manifest version.

### Set A. Colons and lists (Seedling, Sapling)

**A1 (Seedling, guided).** Steps shown first: 1. Cover the list. 2. Is what's left a whole sentence? 3. Then a colon can open the list.
- Card: *We need three things ___ apples, reed and honey.*
- Prompt: Which mark fits the gap?
- Choices: A colon ✔ · A semicolon (`semicolon-for-colon`) · No mark at all (`no-colon`)
- Feedback: Semicolon: A semicolon joins two whole sentences. The list isn't one. · No mark: Then "three things apples" runs together.
- Hint: Is "We need three things" a whole sentence?
- Explanation: "We need three things" is a whole sentence, so a colon opens the list.

**A2 (Seedling).**
- Prompt: Which sentence uses its colon correctly?
- Choices: We need: apples, letters and seeds. (`colon-after-fragment`) · We need three things: apples, letters and seeds. ✔ · We need three: things, apples, letters and seeds. (`colon-anywhere`)
- Feedback: We need: "We need" isn't a whole sentence. Leave the colon out. · We need three: "Things" belongs before the colon, not in the list.
- Hint: Cover each list. Which leaves a whole sentence?
- Explanation: "We need three things" stands alone. The colon then opens the list.

**A3 (Sapling, tile).** Tiles: colon, semicolon, comma.
- Card: *The cart carries three loads [1] apples, six baskets [2] honey cake, two boxes [3] and seeds, one tin.*
- Prompt: Put a mark in each gap.
- Answer: [1] colon · [2] semicolon · [3] semicolon.
- Feedback for a comma in [2] (`commas-split-items`): Now "six baskets" and "honey cake" run together. Try a semicolon.
- Feedback for a comma in [3] (`commas-split-items`): Now "two boxes" and "seeds" run together. Try a semicolon.
- Hint: Each load has a comma inside it already.
- Explanation: A colon opens the list. Semicolons split the three loads.

**A4 (Sapling).**
- Card: *These come down the river: reed, four bundles; honey, two jars; and letters, one bag.*
- Prompt: How many loads does this list name?
- Choices: 6 (`commas-split-items`) · 2 (`count-marks`) · 3 ✔
- Feedback: 6: That counts every bit between commas. Look at the semicolons. · 2: Two semicolons split the list into three parts.
- Hint: Read between the semicolons. Each part is one load.
- Explanation: Reed, honey and letters: three loads, each with its amount.

**A5 (Sapling) *(manifest)*.**
- Card: Nuria's list: each item has a Spanish name and an English name.
- Prompt: Which list keeps each pair of names together?
- Choices: manzanas, apples; carrizo, reed; miel, honey ✔ · manzanas; apples; carrizo; reed; miel; honey (`semicolon-everywhere`) · manzanas, apples, carrizo, reed, miel, honey (`commas-split-items`)
- Feedback: All semicolons: Now every name looks like its own item. · All commas: A reader can't tell which names go together.
- Hint: Where does one item end and the next begin?
- Explanation: Commas join each pair. Semicolons split the pairs apart.

### Set B. Bullet points (Seedling, Sapling)

**B1 (Seedling, guided).** Steps shown first: 1. Check each bullet's first letter. 2. Check each bullet's end. 3. Do all three match?
- Prompt: Which bullet list is punctuated the same way all through?
- Choices: Apples / reed. / Honey (`mixed-bullets`) · apples / reed / honey ✔ · Apples. / Reed / honey. (`mixed-bullets`)
- Display: each choice is a short bullet list; the slash marks a new bullet line.
- Feedback: First list: Two capitals, one period. They don't match. · Third list: Some periods, some not. They don't match.
- Hint: Look at the first and last letter of each bullet.
- Explanation: No capitals and no end marks, every time. Capitals on all three would match too.

**B2 (Seedling, tap).**

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Manifest:** a list of everything a cart or boat carries.
- **Colon ( : ):** two dots. It opens a list or an explanation.
- **Semicolon ( ; ):** a dot over a comma. It joins two whole sentences or splits big list items.
- **Dash ( – ):** a short line between words. Here, one dash joins two whole sentences.
- **Whole sentence:** words that make sense standing alone, with a subject and a verb.
- **Lead-in:** the words before a list that tell what the list is.
- **Bullet point:** a dot that starts one item on its own line.
- **Verb:** a word for doing or being, like *count* or *is*.
- **Adjective:** a describing word, like *cold* or *old*.
- **Italics:** slanted letters, *like these*.
- **Quotation marks:** the marks that go around a poem's or chapter's title, or someone's exact words.
- **Title:** the name of a book, poem, chapter, song or newspaper.
- **Heading:** a title for one part of a page.
- **Subheading:** a smaller heading inside a page.
- **Table:** facts in rows (across) and columns (down), each with a label.
- **Column:** a line of facts running down a page.
- **Ellipsis ( ... ):** three dots that show words left out, or a pause.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** a colon to introduce a list after a whole sentence; semicolons between list items that contain commas; punctuating bullet points consistently and keeping them parallel; semicolons, colons and dashes between independent clauses; titles of works in italics or quotation marks; headings, subheadings, columns, bullets and tables to guide a reader; and, at Keeper's Desk, commas for nonrestrictive clauses and coordinate adjectives, and the ellipsis.

**Terms:** England's national curriculum teaches these in Year 6 and calls a period a full stop; the child text uses American terms. In the Common Core, colons and semicolons for lists and clauses appear in grades 9 to 10, and titles of works in grade 5, so this pack runs ahead of the US grade order for some children. Brackets are the ( ) marks, usually called parentheses in the United States.

**What it does not claim:** style guides differ. The colon is keyed only in running sentences: Chicago leaves it out after *We need*, but some house styles (Microsoft's, for one) allow a colon after a short lead-in such as "You can:" above a bulleted list. Bullet-point punctuation varies by guide, so only consistency within one list is checked. The comma before "and" in a list (the serial comma) is a choice and is never marked. Title styles differ a little between guides such as Chicago, MLA and APA, and newspapers often use quotation marks instead of italics; the pack teaches the common school convention. American books usually print a long dash with no spaces; this pack shows a spaced short dash. Where two marks both work, both are accepted.

**Writing:** the plan in F4 is told to a grown-up or written on paper. Nothing typed is stored, and nothing is graded.

**Spanish:** the item names (*manzanas*, *carrizo*, *miel*, *pastel de miel*, *semillas*, *cartas*) and the crate line *Libros en inglés para Vadoalto* need a fluent reader.
