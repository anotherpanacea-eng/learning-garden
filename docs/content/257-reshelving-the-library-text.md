# Re-shelving the Library: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 257](../specs/257-reshelving-the-library.md). Use as written, after review. `{guide}` is never gendered. Pronouns: Pip he/him; Dot, Bramble, Wren Halloway, the orchard keeper she/her; Hob Pennick and the baker he/him; the librarian they/them. Ember and Puff take no pronouns. Sources: [audit](../source-audits/257-reshelving-the-library.md). Nothing is scored, timed or locked.

**Keeper rule (spec 120):** nobody in this pack names the old Keeper. When Pip carries the parcel, it is "the parcel for the Keeper of the Ways". No page of the parcel is read here.

**Labels used throughout:**
- **A story from Willowbank** marks the town, its people, the notices, and who wrote and drew each library book (fiction).
- **A true thing about living things** and **A true thing about weather** mark the facts inside the three fact books (real, sourced).
- **A true thing about language** marks the teach cards about books and reading.
- **A true thing from history** marks the one real old poem (Robert Louis Stevenson, 1885).

**Every book screen (always shown):** Tap a page to hear it. Pick short, middle or longer.

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer; worked-example steps appear one tap at a time. Cards are **A true thing about language**.

**Card 1. Three kinds of books**
> A story tells what happens to its characters. A poem plays with sounds and short lines. A fact book tells true things about the world.

Worked example: *The Busy Hive.*
1. Is it about characters and what happens to them? No.
2. Is it written in short lines that sound good aloud? No.
3. It tells true things about bees. It's a fact book.

**Card 2. Author and illustrator**
> The author writes the words. The illustrator makes the pictures. Sometimes one person does both jobs.

Worked example: *Ask the Weather.*
1. The cover says: Words and pictures by Wren Halloway.
2. Wren wrote the words.
3. Wren drew the pictures too. One person, both jobs.

**Card 3. Covers and the title page**
> The front cover shows the title and a picture. The back cover tells a little about the book. The title page names the title, author and illustrator.

Worked example: *A book with no cover.*
1. The covers are gone.
2. Open to the first printed page.
3. The title page still tells you the title and who made the book.

**Card 4. Contents and index**
> The contents page is at the front. It lists the parts in page order. The index is at the back. It lists words from A to Z.

Worked example: *Where are the mud nests?*
1. Contents: Summer: Mud nests, page 3.
2. Or the index: mud, 3.
3. Both send you to page 3.

**Card 5. Headings and the glossary**
> A heading tells you what one part is about. A glossary explains special words. In these books, the glossary is called Words to know.

Worked example: *Streamer.*
1. Page 2 says swallows have streamers.
2. Turn to Words to know.
3. Streamer: a long, thin tail feather.

**Card 6. Three ways to build a fact book**
> Some fact books go in time order. Some go part by part, like a list. Some ask questions, then answer them.

Worked example: *Three headings.*
1. "Spring", then "Summer": time order.
2. "One queen", "Many workers": part by part.
3. "Which way is the wind blowing?": questions and answers.

**Card 7. Main topic and key details**
> The main topic is what a whole text is mostly about. Key details are important facts that tell you more about it.

Worked example: *Swallows Come Home.*
1. Every page is about swallows through the year.
2. Main topic: a swallow's year.
3. Key details: mud nests, flying insects, Africa in winter.

**Card 8. Each part has a focus**
> In a longer text, each page or paragraph has its own focus. Together they build the main idea.

Worked example: *The Busy Hive.*
1. Page 2: the queen lays eggs.
2. Page 3: workers do most jobs.
3. Page 4: drones are male bees.
4. Together: each kind of bee has its own job.

**Card 9. Notes, not copying**
> Notes keep only the important facts, in a few words. A table gives each fact its own box.

Worked example: *One sentence into a note.*
1. Read: "In summer, a hive can hold tens of thousands of workers."
2. Keep the important part: tens of thousands of workers.
3. Note in the box: Workers: tens of thousands.

**Card 10. Words that link ideas**
> Some words show order: first, next, finally. Some show cause and effect: because, so, as a result. Some compare: both, but, unlike.

Worked example: *"The tail is wider, so the wind pushes it away."*
1. What happens first? The tail is wider.
2. Link word: so.
3. What does that cause? The wind pushes the tail away.

**Card 11. Why did the author write it?**
> Authors write to inform, which means to tell facts. They write to persuade, to change your mind. They write to entertain, for fun.

Worked example: *Bramble's notice.*
1. It says: Mow it every week!
2. It wants readers to do something.
3. So its purpose is to persuade.

**Card 12. Their view, your view**
> An author's word choices show how they feel. "Scruffy mess" and "little meadow" can name the same grass. You can agree or disagree with them.

Worked example: *Two notices.*
1. Bramble calls the long grass a scruffy mess. She dislikes it.
2. The orchard keeper calls it a little meadow. She likes it.
3. Now ask yourself: what do I think, and why?

### Book 1. Swallows Come Home (time order)

**Front cover:** Swallows Come Home · Words by Hob Pennick · Pictures by Wren Halloway · *(picture: a swallow swooping in at a barn door)*

**Back cover:** Every spring, swallows fly back to Hob's barn. Where have they been all winter? Turn the page and find out.

**Title page:** Swallows Come Home · Words by Hob Pennick · Pictures by Wren Halloway · Willowbank Library

**Contents:** Spring: Back to the barn, 2 · Summer: Mud nests, 3 · Autumn: The long trip, 4 · Winter: Far away, 5 · Words to know, 6 · Index, 7

**Page 2. Spring: Back to the barn** (A true thing about living things)
- Short: Spring is here. The swallows are back!
- Middle: In spring, swallows fly back to the valley. Look for a forked tail. They swoop into barns and sheds to nest.
- Longer: Every spring, swallows fly north to raise their young. Many build their nests inside barns and sheds. You can tell a swallow by its tail. It has a deep fork, with two long, thin streamers.

**Page 3. Summer: Mud nests**
- Short: Two swallows build a nest of mud.
- Middle: Both parents build the nest. They bring mud and shape it into a cup. Soon there are hungry chicks inside.
- Longer: Both parents build the nest. They bring mud, often mixed with bits of grass, and shape it into a cup. They line it with feathers. The chicks are always hungry. Their parents catch small flying insects in the air to feed them.

**Page 4. Autumn: The long trip**
- Short: In autumn, the swallows fly south.
- Middle: In autumn, the days get shorter. Soon there will be few flying insects to eat. So the swallows fly south.
- Longer: Why do swallows leave? Food is the big reason. Swallows eat small flying insects, and in winter there are few insects here. Shorter autumn days are the signal to go. Swallows flap the whole way, over the sea and across the Sahara, a huge desert.

**Page 5. Winter: Far away**
- Short: In winter, the swallows are in Africa.
- Middle: Many swallows spend the winter in Africa, south of the Sahara. There are plenty of insects there in our winter.
- Longer: Many swallows spend our winter in Africa, south of the Sahara, where there are insects to eat. How do we know? Scientists put a tiny numbered ring on a bird's leg. In 1912, a swallow ringed in Britain was found in South Africa. Its ring showed how far swallows go.

**Page 6. Words to know**
- desert: a very dry place where little rain falls.
- insect: a small animal with six legs, like a fly.
- migrate: to travel to another place each year, and back again.
- ring: a tiny numbered band put on a bird's leg.
- streamer: a long, thin tail feather.

**Page 7. Index:** Africa 5 · chicks 3 · desert 4 · feathers 3 · insects 3, 4, 5 · mud 3 · nest 2, 3 · ring 5 · Sahara 4, 5 · tail 2

### Book 2. The Busy Hive (part by part)

**Front cover:** The Busy Hive · Words by Wren Halloway · Pictures by Hob Pennick · *(picture: bees on a honeycomb)*

**Back cover:** Thousands of bees share one home. Who does what? Meet the whole team.

**Title page:** The Busy Hive · Words by Wren Halloway · Pictures by Hob Pennick · Willowbank Library

**Contents:** One queen, 2 · Many workers, 3 · A few drones, 4 · The waggle dance, 5 · Words to know, 6 · Index, 7

**Page 2. One queen** (A true thing about living things)
- Short: This is the queen. She lays eggs.
- Middle: Each hive has one queen. Her job is to lay eggs. In early summer she lays more than a thousand eggs a day.
- Longer: Each hive has one queen bee. Her job is to lay eggs. In early summer she can lay about 1,500 eggs in a single day. She does not give orders. The workers share out the other jobs.

**Page 3. Many workers**
- Short: These are workers. They do lots of jobs.
- Middle: Most bees in a hive are workers. They are all female. Workers clean, feed young bees, build, guard the door and fetch food.
- Longer: In summer, a hive can hold tens of thousands of workers. They are all female. A young worker starts inside, cleaning cells and feeding young bees. Later she builds with wax, stores food or guards the door. From about three weeks old, she flies to flowers for nectar and pollen.

**Page 4. A few drones**
- Short: These are drones. They are male bees.
- Middle: A hive has only a few hundred drones. Drones are male bees. They have no sting.
- Longer: A summer hive has a few hundred drones. Drones are male bees, and they have no sting. They do not fetch food or guard the door. Their job is to mate with a young queen.

**Page 5. The waggle dance**
- Short: A worker dances to show where flowers are.
- Middle: A worker finds good flowers. Back in the hive, she does a waggle dance. The dance shows other workers which way to fly.
- Longer: When a worker finds good flowers, she flies home and dances. She runs in a straight line, waggling her body, then loops round and runs again. The direction of the run shows which way to fly. A longer waggle means the flowers are farther away.

**Page 6. Words to know**
- cell: one small wax room in a hive.
- colony: a big group of animals that live and work together.
- mate: when a male and a female join to make young.
- nectar: sweet juice inside flowers.
- pollen: fine powder made by flowers.
- wax: what bees make to build their cells.

**Page 7. Index:** cells 3 · dance 5 · drones 4 · eggs 2 · flowers 3, 5 · nectar 3 · pollen 3 · queen 2, 4 · sting 4 · wax 3 · workers 2, 3, 5

### Book 3. Ask the Weather (questions and answers)

**Front cover:** Ask the Weather · Words and pictures by Wren Halloway · *(picture: a wind vane on a roof, a rain gauge on the grass)*

**Back cover:** Why does the arrow point that way? Where does rain come from? Five questions, five answers.

**Title page:** Ask the Weather · Words and pictures by Wren Halloway · Willowbank Library

**Contents:** What does a rain gauge do? 2 · How do you read a rain gauge? 3 · Which way is the wind blowing? 4 · Where does rain come from? 5 · Why does a river rise after the rain stops? 6 · Words to know, 7 · Index, 8

**Page 2. What does a rain gauge do?** (A true thing about weather)
- Short: A rain gauge catches rain.
- Middle: A rain gauge catches rain in a funnel. Then you can measure how much rain fell. Stand it in the open, away from trees.
- Longer: A rain gauge catches rain in a funnel and holds it in a tube. We measure rain as a depth, in millimeters. Stand the gauge in the open. Under a tree, the leaves catch some rain first, so the gauge shows too little.

**Page 3. How do you read a rain gauge?**
- Short: Look. Write it down. Tip it out.
- Middle: First, read the water level. Next, write the number down. Finally, tip the water out.
- Longer: Read the gauge at the same time each day. First, bend down so your eyes are level with the water. Next, read the number on the side and write it down. Finally, tip the water out. Then tomorrow's rain starts from zero.

**Page 4. Which way is the wind blowing?**
- Short: The arrow points to where the wind comes from.
- Middle: A wind vane has an arrow and a wide tail. The wind pushes the tail away. So the arrow points into the wind.
- Longer: A wind vane turns on a pole. Its tail is wider than its arrow, so the wind pushes hardest on the tail. The tail swings away. As a result, the arrow points into the wind. Winds are named for where they come from. A west wind blows from the west.

**Page 5. Where does rain come from?**
- Short: Water goes up. Rain comes down.
- Middle: The sun warms water, and some turns into vapor, a gas you cannot see. The vapor rises, cools and makes tiny drops: a cloud. Heavy drops fall as rain.
- Longer: The sun warms water in puddles, ponds and rivers. Because of that warmth, some water evaporates into vapor, a gas you cannot see. Rising air carries the vapor up, where it cools. As a result, the vapor condenses into tiny drops, and we see a cloud. When drops join and grow heavy, they fall as rain.

**Page 6. Why does a river rise after the rain stops?**
- Short: Rain takes time to reach the river.
- Middle: Rain that falls on hills takes time to reach the river. So a river can keep rising after the rain stops.
- Longer: When rain falls on hills and fields, some soaks into the ground and some runs over it. That water takes time to reach the river. So a river can keep rising after the rain has stopped. How long depends on the ground and the slope.

**Page 7. Words to know**
- condense: to change from a gas into tiny drops of water.
- evaporate: to change from a liquid into a gas.
- funnel: a cone that guides water into a narrow tube.
- gauge: a tool for measuring something.
- millimeter: a very small length. Ten make one centimeter.
- vapor: water as a gas you cannot see.
- wind vane: a pointer that shows where the wind comes from.

**Page 8. Index:** cloud 5 · funnel 2 · millimeters 2 · rain 2, 3, 5, 6 · river 5, 6 · sun 5 · tree 2 · vapor 5 · wind 4

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Author:** the person who writes the words of a book.
- **Illustrator:** the person who makes the pictures in a book.
- **Title:** the name of a book.
- **Front cover:** the outside front of a book, with the title.
- **Back cover:** the outside back of a book. It often tells a little about it.
- **Title page:** a page near the front with the title, author and illustrator.
- **Story:** a text about characters and what happens to them.
- **Poem:** a text in short lines that plays with sounds.
- **Fact book:** a book that tells true things about the world.
- **Contents:** a list of a book's parts and their pages, at the front.
- **Heading:** words at the top of a part that say what it is about.
- **Glossary:** a list that explains a book's special words.
- **Index:** a list of words from A to Z, with their pages, at the back.
- **Main topic:** what a whole text is mostly about.
- **Key detail:** an important fact that tells more about the main topic.
- **Focus:** what one part of a text is about.
- **Notes:** the important facts, written in a few words.
- **Summary:** a short telling of the main points, in your own words.
- **Cause:** something that makes another thing happen.
- **Effect:** what happens because of a cause.
- **Inform:** to tell people facts.
- **Persuade:** to try to change what people think or do.
- **Entertain:** to give people fun or enjoyment.
- **View:** what someone thinks or feels about something.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** the parts of a book (covers, title page, contents, headings, glossary, index) and why each is there; the author's and illustrator's jobs; stories, poems and fact books; three ways fact books are built; main topic, key details and each part's focus; short notes in a table and a short summary; words that link ideas in order or as cause and effect; and why an author wrote something, with the difference between the author's view and the reader's.

**About the books:** the three fact books were written for this game. Their authors and illustrators are Willowbank characters, but the facts about swallows, honeybees and weather are real, checked in the source audit, and shared with the Bird, Bee Meadow and Weather Station packs. *Rain* is a real poem by Robert Louis Stevenson (1885), long out of copyright. The baker's poem and the other covers are invented.

**Reading lengths:** short, middle and longer readings tell the same facts at three sizes. The length a child picks is never recorded and says nothing about their reading. Read-aloud is always there.

**What it does not claim:** this is not a decoding check, a reading level or a test. Words like "inform" and "persuade" follow the US school terms; English schools teach the same ideas.

**Talk:** the "which notice do you agree with?" question is for talking. Either view, or both, is a fine answer. Nothing is stored.
