## 1. Why this case

In Act IV the miller says: "I thought nobody ever undid a wall. Then I read about the Dutch rivers." This case is what the miller read. The Netherlands, famous for dikes, decided after two near-disasters in the 1990s that higher dikes alone weren't enough, and spent years giving its rivers more room: moving dikes back, digging side channels and letting some farmland flood on purpose. It is the Wall and the Sponge as a real national argument, and its answer looks a lot like Some of Each.

The case adds a fixed-area rectangular arithmetic model. Under its stated assumptions, a wider rectangle is shallower. That is not a general river prediction, and the model cannot set a safe dike height.

## 4. Step 1: Two near misses

**Reading panel** (English at three lengths; Dutch words are glossed on tap).

**Short**

> Much of the Netherlands is low land beside big rivers, protected by earth walls called **dikes**. High water in 1993 and 1995 brought danger. Afterward, the country combined dike work with more space for rivers, preparing for future high water. Most projects finished later.

The common timeline keeps **1993 high water → 1995 high water → programme decision → later project completion** visible beside every passage amount. Detailed dates and figures remain source-review gates; no task requires a hidden longer passage.

**Medium** adds:

> After 1995, the government asked a hard question. The rivers were carrying more water than before, and might carry even more in future. Should the country keep making its dikes higher? Or should it give the rivers more space to spread out safely? It decided to do some of both, and to put more weight on space. The plan was called **Ruimte voor de Rivier**: Room for the River.

**Long** adds:

> Room for the River was a programme of about thirty projects along the Rhine and its branches and the Meuse. Some moved dikes further back from the river. Some dug new side channels, so floodwater had a second path. Some lowered the land next to the river so it could hold more water. In a few places, farmland behind a dike was turned into land that is allowed to flood in very high water, and the farms were moved. Most of the work was finished by the mid-2010s, at a cost of about 2.3 billion euros.

**Glossary:** *dijk* (dike: a long bank of earth that keeps water out); *rivier* (river); *ruimte* (room, space); *uiterwaard* (the land between the river and its dike, which floods in high water).

**Learn card:**

> A **near miss** is a disaster that almost happened. It can change minds as much as a real one, because people see what could have happened without paying the full price.

Tasks:

- **1a.** Put in order: the 1993 high water; the 1995 high water and evacuation; the decision to give rivers more room; most of the projects finished. *Answer: in that order.*
- **1b.** "Why did the government change its approach if the dikes held?" Choices:
  - "Because the dikes only just held, and the rivers might carry more water in future." ← fits the reading
  - "Because the dikes broke in 1995."
  - "Because people preferred parks to dikes."

  Reply to the second: "This reading describes a danger and the response; it does not say the dikes broke in1995. Check the reviewed source card before making that claim."
- **1c.** "What did the Netherlands decide?" Choices: "Only room for the river."; "Only higher dikes."; "Some of both, with more weight on room." The third fits; the reply to the first: "Every passage amount says the response combined dike work and more room. It was not instead of all dike work."

## 5. Step 2: Higher walls, or more room?

**Learn card:**

> There are two ways to keep a river from flooding a place. **Hold it in:** make the banks higher, so more water fits between them. **Let it spread:** give it more space somewhere it does less harm, so it doesn't rise as high where people live.
>
> Willowbank's three plans are the same two ideas. The Wall holds it in. The Sponge lets it spread. Some of Each does a bit of both.

Tasks:

- **2a.** Sort each measure into **Hold it in** or **Let it spread**: a higher dike; moving a dike further back from the river; digging a side channel; lowering the land beside the river; letting a farmed area flood in very high water; strengthening an old dike. *Answers: hold in: higher dike, strengthening an old dike. Spread: the other four.*
- **2b.** Match each Room for the River measure to the Willowbank plan most like it: moving a dike back (the Sponge, or Some of Each); letting farmland flood (the Sponge); a higher dike (the Wall). Not marked; each match gets a reply explaining the likeness and one difference.
- **2c.** "Is anything in Room for the River like Valencia's choice (Case 2)?" Choices: "Digging a side channel gives the water a second path, a little like Valencia's new channel."; "No, Valencia did nothing like any of this."; "Yes, the Netherlands moved whole rivers away from cities." The first gets: "Yes, on a much smaller scale. Valencia moved the whole river; a side channel only takes the extra in a flood." The third gets: "Not whole rivers. Find what a side channel does in the long reading." (Shown only if the explorer has Case 2.)

## 6. Step 3: A wider rectangle with the same area

**Model label on every panel: Simplified model. Real rivers are not rectangles, and engineers use much more detailed models.**

**Learn card** (an original fixed-area arithmetic diagram, not a result from the current Water Lab):

> Picture a slice straight across the river, from bank to bank. The water in that slice is roughly a rectangle: **width × depth**.
>
> For this diagram we fix the cross-section area at2,000 m². If we also pretend flow speed is unchanged and the beds are level, a wider rectangular channel would need less depth for that same area. Real water does not keep those assumptions for us. The arithmetic does not predict a real flood.
>
> Worked example: a flood needs a cross-section of 2,000 square metres. In a channel 400 m wide: 2,000 ÷ 400 = **5 m** deep. Widen it to 500 m: 2,000 ÷ 500 = **4 m** deep.

**Guided first task (3a):** the worked example's rectangle, with width and depth sliders that keep the area fixed.

Tasks (Check my working):

- **3a.** The same flood needs **2,000 m²**. The river is **250 m** wide. How deep? *Answer: 2,000 ÷ 250 = **8 m**.*
- **3b.** A side channel adds **150 m** of width. How deep now? *Answer: 2,000 ÷ 400 = **5 m**: 3 m lower.*
- **3c.** "To bring the water down to **4 m**, how wide must the river be?" *Answer: 2,000 ÷ 4 = **500 m**.*
- **3d.** In the diagram, add a **0.5 m drawn margin** above an **8 m rectangle**. How high is the drawn top? *Answer: **8.5 m**.* This checks addition in the model only, never a dike design or a safe real-world height.
- **3e.** "What does this simple model leave out?" Choose any; each has a reply; none marked wrong:
  - Water flows faster in some places and slower in others.
  - Real riverbeds are curved, not flat.
  - Where the extra space is: the water must be able to reach it.
  - Nothing important. *Reply: "It gets the main idea right. But the book says engineers spent years on the details. What might they have been checking?"*

**I'm stuck (3a):**

1. *Hint:* "Area is width × depth. You know the area and the width. What do you do to find the depth?"
2. *Similar example:* "A rectangle has area 60 and width 12. Depth = 60 ÷ 12 = 5. Now: area 2,000, width 250."
3. **Skip for now.**

**Replies to likely errors:**

| Entered | Misconception | Reply |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 500,000 for 3a | Multiplying area by width | "That would be an enormously deep river. Area is width × depth; you need to undo the width." |
| 1,750 for 3a | Subtracting | "Width and depth multiply to make the area. Which operation undoes a multiplication?" |
| 8 m for 3b | Not using the new width | "The river is wider now: 250 + 150. Divide by that." |
| 650 for 3c | Adding instead | "Width × 4 must make 2,000. What times 4 is 2,000?" |

## 7. Step 4: Lent: who gained, who gave something up?

**Reading panel** (English, two lengths):

**Short**

> At the city of Nijmegen, the river Waal makes a sharp bend and narrows. Across the river is the village of Lent. To give the river room, the dike in front of Lent was moved about **350 metres** back, and a new side channel was dug. Some families had to leave their homes so the work could be done. The new island between the river and the channel became a park.

**Longer** adds:

> Around fifty homes were taken down. Some people who lived in Lent were angry and sad for a long time; others came to like the new riverside. The project was finished around 2016. Engineers expected it to lower the highest floods at Nijmegen by about a third of a metre. A bridge now crosses the new channel, and the city later decided to keep most of the island as parkland.

Tasks:

- **4a.** On a map at **1 cm for 50 m**, how far back was the dike moved? *Answer: 350 ÷ 50 = **7 cm**.* (Revisits the Cartographer's Table.)
- **4b.** "A third of a metre is about how many centimetres?" *Answer: about **33 cm**.* (Reply to 30: "Close. A third of 100 is 33 and a bit.")
- **4c.** Perspective cards. For each person, choose what they **gained**, what they **gave up**, or **both**. None is marked wrong; each choice has a reply.
  - A family whose house in Lent was taken down.
  - Someone in Nijmegen whose street floods less.
  - A farmer elsewhere whose land is now allowed to flood.
  - Someone who now walks in the island park.
  - Someone who paid taxes towards the 2.3 billion euros.
- **4d.** "Whose voice is missing from this book?" Free choice with authored replies: the families who left; the farmers who moved; people downstream; people who wanted higher dikes instead. Each reply names the kind of source that would hold that voice (an interview, a newspaper letter, a council meeting record).

Three parts, each with **Write it yourself** (free text) and **Choose and adapt**:

> "That's the best kind of history lesson," says the librarian. "It doesn't tell us what to do. It tells us we're not the first to argue about it." Behind them, the miller says "huh" again, quietly.

**Supported route:** Choose and adapt alone completes Step 5; steps 1 to 4 can stay open.
