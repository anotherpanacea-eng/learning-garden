## Page 1. River and land

**Teach card 1:** Egypt is mostly desert. The Nile River runs through it. Farms grew only where the river's flood reached.

**Teach card 2:** People called their land *Kemet*, the black land, for its dark soil. The desert was the red land.

**Worked example (one step per tap):**
1. Find the blue river on the map.
2. See the green on each side? That's where the flood reached.
3. The green stops where the sand starts. Farms stop there too.

**Teach card 3:** The Nile flows north to the sea. Upstream is south. So Upper Egypt is the south.

**Worked example:**
1. Follow the arrow. The water runs toward the top of the map.
2. Upstream is where the water comes from: the bottom of the map.
3. The Egyptians called the upstream land Upper Egypt. It's in the south.
4. The wide delta near the sea is Lower Egypt, in the north.

**Map note (always shown):** A simplified map. Not to scale. North is at the top.

## Page 2. The flood year

**Teach card 1:** Every summer the Nile rose and spread over the fields. When it went down, it left new, dark soil.

**Teach card 2:** The Egyptian year had three seasons: Akhet, the flood. Peret, the growing time. Shemu, the harvest.

**Season wheel lines:**
- **Akhet · Flood:** The river covers the fields. Farmers mend tools and banks.
- **Peret · Growing:** The water goes down. Seeds go into the wet soil. Wheat and barley grow.
- **Shemu · Harvest:** The crops are cut. The river is at its lowest.
- **Wheel note:** These are English names for Egyptian words. Books translate them a little differently.

**Teach card 3:** Farmers built earth banks to hold floodwater on their fields. A shaduf lifted water by hand when they needed more.

**Shaduf worked example:**
1. A long pole sits on a post, like a seesaw.
2. A bucket hangs on the long end. A heavy weight sits on the short end.
3. Pull the bucket down into the water.
4. The weight helps swing it up. Tip it into the field channel.

**Shaduf bench line (always shown):** Made-up numbers for practice. Each lift here brings 10 liters. Real buckets varied.
**Shaduf history note:** The oldest Egyptian pictures of shadufs are more than 3,000 years old. That's long after the Giza pyramids.

## Page 3. Measuring the flood

**Teach card 1:** A nilometer is a gauge for the river. Water climbs steps or a column with marks. People read the highest mark.

**Teach card 2:** Too low a flood: dry fields and hunger. Too high: broken banks and spoiled crops. People hoped for in between.

**Teach card 3:** They measured in cubits. A royal cubit was about 52 cm. In this game, we use 52 cm.

**Worked example (one step per tap):**
1. The water has reached the 14th mark.
2. So the reading is 14 cubits.
3. Find 14 on the judging card: "gladness".
4. A good flood.

**Judging card:**
| Cubits | The card says |
| --- | --- |
| 12 | Famine |
| 13 | Hunger |
| 14 | Gladness |
| 15 | Security |
| 16 | Plenty |
| above 17 | Too high: banks may break |

**Judging card note (always shown):** Based on a scale a Roman writer, Pliny the Elder, gave for one gauge, nearly 2,000 years ago. Our "too high" line is made for this game.

**Teach card 4 (Sapling and Keeper's Desk):** Each gauge counted from its own bottom mark. So a good flood was a different number on different gauges.

**Nilometer places note:** One famous nilometer is a stone stair on Elephantine Island, at Aswan, near Egypt's old southern edge. Its marks are written in Greek and demotic. Another is on Roda Island in Cairo. That one was built more than 1,100 years ago, long after the pyramids.

## Page 4. Counting like a scribe

**Teach card 1:** Scribes were people trained to write. They counted harvests, stores and taxes, and wrote them down.

**Teach card 2:** Egyptian number signs add up. A stroke is 1. A hobble is 10. A coil of rope is 100. A lotus is 1,000.

**Sign note:** The sign for 10 is a hobble, a loop once used for tying cattle. Some books call it a heel bone.

**Worked example:**
1. Look at the signs: one hobble and two strokes.
2. The hobble is 10.
3. Two strokes are 2.
4. 10 and 2 make 12.

**Teach card 3:** There is no sign for zero. If a number has no tens, you simply leave the hobbles out.

**Teach card 4 (calendar):** Their calendar had 12 months of 30 days, plus 5 extra days. That makes 365 days.

**Teach card 5 (calendar, Sapling):** The real year is about 365 and a quarter days. So their calendar slipped about 1 day every 4 years.

**Worked example (calendar):**
1. Each year, the calendar is about a quarter day short.
2. Four quarters make one whole day.
3. So after 4 years, it is 1 day out.
4. After 40 years: 10 days out.

**Calendar slider line (always shown):** A simplified picture. The real year is a tiny bit less than 365 and a quarter days. Real floods also came early or late.

## Page 5. How we know

**Teach card 1:** We learn about ancient Egypt from things that survived: buildings, objects, and writing on stone and papyrus.

**Teach card 2:** An object shows some things. People who study it explain other things. Both matter. They are different.

**Worked example (granary model):**
1. The photo shows little wooden figures. Some carry sacks.
2. Some figures hold boards or rolls for writing.
3. The Met explains it as a granary with an area for counting grain.
4. The photo shows the figures. The explanation comes from the museum.

**Granary source card:** *Model of a Granary with Scribes.* Ancient Egypt, from the tomb of Meketre, about 1981 to 1975 BCE. Wood, plaster, paint, linen and grain. The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 20.3.11. *(Build note: confirm the image's Open Access license and alt text against the delivered photo.)*
**Alt text:** A small wooden model building with an open courtyard. Little figures carry sacks; others sit holding writing boards.

**Teach card 3 (Rosetta):** The Rosetta Stone has one message in three scripts. One was Greek, which scholars could read.

**Rosetta worked example:**
1. The stone has the same decree three times: hieroglyphs, demotic and Greek.
2. Scholars could read the Greek.
3. So they could guess what the hieroglyphs said.
4. In 1822, Champollion announced how the hieroglyphs worked.

**Teach card 4 (tombs, gently):** In 1922, Howard Carter found Tutankhamun's tomb almost untouched. A king's burial place is also a person's grave.

**Teach card 5 (ethics):** Today people ask: who should keep old objects? How do we treat the dead with respect? People disagree.

**Rosetta source card:** *The Rosetta Stone.* A decree from 196 BCE. Found in 1799. In the British Museum, London, since 1802. Described in words here.

## Then and now

**Card:**
> In 1970, a huge dam at Aswan was finished. Behind it, a lake holds back the flood. Below the dam, the Nile no longer floods every year.
>
> **What farmers gained:** steady water all year, and more than one crop.
> **What changed:** the dark silt stays behind the dam. Farmers add fertilizer instead.
>
> The old Nile year of flood, growing and harvest ended below the dam. Its names and its story are still here.

### Set E. How we know

**E1 · Sapling · order the cards**
Prompt: Put these in time order, earliest first.
Cards: The Great Pyramid is built (about 2550 BCE) · The granary model is made (about 1980 BCE) · The Rosetta Stone decree (196 BCE) · Champollion explains hieroglyphs (1822 CE)
Answer: Pyramid, granary model, Rosetta decree, Champollion.
Misconception: `bce-bigger-is-later` (placing 196 BCE before 1980 BCE).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Nile | The river that flows north through Egypt to the Mediterranean Sea. |
| Delta | Flat, wide land where a river splits into many channels near the sea. |
| Upper Egypt | The southern valley, upstream. |

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches.** Egypt's geography (a green river strip in desert; Upper Egypt is south), the yearly Nile flood and the three seasons, basin farming and the shaduf, reading a nilometer in cubits and why too low or too high a flood was trouble, Egyptian numerals, the 365-day calendar and its drift, and the difference between what an object shows and what people interpret. At the most advanced depth: the Rosetta Stone and Champollion, Tutankhamun's tomb, evidence about the pyramid builders, and questions about who keeps ancient objects and how the dead are treated.

**What it does not claim.** It is not a full course on ancient Egypt. Gods, mummification and pharaohs are left to other specs. The judging card is based on one Roman writer's scale for one gauge; its "too high" line is made for the game. Shaduf and harvest numbers are made up. The calendar slider is simplified. Willowbank, Hob, Hollis and Nuria are fiction.

**Sources in brief.** Britannica (Nile hydrology, Egyptian calendar, Aswan High Dam, shaduf, Rosetta Stone), The Metropolitan Museum of Art (telling time, papyrus, granary model), the British Museum (Rosetta Stone and decipherment), Harvard's Digital Giza and AERA (Giza and the workers' town), MacTutor and the MAA (Egyptian numerals), UCL Digital Egypt (the cubit), National Geographic (nilometers and taxes). Full list in the source audit.

**Off-screen, adult-led (optional).**
- **Make a gauge.** With a grown-up, stand a ruler in a clear jar and mark it with tape. Add water in steps and read the level. Then use a second ruler that starts higher. Do the two read the same? *Adult-led. Use cold water; mop spills to avoid slips.*
- **Count like a scribe.** Draw strokes, hobbles and coils on paper and write a page number from a favorite book.
- **A seesaw lift.** With a grown-up, balance a ruler on a pencil with a small cup taped to one end and a lump of clay on the other. *Adult-led; no water needed.*
