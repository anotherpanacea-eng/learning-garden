# The Measuring Walk: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 208](../specs/208-measuring-walk.md). Use as written, after independent spec review and a math check. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion and is never gendered. Pip is he/him, Dot she/her, Bramble she/her (with her magpie), the baker he/him, Hob Pennick he/him, the miller she/her. Real-world facts are in the [source audit](../source-audits/208-measuring-walk.md); items still marked `[check]` stay off screen until cleared. Nothing here is scored or timed.

## Labels used on every card

- **A story from Willowbank**: the town, its planks, loaves, sacks and every character are made up.
- **A true thing about math**: checked math, explained simply.
- **A true thing from history**: something people really made or did.
- **A made-up model**: a simple game rule, shown on the card, that is not a real measurement.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. Pip's two planks (Act I, chapter 4: mend the village)

**Hook:** The footbridge needs planks. Pip is sure which plank is longer. He has not lined them up.

**Short:**
> Two planks lie on the grass. "This one's longer," says Pip. "It sticks out." Dot slides both to the gate post. Now the other one sticks out.

**Longer:**
> The footbridge still has gaps, and Hob Pennick has brought two planks up from the ford on his cart.
>
> "We need the longer one for the middle," says Dot.
>
> Pip points. "That one. Look how far it sticks out."
>
> The red plank does stick out. It also starts far past the other one.
>
> "Where does each one start?" asks {guide}.
>
> Pip looks at the near ends. One is by his paw. One is by his tail.
>
> Hob drags both planks until their ends touch the gate post. Now the blue plank goes past the red one by a hand.
>
> "Huh," says Pip. "The blue one was hiding."
>
> "Line up the start," says Hob, "then look at the finish. My father said that about fence posts."
>
> Pip lines up two sticks, just to check.

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> The red plank reached farther only because it started farther along. Comparing far ends only works when the near ends match. That is why races begin on one line.

### Scene 2. The door that would not move (Act I, chapter 4: mend the village)

**Hook:** The bakery needs a new shelf. Will it stand under the door frame on its way in? Neither one can be carried to the other.

**Short:**
> The shelf is in the yard. The door is in the wall. "Bring the string," says {guide}. The string can travel. The door can't.

**Longer:**
> The miller has built the baker a tall new shelf.
>
> "Will it go through my door standing up?" he asks. "I'd rather not find out by getting it stuck."
>
> The door can't come to the yard. The shelf is too heavy to try.
>
> {guide} holds up a piece of string. "Let's copy the shelf's height."
>
> Dot holds one end at the bottom of the shelf. Pip pulls the string to the top and pinches it there.
>
> Then they carry the string, still pinched, to the door. One end on the doorstep, the other end up.
>
> The pinch stops well below the top of the frame.
>
> "The string is shorter than the door," says Pip. "And the string is the shelf. So the shelf is shorter than the door."
>
> The baker carries the shelf in standing up. It clears the frame with room for a loaf on top.

### Scene 3. Small and heavy (Act II, chapter 1: the weekly market)

**Hook:** At market, Hob Pennick's small sack of potatoes and the baker's big bag of rolls go on the balance. Pip bets on the big one.

**Short:**
> "The big bag is heavier," says Pip. "Obviously." The baker sets both on the balance. The small potato sack goes down.

**Longer:**
> The market is back on the green. Hob Pennick has a small sack of potatoes. The baker has a huge paper bag of bread rolls, light and puffy.
>
> "They want to swap," says Dot. "Fair swap means the same weight."
>
> "Then the baker loses," says Pip. "The bag is way bigger."
>
> Bramble lifts one in each hand. Her eyebrows go up. "Pip," she says, "hold these."
>
> Pip holds them. His left paw sinks. That is the potato paw.
>
> The baker sets them on his balance. The potato side goes down. The roll side goes up.
>
> "The side that goes down is heavier," says the baker. "Size can fool your eyes. It can't fool a balance."
>
> The baker adds two more bags of rolls to make it fair. The balance sits level. Everyone shakes hands, and Pip eats a roll to celebrate.

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> A big thing can be light and a small thing can be heavy. Your eyes see size. Your hands and a balance feel weight. Trust the balance when they disagree.

### Scene 4. A hundred meters of ribbon (Act II, chapter 3: Ember's birthday)

**Hook:** Bramble wants a banner across the café for Ember. She guesses it needs a hundred meters of ribbon.

**Short:**
> "A hundred meters of ribbon," says Bramble. Dot taps the benchmark card. "A door is about two meters. Is the café fifty doors wide?"

**Longer:**
> Ember's birthday is tomorrow. Bramble wants a ribbon banner across the front of the café.
>
> "I'll need a hundred meters," she says.
>
> Dot pulls out the benchmark card. It says a door is about 2 meters tall.
>
> "Lie a door down along the café," says Dot. "In your head. How many doors fit?"
>
> Bramble squints at the café. "Three. Maybe four."
>
> "So about how many meters?"
>
> "Six to eight," says Bramble. "Fine. I'll say seven."
>
> They measure with the tape. The café front is 7 meters and 40 centimeters.
>
> "Under," says Pip, "by a bit."
>
> "Close," says Bramble. "Much closer than a hundred." She cuts 8 meters of ribbon, to have some left for bows.

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> A guess with nothing to compare it to can be very far off. A benchmark, a length you already know, keeps an estimate close. Then you measure and see how near you came.

## Teach cards

Each card is under 30 words. Worked steps appear one tap at a time.

**T1. Line up the ends** (A true thing about math)
> To compare two lengths, line up one end of each. Then look at the other end. The one that goes farther is longer.
Worked example: 1. Two planks, starts not lined up. 2. Slide both to the gate post. 3. The blue plank goes farther. It is longer.

**T2. Taller starts at the floor** (A true thing about math)
> To compare heights, both things must stand on the same floor. A step under one makes it look taller.
Worked example: 1. One tower stands on a step. 2. Move it to the floor. 3. Now the other tower is taller.

**T3. Bending doesn't change length** (A true thing about math)
> Curl a rope and its ends come closer. The rope is still the same length. Straighten it to check.
Worked example: 1. Two ropes match. 2. Curl one. 3. Straighten it again. 4. They still match.

**T4. Put three in order** (A true thing about math)
> Find the shortest first. Then find the shortest of the ones left. Keep going.
Worked example: 1. Three ribbons. 2. Shortest: gold. 3. Next: red. 4. Last: blue, the longest.

**T5. A string can carry a length** (A true thing about math)
> If two things can't meet, copy one length with a string. Keep the string the same. Compare it with the other.
Worked example: 1. String matches the shelf. 2. At the door, the string is shorter. 3. So the shelf is shorter than the door.

**T6. Same units, no gaps** (A true thing about math)
> Measure with units that are all the same size. Lay them end to end. No gaps, no overlaps. Count them and say the unit.
Worked example: 1. Clips touch, end to end. 2. Count: 8. 3. Say "8 clips long."

**T7. Small units, more of them** (A true thing about math)
> Measure one bench with small cubes, then big blocks. Small units take more. Big units take fewer. The bench stays the same.
Worked example: 1. Bench: 4 big blocks. 2. Same bench: 8 small cubes. 3. Each block is two cubes.

**T8. Start at zero** (A true thing about math)
> Put the start of the thing at the ruler's 0. Read where it ends. Count spaces, not marks.
Worked example: 1. Spoon starts at 0. 2. It ends at 6. 3. Six spaces: 6 cm.

**T9. A broken ruler still works** (A true thing about math)
> If the ruler starts at 3, count the spaces from the start to the end. Or take away: end minus start.
Worked example: 1. Start at 3. 2. End at 11. 3. 11 − 3 = 8 cm.

**T10. cm, m, mm, km** (A true thing about math)
> A fingertip is about 1 cm wide. A door is about 2 m tall. Ladybugs measure in mm. Long walks measure in km.
Worked example: 1. 10 mm make 1 cm. 2. 100 cm make 1 m. 3. 1000 m make 1 km.

**T11. How much longer?** (A true thing about math)
> Measure both in the same unit. Take the shorter from the longer. Say the answer with its unit.
Worked example: 1. New plank: 110 cm. 2. Old plank: 90 cm. 3. 110 − 90 = 20. 4. The new plank is 20 cm longer.

**T12. The heavier side goes down** (A true thing about math)
> On a balance, the heavier side goes down. Level means the same weight. Big things can be light.
Worked example: 1. Potatoes left, rolls right. 2. Left goes down. 3. The potatoes are heavier.

**T13. Weigh with units** (A true thing about math)
> Put the thing on one side. Add the same-size cubes to the other side until it sits level. Count the cubes.
Worked example: 1. Apple on the left. 2. Add cubes until level. 3. Five cubes: "5 cubes heavy."

**T14. Grams, kilograms and the scale** (A true thing about math)
> Grams (g) weigh light things. Kilograms (kg) weigh heavy things. On a scale, find what one space is worth first.
Worked example: 1. 100 g between labels, 5 spaces. 2. One space: 20 g. 3. Two spaces past 100: 140 g.

**T15. Benchmarks** (A true thing about math)
> A benchmark is a length you already know. Use it to guess other lengths. A fingertip is about 1 cm. A door is about 2 m.
Worked example: 1. Bramble's hand span: 15 cm. 2. The card is two spans long. 3. About 30 cm.

**T16. Estimate, then check** (A true thing about math)
> Say your estimate before you measure. Then measure. Were you over or under? By how much?
Worked example: 1. Estimate: 50 cm. 2. Measured: 48 cm. 3. Over, by 2 cm.

## Real-world and history cards

**H1. The cubit rod** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> In ancient Egypt, builders used a measuring rod called a royal cubit. It was about 52 cm long.
Keeper's note:
> A cubit was split into 7 palms. Each palm was split into 4 fingers, so a cubit had 28 fingers. Rods like this are more than 3,000 years old. One came from the tomb of Maya, a treasurer of King Tutankhamun. It is now in the Louvre in Paris.

**H2. Stone weights from the Indus** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> About 4,000 years ago, in cities by the Indus River, people weighed things with small stone cubes. The weights matched from city to city.
Keeper's note:
> Most were cubes of a stone called chert. The sizes went 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, each double the last. A common weight was about 14 grams. With one of each, you can balance many different amounts.

**H3. The meter and the long survey** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> In the 1790s, two French astronomers measured a long line of land from Dunkirk to Barcelona. They used it to set the length of the meter.
Keeper's note:
> Their names were Jean-Baptiste Delambre and Pierre Méchain. The meter was meant to be one ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the equator. In 1799 a platinum bar of that length was kept in the national archives in Paris.

## Stop (a): The Line-Up Gate

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> Planks, towers and ropes are pictures. They slide but never stretch.

**Opening:** Which is longer? Line them up first.
**Buttons:** Slide to the post · Move to the floor · Straighten · Curl · Which is longer? · Which is taller?
**Pip's turn:** Pip compares without lining up. Can you fix it?
**After a fix:** Now both start together. Look at the far end.

**Guided item:** Pip says the red plank is longer. It sticks out.
- Step 1: Where does each plank start? *(Not in the same place.)*
- Step 2: Slide both to the gate post.
- Step 3: Which goes farther now? *(The blue plank.)*

## Stop (b): The String Carrier

**Model card** (A made-up model):

**Opening:** Some things can't meet. A string can carry a length between them.
**Buttons:** Copy this length · Carry · Hold it here · Order these · Add one more
**Order mode:** Put them in order, shortest first.
**Insert mode:** Where does the new ribbon go?

**Guided item:** Will the shelf fit under the bakery door?
- Step 1: Copy the shelf's height with the string.
- Step 2: Carry it to the door. *(The string is shorter than the door.)*

## Stop (c): The Unit Path

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> Each block is 30 cm. Each cube is 15 cm. Small clips are 3 cm; big clips are 5 cm. Real clips vary.

**Opening:** Measure the bench with units. Say the unit every time.
**Buttons:** Lay a cube · Lay a block · Lay a clip · Close gaps · Count · Use the other unit
**Count line pattern:** "[number] [units] long."
**Gap note:** There's a gap. That part isn't being counted.
**Overlap note:** These overlap. That part is counted twice.
**Mixed note:** These units are not the same size. Use one kind.

**Guided item:** Dot laid clips along a strip with gaps. She counted 6.
- Step 1: Find the gaps. Is that length counted? *(No.)*
- Step 2: Close the gaps and count again. *(8 clips.)*

## Stop (d): The Tape Table

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> A drawn ruler (0 to 30 cm, mm marks), a meter tape (0 to 5 m) and a broken ruler that starts at 4.

**Opening:** Measure from zero. Count spaces, not marks.
**Buttons:** Slide to zero · Use the broken ruler · Zoom to mm · Measure both · How much longer?
**Pick a tool:** Short things fit the ruler. Long things, like a door, need the tape.
**Difference strip:** Both lines start together. The extra bit is the difference.

**Guided item:** How much taller is the bakery door than the shelf?
- Step 1: The door is too tall for the ruler. Use the tape: 200 cm.
- Step 2: Shelf: 150 cm.
- Step 3: Both in cm, so take away: 200 − 150.
- Step 4: 50 cm taller.

## Stop (e): The Bakery Scales

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> The baker's balance and dial scale are pictures. A loaf here is 600 g; real loaves vary.

**Opening:** Which is heavier? Feel, then check on the balance.
**Buttons:** Hold both · Put on the balance · Add a cube · Take a cube · Use big weights · Use the dial
**Balance status lines:** "Left side down: left is heavier." · "Right side down: right is heavier." · "Level: the same weight."
**Dial prompt:** What is one space worth? Then count spaces.

**Guided item:** A small tin and a big puffy loaf. Which is heavier?
- Step 1: Hold one in each hand. *(The tin pulls down.)*
- Step 2: Put them on the balance. *(The tin side goes down.)*
- Step 3: The tin is heavier, even though it's smaller.

## Stop (f): The Benchmark Card

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> Bramble's hand span is 15 cm, as drawn. Your own hand span is your own number.

**Card lines:** fingertip: about 1 cm · Bramble's hand span: 15 cm · a grown-up's big step: about 1 m · a door: about 2 m
**Opening:** Estimate first. Then measure.
**Buttons:** My estimate · Measure · Over or under?
**Strip headers:** Estimate · Measured · Over or under, by how much

**Guided item:** About how tall is the bakery door?
- Step 1: What benchmark helps? *(A door is about 2 m.)*
- Step 2: Estimate: about 2 m.
- Step 3: Measure with the tape: 200 cm, which is 2 m. Right on.

## Problem sets

Eighteen three-choice items put the right answer at A six times, B six times and C six times.

### Set A. Line up and compare (Sprout and Seedling)

**A1 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Pip says the red plank is longer. It sticks out. What first?
- A. Line up the starts, then look at the ends. *(right)*
- B. Agree with Pip. Sticking out means longer. *(tests: judging by one end)*
- C. Pick the wider plank. *(tests: "bigger" for longer)*
Hint: Where does each plank start?
Explanation: Line up one end. Then the far ends show which is longer.
Feedback A: Yes. Same start, then look at the finish.
Feedback B: It sticks out because it starts farther along. Line up the starts.
Feedback C: Wide isn't long. Line them up end to end.

**A2 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: One tower stands on a step. Which tower is taller?
- A. The one on the step, because its top is higher up. *(tests: ignoring the step)*
- B. The one that is taller when both stand on the floor. *(right)*
- C. The one with the biggest block sitting on its top. *(tests: judging by one part)*
Hint: Do both towers start on the floor?
Explanation: Heights compare from the same floor. The step adds height that isn't the tower.
Feedback A: The step lifts it. Move it to the floor and look again.
Feedback B: Yes. Same floor, then compare the tops.
Feedback C: One block doesn't tell. Compare whole towers from the floor.

**A3 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Bramble curls one rope. Is it shorter now?
- A. Yes. Its ends are closer together. *(tests: length changes when bent)*
- B. It got longer. It has loops now. *(tests: loops add length)*
- C. No. Straighten it and the ropes still match. *(right)*
Hint: What happens if you straighten it?
Explanation: Curling moves the ends. The rope itself stays the same length.
Feedback A: The ends moved. The rope didn't shrink. Straighten it to check.
Feedback B: Loops don't add rope. Straighten it and see.
Feedback C: Yes. Same rope, same length, curled or straight.

**A4 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Blue is longer than red. Red is longer than gold. Longest?
- A. Gold, the last one named. *(tests: reversing the order)*
- B. Blue. *(right)*
- C. Nobody can tell until blue sits next to gold. *(tests: no chaining; withholds more than the evidence needs)*
Hint: Put them in a line: which beats red?
Explanation: Blue beats red, and red beats gold. So blue beats gold too.
Feedback A: Gold is shorter than red. Red is shorter than blue.
Feedback B: Yes. Blue, then red, then gold.
Feedback C: Red links them. Blue beats red, red beats gold, so you can tell.

**A5 (Seedling, order)**
Prompt: Put the three planks in order, shortest first.
Picture: plank 1 (long), plank 2 (short), plank 3 (middle). All start at the gate post.
Answer: plank 2, plank 3, plank 1.
Tests: ordering by where the plank is in the picture; starting with the longest.
Hint: Find the shortest one first.
Explanation: Shortest, then next shortest, then the longest.

**A6 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: The string matches the shelf. At the door, it's shorter. So?
- A. The door is taller than the shelf. *(right)*
- B. The shelf is taller than the door. *(tests: reversing the link)*
- C. The string shrank on the way. *(tests: the go-between changes)*
Hint: The string is a copy of the shelf.
Explanation: The string is the shelf's height. It's shorter than the door, so the shelf is too.
Feedback A: Yes. Shelf equals string, string is shorter than door.
Feedback B: The string matched the shelf and didn't reach the door's top.
Feedback C: Strings don't shrink when carried. Pinch it and it stays the same.

### Set C. How much longer, and which unit (Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**C4 (Keeper's Desk, number)**

**C5 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**

- C. No. Table B is longer. *(right)*

### Set D. Heavier, lighter, grams (Sprout, Seedling and Sapling)

**D1 (Sprout, 3-choice)**

Feedback A: Big can be light. Look at which side went down.
Feedback B: The lighter side goes up. Which went down?

**D2 (Sprout, 3-choice)**

- B. The pillow. It's bigger. *(tests: bigger means heavier)*
- C. The pillow. It's taller. *(tests: mixing up height and weight)*

Explanation: The tin is small and heavy. The pillow is big and light.
Feedback A: Yes. Small, but heavy.
Feedback B: Big things can be light. Feel them both.
Feedback C: Tall isn't heavy. Which pulls your hand down?

**D3 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: An apple balances 5 cubes. A pear balances 7. Which is heavier?
- A. The pear. *(right)*
- B. Same. Both made the balance level. *(tests: level means equal across different tests)*
- C. Nobody can tell. They're different fruits. *(tests: withholds more than the evidence needs)*
Hint: Same cubes. Which needed more?
Explanation: The cubes are all the same. More cubes means heavier: 7 is more than 5.
Feedback A: Yes. 7 cubes is heavier than 5 of the same cubes.
Feedback B: Level means each fruit matched its own cubes. The pear needed more.
Feedback C: The same cubes let you compare: 7 beats 5.

**D4 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: A loaf balances 4 big weights, or 12 small ones. Heavier when?
- A. With 12. It's a bigger number. *(tests: the count is the weight, any unit)*
- B. Neither. One loaf, two units. *(right)*
- C. With 4. Big weights win. *(tests: big units mean more weight)*
Hint: Did the loaf change?
Explanation: The loaf stays the same. Small weights take more of them.
Feedback A: More small weights, same loaf. Each small one is lighter.
Feedback B: Yes. Same loaf; the unit changed the count.
Feedback C: Fewer big weights, same loaf. Nothing got heavier.

**D5 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: Labels go 0, 100, 200, 300 g. Five spaces between. The arrow?
Picture: the arrow sits 3 spaces past 200.
Answer: 260 g.
Worked steps: 1. 100 g across 5 spaces. 2. One space: 100 ÷ 5 = 20 g. 3. 200 + 20 + 20 + 20 = 260 g.
Tests: 203 (each space as 1 g); 230 (each space as 10 g).
Hint: What is one space worth?
Explanation: Find a space's worth first. Then count spaces from the last label.

**D6 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which fits the baker's flour sack?
- A. 5 g. *(tests: a unit far too small)*
- B. 5 km. *(tests: a length unit for weight)*
- C. 5 kg. *(right)*
Hint: Grams for light things, kilograms for heavy ones.
Explanation: A sack of flour is heavy. Kilograms fit.

## "I'm stuck" worked examples

Step 2 of the ladder: an easier case, then how to start this one.

- **Line-up items:** "Two pencils start at one line. The one that goes farther is longer. Start: do yours start together?"
- **Order items:** "Three socks: find the shortest first. Then the shortest one left. Start: which is shortest?"
- **String items:** "The string fits the box. It's shorter than the table. So the box is shorter. Start: what did the string copy?"
- **Unit items:** "Two hops or four little steps across one rug. Little steps take more. Start: are your units big or small?"
- **Ruler items:** "From 2 to 5 is three spaces: 3 cm. Start: count spaces from the start."
- **Difference items:** "A 10 cm stick and a 7 cm stick: 3 cm longer. Start: are both in the same unit?"
- **Weight items:** "A rock and a balloon: the rock side goes down. Start: which side went down?"
- **Scale items:** "Labels every 10, two spaces between: each space is 5. Start: what is one space worth?"
- **Estimate items:** "A door is about 2 m. Two doors are about 4 m. Start: what benchmark fits?"

Step 3: **Skip for now.** You can come back any time.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **length**: how long something is, from one end to the other.
- **height**: how tall something is, from the floor up.
- **longer, shorter, taller**: words for comparing lengths and heights.
- **unit**: the same-size piece you count when you measure.
- **centimeter (cm)**: a small unit of length, about a fingertip wide.
- **millimeter (mm)**: a tiny unit; 10 mm make 1 cm.
- **meter (m)**: 100 cm; a door is about 2 m.
- **kilometer (km)**: 1000 m; for long walks.
- **inch**: a unit of length a bit longer than 2 cm.
- **ruler**: a strip marked in equal units, starting at 0.
- **difference**: how much longer, taller or heavier one thing is.
- **heavier, lighter**: words for comparing weight.
- **balance**: a scale with two sides; the heavier side goes down.
- **gram (g)**: a small unit of weight.
- **kilogram (kg)**: 1000 g; for heavy things.
- **benchmark**: a length or weight you already know, used to estimate.
- **estimate**: a careful guess, made before measuring.

## Off-screen invitation (optional, grown-up-led)

**Benchmark hunt** (Seedling and up): with a grown-up, find something about 1 cm wide (a fingertip, perhaps) and something about 1 m long. Guess the length of a table in your benchmark, then measure it with a tape. Were you over or under? *Grown-up note: nothing is entered on screen. ## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** comparing lengths and heights directly by lining up one end; ordering three and comparing with a string as a go-between; measuring with equal non-standard units, no gaps or overlaps; how the unit's size changes the count; reading a ruler from zero and from a broken start; cm, m, mm and km as sizes; how much longer, in one unit; comparing weight by feel and balance, weighing with cubes, grams and kilograms, reading a scale between labels; estimating lengths with benchmarks, then checking.

**What it does not claim:** the pictures are models, not real measuring. Unit conversion is only recapped (1 m = 100 cm) and is taught in a separate pack. A screen cannot give the feel of weight; the hefting is best done with real objects. Bramble's hand span is made up; your child's span is their own.
