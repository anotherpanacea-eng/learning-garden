## Labels used on every card

- **A story from Willowbank**: the town, the Gazette, the plans, prices and every character are made up.
- **A true thing about math**: checked math, explained simply.
- **A true thing from history**: something people really did or wrote.
- **A made-up model**: a simple game rule, shown on the card, that is not real measurement.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. Two lines on the meeting board (Act IV, chapter 4: the proposal for the low fields)

**Hook:** Before the meeting, the librarian draws what the Wall and the Sponge would cost, year by year, as two lines.

**Short:**
> The librarian draws two lines: the Wall's cost and the Sponge's. "The Sponge is cheaper," says Bramble. "Look at year twenty," says Pip.

**Longer:**
> The librarian pins a graph to the meeting board: years along, dollars up.
> "The Wall costs $9,000 to build," they say, "then $600 a year to mend. The Sponge costs $3,000 to start. Then $1,200 a year, to help the orchard keepers."
> Two lines climb the paper. The Wall's starts higher but climbs more gently.
> "The Sponge is cheaper," says Bramble. "Done."
> Pip puts his paw on the right-hand edge. "Not at year twenty. Look."
> The lines cross at year ten. Before it, the Sponge has cost less. After it, the Wall has.
> "And Some of Each?" asks Dot.
> "No line for it yet," says the librarian. "It's still on the board."
> "So which is cheaper?" asks the orchard keeper.
> "It depends how long you look," says {guide}.
> The librarian caps their pen. "The graph says when. It doesn't say which plan to keep. That part is ours."

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> Wall: 600t + 9000. Sponge: 1200t + 3000, with t in years. They meet at t = 10, both $15,000. Some of Each, the third plan, has no line here. Cost is one thing on the board. Frogs, apples and Les Saules are others.

### Scene 2. A graph a week (Act V, path 2: a Willowbank newspaper)

**Hook:** The librarian wants a graph on every Gazette's back page, with a caption anyone can read.

**Short:**
> "Every Gazette needs a graph," says the librarian. "And a caption that says what the line means. In words."

**Longer:**
> The new Gazette has a back page with nothing on it but a grid.
> "One graph a week," says the librarian. "This week: the press. Two copies are already on the stack. It prints three more each minute."
> Dot writes a table. Pip plots the dots. They sit in a perfect line.
> "Now the caption," says the librarian. "What do the three and the two mean?"
> "Three more each minute," says {guide}. "That's how steep. Two at the start. That's where it begins."
> Bramble reads it over their shoulders. "Put in what it can't tell you," she says. "The press jams. Lines don't."
> The librarian adds a line in small print: *If the press runs smoothly.*

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> Copies = 3 × minutes + 2. A straight line fits a steady press. Real machines stop, so a line is only a guess past the table.

### Scene 3. The photo with a border (Act V, path 2: a Willowbank newspaper)

**Hook:** A square photo of the new reeds gets a border: 2 cm wider one way and 3 cm the other. How much paper?

**Short:**
> The photo is square. The frame adds 2 cm one way, 3 cm the other. "Four pieces," says Dot, drawing a grid.

**Longer:**
> Nuria sent a photo of Vadoalto's reed beds, and it goes on page one. It is square, but nobody can agree how big to print it.
> "Call the side x," says the librarian. "Then the frame adds 2 one way and 3 the other."
> Dot draws a rectangle and splits it into four parts. A big square, two strips and a little corner.
> "x times x," she says. "Then 3x, 2x, and six."
> "Bramble said x squared plus six," says Pip.
> "Bramble forgot the strips," says Dot. "Everyone does, the first time."
> Bramble's magpie steals the ruler.

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> (x + 2)(x + 3) = x² + 3x + 2x + 6 = x² + 5x + 6. Each part of the grid is one product. Four parts, then collect.

## Teach cards

Each card is under 30 words. Worked steps appear one tap at a time.

**T1. Each row is a point** (A true thing about math; Seedling)
> Each row of a table is a point. The first number tells you how far across. The second tells you how far up.
Worked example: 1. Row: 2 minutes, 8 copies. 2. Go 2 across, then 8 up. 3. Put a dot at (2, 8).

**T2. Three points, then a ruler** (A true thing about math; Seedling)
> Plot at least three points. If they sit in a line, join them with a ruler. A point off the line means a slip.
Worked example: 1. (0, 2), (1, 5), (2, 8). 2. They line up. 3. Check (3, 11): on the line too.

**T3. Up per one across** (A true thing about math)
> The gradient is how far a line goes up for each one across. Count in the axis numbers, not in squares.
Worked example: 1. Copies line: go 1 minute across. 2. It goes 3 copies up. 3. Gradient: 3.

**T4. The starting value** (A true thing about math)
> Where the line crosses the up axis, across is zero. That height is the starting value. Its name is the intercept.
Worked example: 1. At 0 minutes the stack has 2 copies. 2. The line crosses the up axis at 2. 3. Intercept: 2.

**T5. y = mx + c** (A true thing about math)
> In y = mx + c, m is the gradient and c is the intercept. Change m and the line tilts. Change c and it slides.
Worked example: 1. y = 3x + 2. 2. m = 3: up 3 for each one across. 3. c = 2: it crosses the up axis at 2.

**T6. Lines that go down** (A true thing about math)
> A negative gradient goes down as you go across. y = −2x + 5 drops 2 for each one across.
Worked example: 1. x = 0: y = 5. 2. x = 1: y = 3. 3. x = 2: y = 1. Down 2 each time.

**T7. Gradient from two points** (A true thing about math)
> Gradient = rise ÷ run. Rise is the change up. Run is the change across. Up goes on top.
Worked example: 1. From (1, 2) to (3, 8). 2. Rise: 8 − 2 = 6. Run: 3 − 1 = 2. 3. 6 ÷ 2 = 3.

**T8. Straight or not?** (A true thing about math)
> Step across by one each time. If y changes by the same amount every step, the graph is a straight line.
Worked example: 1. y: 1, 3, 5, 7. Steps: +2, +2, +2. Straight. 2. y: 0, 1, 4, 9. Steps: +1, +3, +5. Curved.

**T9. Tidy it into y = mx + c** (A true thing about math; Keeper's Desk)
> Get y alone. Whatever you do, do it to every term on both sides.
Worked example: 1. 4x + 2y = 10. 2. Take 4x from both sides: 2y = 10 − 4x. 3. Halve every term: y = 5 − 2x, so y = −2x + 5.

**T10. Where two lines cross** (A true thing about math)
> One rule has many pairs: its whole line. Two lines share one point. That pair fits both rules at once.
Worked example: 1. y = x + 2 and y = 3x − 2. 2. At x = 2: 2 + 2 = 4, and 6 − 2 = 4. 3. They cross at (2, 4).

**T11. Check in both rules** (A true thing about math)
> A drawn crossing can be a little off. Put the pair back into both rules. Both must come out true.
Worked example: 1. Try (1, 3). 2. y = x + 2: 3 = 3, true. 3. y = 3x − 2: 3 = 1? Not true. Not the pair.

**T12. Take one rule away from the other** (A true thing about math; Keeper's Desk)
> Line the two rules up. Add or subtract them so one letter disappears. Solve, then put it back to find the other.
Worked example: 1. p + m = 40 and p + 3m = 64. 2. Subtract: 2m = 24, so m = 12. 3. p + 12 = 40, so p = 28.

**T13. Put one rule into the other** (A true thing about math; Keeper's Desk)
> If one rule says what a letter equals, put that into the other rule in its place.
Worked example: 1. b = 2s and b + s = 18. 2. Put in 2s for b: 2s + s = 18. 3. 3s = 18, so s = 6 and b = 12.

**T14. Two brackets make four parts** (A true thing about math)
> Two brackets make a rectangle with four parts. Every term times every term. Then collect like terms.
Worked example: 1. 12 × 13 = (10 + 2)(10 + 3). 2. Parts: 100, 30, 20, 6. 3. Total: 156. 4. (x + 2)(x + 3) = x² + 3x + 2x + 6 = x² + 5x + 6.

**T15. Minus signs in the grid** (A true thing about math)
> A minus sign belongs to its term. +4 times −1 is −4. Fill all four parts first, then collect.
Worked example: 1. (x + 4)(x − 1). 2. Parts: x², −x, 4x, −4. 3. Collect: x² + 3x − 4.

**T16. A bracket squared** (A true thing about math; Keeper's Desk)
> (a + 3)² means (a + 3)(a + 3). It has two middle parts. It is not a² + 9.
Worked example: 1. Parts: a², 3a, 3a, 9. 2. Collect: a² + 6a + 9. 3. Check a = 2: 5² = 25, and 4 + 12 + 9 = 25.

**T17. The U shape** (A true thing about math)
> In y = x², a negative number times itself is positive. So −3 and 3 give the same height. Join the points in a smooth U.
Worked example: 1. x = −3 and x = 3: y = 9. 2. x = −1 and x = 1: y = 1. 3. x = 0: y = 0, the lowest point.

**T18. The fold and the crossings** (A true thing about math)
> A U folds along its symmetry line. Where it crosses the across axis, y is 0. Those x values are the roots.
Worked example: 1. y = x² − 4. 2. It crosses at x = −2 and x = 2. 3. Fold line: x = 0. Lowest point: (0, −4).

**T19. Read up, then across** (A true thing about math)
> Read the scale first. Find your number on one axis. Go to the line, then across to the other axis. Say "about".
Worked example: 1. Up axis: each square is 2. 2. From x = 1.5, go up to the press line. 3. Across: between 6 and 7, about 6.5.

## Real-world and history cards

**H1. Pictures of change** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> About 670 years ago in France, Nicole Oresme drew how a speed changed. Time ran along a line. The speed at each moment stood up as a height.
Keeper's note:
> Oresme wrote about "configurations" of qualities and motions in the 1350s. For a speed growing steadily, the tops of his heights made a straight slanting edge. He used the shape's area to find the distance covered. A graph today works on a similar idea: one thing along, another thing up.

**H2. Rods on a counting board** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> An old Chinese book, The Nine Chapters, solves problems with several unknowns at once. Counting rods were set out in columns, then one column was taken from another.
Keeper's note:
> Chapter 8 is called *fangcheng*, often translated "rectangular arrays". Its first problem: 3 bundles of top grain, 2 of middle and 1 of low make 39 measures; two more lines like it follow. Taking columns away from each other leaves one unknown at a time. The book was put together about 2,000 years ago. It is the same chapter as the red and black rods at the flood post.

**H3. A ball's curved path** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> In 1638, Galileo showed that a ball rolled off a table falls along a curve. The curve is part of a parabola, the shape of y = x².
Keeper's note:
> In *Two New Sciences*, Galileo joined a steady sideways motion with a fall that speeds up. The fall grows with the square of the time, so the path bends. Its shape is half of an upside-down U.

## Bench (a): Table to Points

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> The press starts with 2 copies and prints 3 more each minute. Real presses jam.

**Opening:** Each row of the table is a point. Plot them.
**Buttons:** Plot this row · Check a third point · Join with a ruler · Show the table
**After three points:** Do your points sit in a line?
**If a point is off the line:** One point is off the line. Check that row again.
**Table headers:** Minutes · Copies

**Guided item:** Plot the row "2 minutes, 8 copies".
- Step 1: Find 2 on the across axis.
- Step 2: Go up to 8.
- Step 3: Put your dot there: (2, 8).

## Bench (b): The Line Slider

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> m goes from −3 to 3 in halves. c goes from −5 to 5. The grid is drawn to scale.

**Opening:** Move m. Then move c. Watch what changes.
**Buttons:** m slider · c slider · Show up-per-one-across · Show where it crosses · Same line, new scale
**Step triangle label:** "1 across, [m] up" (or "1 across, [m] down" when m is negative)
**Scale card:** Same line, new scale. It looks steeper. The gradient is still [m].

**Guided item:** Make y = 3x + 2.
- Step 1: Set c to 2. Where does the line cross the up axis? *(At 2.)*
- Step 2: Set m to 3. *(It climbs 3 for each one across.)*
- Step 3: Compare with the press line. *(The same line.)*

## Bench (c): Straight or Not?

**Model card:** Every table steps across by 1.

**Opening:** Is the graph a straight line? Check the steps.
**Buttons:** Show the steps · Plot it · Straight · Curved
**Step row label:** "Change each step: [list]"
**After sorting:** Same change every step: straight. Changing change: curved.

**Guided item:** y: 2, 5, 8, 11. Straight or curved?
- Step 1: Find each change. *(+3, +3, +3.)*
- Step 2: The same every step. *(Straight.)*

## Bench (d): Where Lines Cross

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> The plans' costs are invented. Real costs change with prices and floods.

**Opening:** Two rules, two lines. Where do they share a point?
**Buttons:** Draw rule 1 · Draw rule 2 · Show the crossing · Check in both · Subtract the rules · Put one into the other
**Year slider label:** Year [t]: Wall $[W], Sponge $[S]
**After a crossing is shown:** Check it: put the pair into both rules.
**Ending note** (shown after any work on the plans): The lines show when each costs less. Some of Each has no line here. Which plan to keep is still your choice.

**Guided item:** y = x + 2 and y = 3x − 2. Where do they cross?
- Step 1: Draw both lines.
- Step 2: Read the crossing. *(About (2, 4).)*
- Step 3: Check: 2 + 2 = 4, and 3 × 2 − 2 = 4. Both true.

## Bench (e): The Area Grid

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> Letter sides can be any length. The picture is not to scale.

**Opening:** Split the rectangle. Find each part. Then collect.
**Buttons:** Split the sides · Fill a part · Collect · Check with a number
**Number check label:** Put in x = [n]: [before] and [after]. Same?

**Guided item:** (x + 2)(x + 3).
- Step 1: The four parts: x times x, x times 3, 2 times x, 2 times 3.
- Step 2: x², 3x, 2x, 6.
- Step 3: Collect: x² + 5x + 6.

## Bench (f): The U Curve

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> x goes from −3 to 4. Points are joined with a smooth curve.

**Opening:** Make the table. Plot the points. Join them smoothly.
**Buttons:** Fill the table · Plot · Join smoothly · Show the fold line · Show the crossings · Read up, then across
**If joined with straight pieces:** A U bends all the way. Try a smooth curve.

**Guided item:** y = x² at x = −3.
- Step 1: −3 × −3. *(A negative times a negative is positive.)*
- Step 2: 9.
- Step 3: The point (−3, 9) is level with (3, 9).

## Problem sets

Twenty-four three-choice items put the right answer at A eight times, B eight times and C eight times.

### Set A. From a table to points (Seedling and Sapling)

All Seedling items use the press table: minutes 0, 1, 2, 3; copies 2, 5, 8, 11.

**A1 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: The table says 2 minutes, 8 copies. Where is that point?
- A. 2 across, then 8 up. *(right)*
- B. 8 across, then 2 up. *(tests: swapping the order)*
- C. 10 across, the two added. *(tests: combining the pair into one number)*
Hint: The first number tells you how far across.
Explanation: Across first, then up. The point is (2, 8).
Feedback A: Yes. Across 2, up 8.
Feedback B: That point is (8, 2). Which number comes first?
Feedback C: A point needs both numbers: one across, one up.

**A2 (Seedling, number)**
Prompt: What comes next in the table, at 4 minutes?
Answer: 14 copies.
Worked steps: 1. Each minute adds 3. 2. At 3 minutes: 11. 3. 11 + 3 = 14.
Tests: 12 (forgetting the 2 at the start: 4 × 3); 16 (doubling the 2-minute count).
Hint: How many does each minute add?
Explanation: The stack grows by 3 each minute, from 11 to 14.

**A3 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Pip plotted (3, 10). It sits just below the line. Why?
- A. The line must bend at 3 minutes. *(tests: an off-line point means a curve)*
- B. A slip. The table says 11 at 3 minutes. *(right)*
- C. Move the line to go through his dot. *(tests: trusting one point over three)*
Hint: Look at the 3-minute row again.
Explanation: Three points lined up. The fourth was a plotting slip.
Feedback A: The table adds 3 every minute, so the line stays straight.
Feedback B: Yes. Check the row, then move the dot.
Feedback C: Three points agree. Check the odd one first.

**A4 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: At 0 minutes, where does the point go?
- A. At (0, 0). Every line starts there. *(tests: all lines go through zero)*
- B. Nowhere. Zero minutes means no point. *(tests: zero as nothing to plot)*
- C. At (0, 2), on the up axis. *(right)*
Hint: How many copies are on the stack at the start?
Explanation: Two copies were there before the press started.
Feedback A: The stack had 2 copies already. Lines can start higher.
Feedback B: Zero minutes is a real row: 0 minutes, 2 copies.
Feedback C: Yes. The line starts at 2.

**A5 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: y = 2x − 1. What is y when x = 3?
Answer: 5.
Worked steps: 1. 2 × 3 = 6. 2. 6 − 1 = 5. 3. Point (3, 5).
Tests: 6 (leaving out the − 1); 4 (taking 1 before multiplying: 2 × 2).
Hint: Multiply first, then take away.
Explanation: Twice 3 is 6, and one less is 5.

**A6 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: y = 2x − 1. Which point goes with x = −2?
- A. (−2, −5) *(right)*
- B. (−2, 3) *(tests: 2 × −2 read as +4)*
- C. (−5, −2) *(tests: swapping the order)*
Hint: What is 2 × −2?
Explanation: 2 × −2 = −4, and −4 − 1 = −5.
Feedback A: Yes. Across −2, down 5.
Feedback B: 2 × −2 is −4, a negative. Try again from there.
Feedback C: Right numbers, swapped. x goes first.

### Set B. What m and c mean (Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**B1 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: y = 2x + 3. Where does the line cross the up axis?
- A. At 2. *(tests: swapping gradient and intercept)*
- B. At 3. *(right)*
- C. At 5. *(tests: adding m and c)*
Hint: Put x = 0. What is y?
Explanation: At x = 0, y = 3. That is the intercept, c.
Feedback A: The 2 says how steep. Put x = 0 to find the crossing.
Feedback B: Yes. c is where it crosses.
Feedback C: At x = 0, the 2x part is 0.

**B2 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which line is steeper: y = 3x + 1 or y = x + 5?
- A. y = x + 5. It starts higher. *(tests: higher read as steeper)*
- B. Neither. Straight lines are all equally steep. *(tests: straight means same tilt)*
- C. y = 3x + 1. It climbs 3 per one across. *(right)*
Hint: Look at m, the number with x.
Explanation: Steepness is m. 3 is more than 1.
Feedback A: Higher is where it starts. Steeper is how fast it climbs.
Feedback B: Straight lines can tilt by different amounts. Compare m.
Feedback C: Yes. Bigger m, steeper line.

**B3 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: The Wall costs W = 600t + 9000 after t years. What does 600 mean?
- A. $600 more for each year. *(right)*
- B. The wall costs $600 to build. *(tests: reading m as the start)*
- C. The wall lasts 600 years. *(tests: losing the units)*
Hint: What goes up by 600 when t goes up by 1?
Explanation: The gradient is the cost per year: $600 of mending each year.
Feedback A: Yes. Up $600 for each one year across.
Feedback B: Building is the 9000, at t = 0.
Feedback C: t counts years. 600 is dollars for each one.

**B4 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: y = 2x + 1 becomes y = 2x + 4. What happens?
- A. It gets steeper. *(tests: c changes the steepness)*
- B. It slides up 3, with the same tilt. *(right)*
- C. It flips to go down. *(tests: any change flips the line)*
Hint: Which number changed, m or c?
Explanation: Only c changed. The line slides up from 1 to 4.
Feedback A: m is still 2, so the steepness is the same.
Feedback B: Yes. Same m, new starting value.
Feedback C: m is still positive, so the line still climbs.

**B5 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: A line starts at 5 and goes down 2 per one across. Which rule?
- A. y = 5x − 2 *(tests: swapping m and c)*
- B. y = 2x + 5 *(tests: losing the minus)*
- C. y = −2x + 5 *(right)*
Hint: Down means a negative gradient.
Explanation: m = −2 for going down 2. c = 5 for the start.
Feedback A: That climbs 5 each step and starts at −2.
Feedback B: That one climbs. Down needs a minus.
Feedback C: Yes. Down 2, start at 5.

**B6 (Keeper's Desk, say why; spoken to a grown-up, not stored)**
Prompt: Bramble says, "The Sponge is cheaper. Its line starts lower." What would you tell her?
Model answer for the grown-up: It is cheaper at the start. Its line is steeper, $1,200 a year against $600, so it catches up. They cross at year 10; after that the Wall has cost less. Cost is also not the only thing to weigh, and Some of Each is a third plan the graph doesn't show.
Tests: starting value read as the whole story; the graph read as choosing the plan.
Hint: Which line climbs faster? What happens at year 10?
Explanation: The start is one part. The gradient decides what happens later.

### Set C. Straight or not, and gradient from two points (Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**C1 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: x goes 0, 1, 2, 3. Which y values make a straight line?
- A. 0, 1, 4, 9 *(tests: always growing means straight)*
- B. 1, 2, 4, 8 *(tests: a pattern means straight)*
- C. 10, 7, 4, 1 *(right)*
Hint: Find the change at each step.
Explanation: 10, 7, 4, 1 goes down 3 every step. Same change, straight line.
Feedback A: The steps are +1, +3, +5. They grow, so it curves.
Feedback B: The steps are +1, +2, +4. They grow, so it curves.
Feedback C: Yes. −3 each step, a straight line going down.

**C2 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: A line goes through (1, 2) and (3, 8). What is its gradient?
Answer: 3.
Worked steps: 1. Rise: 8 − 2 = 6. 2. Run: 3 − 1 = 2. 3. 6 ÷ 2 = 3.
Tests: 1/3 (run ÷ rise); 6 (the rise alone).
Hint: How far up, for how far across?
Explanation: Up 6 for 2 across is up 3 for each one across.

**C3 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which rule gives a curved graph?
- A. y = x² + 1 *(right)*
- B. y = 4 − x *(tests: a minus means curved)*
- C. y = 5 *(tests: no x means no line)*
Hint: Make a short table for each. Check the steps.
Explanation: x² + 1 gives 1, 2, 5, 10. The steps grow, so it curves.
Feedback A: Yes. The x² makes the steps grow.
Feedback B: That goes down 1 every step: straight.
Feedback C: y is 5 for every x: a flat straight line.

**C4 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: A line goes through (0, 5) and (2, 1). What is its gradient?
Answer: −2.
Worked steps: 1. Rise: 1 − 5 = −4. 2. Run: 2 − 0 = 2. 3. −4 ÷ 2 = −2.
Tests: 2 (losing the minus); −0.5 (run ÷ rise).
Hint: Does the line go up or down from left to right?
Explanation: It drops 4 over 2 across: down 2 for each one across.

**C5 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Tidy 4x + 2y = 10 into y = mx + c.
- A. y = 10 − 4x *(tests: halving only one side's term)*
- B. y = −2x + 5 *(right)*
- C. y = 2x + 5 *(tests: losing the minus when moving 4x)*
Hint: Take 4x from both sides. Then halve every term.
Explanation: 2y = 10 − 4x, so y = 5 − 2x, which is y = −2x + 5.
Feedback A: That is 2y. Halve every term to get y.
Feedback B: Yes. m = −2, c = 5.
Feedback C: Taking 4x away leaves −4x. Keep the minus.

**C6 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Two drawings of y = 2x. One looks steeper. Which gradient is right?
- A. The steeper drawing has the bigger gradient. *(tests: judging by angle)*
- B. Both are 2. Their scales are different. *(right)*
- C. Neither. Graphs can't show a gradient. *(tests: more doubt than the evidence needs)*
Hint: Read each axis's numbers, then count rise and run.
Explanation: Up 2 for each one across, in axis numbers, on both.
Feedback A: The look depends on the scale. Count with the axis numbers.
Feedback B: Yes. Same rule, same gradient, different drawing.
Feedback C: Each graph shows it. Read rise and run with its scale.

### Set D. Where two lines cross (Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**D1 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: y = x + 2 and y = 3x − 2. Which pair fits both?
- A. (2, 4) *(right)*
- B. (1, 3) *(tests: fitting only one rule)*
- C. (0, 2) *(tests: one line's axis crossing)*
Hint: Put each pair into both rules.
Explanation: 2 + 2 = 4 and 3 × 2 − 2 = 4. Both true.
Feedback A: Yes. It fits both, so the lines cross there.
Feedback B: 3 = 1 + 2 works. But 3 × 1 − 2 is 1, not 3.

**D2 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: Wall: 600t + 9000. Sponge: 1200t + 3000. When do they cost the same?

Worked steps: 1. The Sponge starts $6,000 cheaper: 9000 − 3000. 2. It costs $600 more each year: 1200 − 600. 3. 6000 ÷ 600 = 10 years. 4. Check: 600 × 10 + 9000 = 15000; 1200 × 10 + 3000 = 15000.
Tests: 5 (6000 ÷ 1200); 15 (9000 ÷ 600).
Hint: How big is the head start? How fast does it shrink?
Explanation: A $6,000 head start, used up at $600 a year, lasts 10 years.

**D3 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: After 15 years, which plan has cost less?

- B. The Sponge, because it started cheaper. *(tests: the start decides forever)*
- C. Neither. The lines met, so they're equal. *(tests: a crossing holds everywhere)*
Hint: Put t = 15 into both rules.
Explanation: Wall: 9000 + 9000 = 18000. Sponge: 3000 + 18000 = 21000.

Feedback B: It was cheaper until year 10. Check year 15.
Feedback C: They are equal only at year 10.

**D4 (Keeper's Desk, number)**

**D5 (Keeper's Desk, number)**

**D6 (Keeper's Desk, number)**

**E3 (Sapling, 3-choice)**

## Key terms (glossary)

- **table of values**: rows of matching pairs, such as minutes and copies.
- **point**: a place on a grid, written (across, up).
- **gradient**: how far a line goes up for each one across. Negative means down.
- **intercept**: where a line crosses the up axis; the starting value.
- **linear**: makes a straight line; the same change every step.
- **y = mx + c**: a straight line with gradient m and intercept c.
- **rise and run**: the change up and the change across between two points.
- **simultaneous**: true at the same time, for both rules.
- **solution of two rules**: the one pair that fits both.
- **parallel**: same gradient, never meeting.
- **expand**: multiply out brackets, every term times every term.
- **like terms**: terms with the same letter and power, such as 3x and 2x.
- **quadratic**: a rule with x² as its biggest power; its graph is a U.
- **parabola**: the U-shaped curve of a quadratic.
- **symmetry line**: the fold line of a U.
- **root**: an x value where a graph crosses the across axis.
- **estimate**: a careful "about", read from a graph.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** tables of values as points; straight-line graphs and y = mx + c, with the gradient as "up per one across" and the intercept as the starting value; gradient from two points; telling linear from non-linear; simultaneous equations as two lines crossing, then by elimination and substitution; expanding two brackets with an area grid; quadratic graphs (the U shape, its symmetry line and roots); estimating values and solutions from graphs.

**What it does not claim:** the plan costs, press counts and prices are made up. The graph of costs shows when each plan is cheaper; it does not choose a plan. It does not teach factorising quadratics, the quadratic formula, or inequalities on graphs.
