# Mixing and Matching: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 201](../specs/201-mixing-and-matching.md). Use as written, after independent spec review and a math check. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion and is never gendered; `{courier}` is the explorer's courier. Pip is he/him, Dot she/her, Bramble she/her (with her magpie), the miller she/her (with her cat), the baker he/him, the orchard keeper she/her, the librarian they/them, Nuria she/her, Wren Halloway she/her, Hob Pennick he/him. Real-world facts are in the [source audit](../source-audits/201-mixing-and-matching.md); items still marked `[check]` stay off screen until cleared. Nothing here is scored or timed. Money is US dollars and cents.

## Labels used on every card

- **A story from Willowbank**: the town, its traders, paints, prices and every character are made up.
- **A true thing about math**: checked math, explained simply.
- **A true thing from history**: something people really did or wrote.
- **A made-up model**: a simple game rule, shown on the card, that is not real measurement.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. The faded heron (Act III, chapter 1: the floating market)

**Hook:** The Vadoalto traders' boat has a heron-blue sign. The sun faded it. They need a big pot of the same blue.

**Short:**
> The traders want their heron blue again. The old note says 2 white for every 3 blue. They need a bigger pot.

**Longer:**
> A trader from Vadoalto carries a faded sign up to the shed. The heron on it used to be a deep, bright blue.
>
> "We have the old note," she says. "Two scoops of white for every three of blue. But that makes a tiny jar. We need a whole pot."
>
> Bramble grabs the scoops. "Easy. Add two more to each. Four white, five blue."
>
> She paints a stripe. It is paler than the heron.
>
> "That's a different blue," says Pip. "That's a sky."
>
> "Every 3 blue needs its 2 white," says {guide}. "Double the blue, double the white."
>
> Bramble tries six blue and four white. The stripe matches. She sniffs. "I meant to make a sky. For later."

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> In this game, a shade depends only on its "for every". Two white for every three blue matches four for every six. Four for every five has more white in each scoop, so it is paler. Real paints vary.

### Scene 2. The miller's chalkboard (Act III, chapter 1: the floating market)

**Hook:** Vadoalto's boats bring honey and chestnut flour. The miller trades her wheat flour. She needs rates everyone agrees on.

**Short:**
> The miller chalks: "3 jars of honey for every 5 kg of flour." Pip offers 1 kg for 1 jar.

**Longer:**
> The miller writes on the chalkboard in big letters.
>
> *3 jars of Vadoalto honey for every 5 kg of wheat flour.*
> *4 kg of chestnut flour for every 5 kg of wheat flour.*
>
> Pip holds up a small bag. "One kilo of flour for one jar of honey?"
>
> "That isn't the rate," says the miller. "Five kilos get three jars. One kilo gets less than one jar."
>
> "How much less?"
>
> Nuria counts on her fingers. "Three fifths of a jar. *Tres quintos.*"
>
> Pip looks at the jar. He looks at his bag. "I'll bring four more kilos."
>
> The miller's cat sits on the scale, so nobody can use it for a while.

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> A rate says how much of one thing goes with how much of another. Multiply both sides by the same number and the deal stays fair. The prices and goods are made up.

### Scene 3. Two headlines (Act IV, chapter 3: the newspaper's two explanations)

**Hook:** The librarian drafts two *Gazette* headlines from two old years of flood-post records. Both say "40 cm more".

**Short:**
> Two draft headlines say Vadoalto's water rose 40 cm more. Nuria says that hides something.

**Longer:**
> The librarian pins two draft headlines for the *Gazette* on the board, one for each of two old flood years.
>
> *First year: Willowbank's post, 80 cm. Vadoalto's, 120 cm. "Vadoalto 40 cm higher!"*
>
> *Second year: Willowbank's post, 40 cm. Vadoalto's, 80 cm. "Vadoalto 40 cm higher again!"*
>
> "Same both years," says Pip.
>
> Nuria shakes her head. "In the second year ours was twice yours. In the first year, only one and a half times."
>
> "So which headline is true?" asks Dot.
>
> "Both," says {guide}. "One subtracts. One asks how many times. They answer different questions."
>
> The librarian writes a third headline in pencil, very small.

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> A difference subtracts: 120 − 80 = 40. A ratio asks how many times: 120 is 1.5 times 80. When the amounts are small, the same difference can be a bigger ratio. These flood marks are made up.

### Scene 4. The meadow seed (Act IV, chapter 4: the proposal for the low fields)

**Hook:** The "Some of Each" plan has a small wet meadow. Before the meeting, the orchard keeper works out the seed it would need, just in case.

**Short:**
> If the town picks Some of Each, the meadow needs 15 kg of seed: 1 part reed for every 4 parts grass.

**Longer:**
> The orchard keeper pours seed onto a cloth. "One part reed seed for every four parts meadow grass," she says. "Nuria's grandmother's mix."
>
> "How much reed is in the whole sack?" asks Dot.
>
> "Fifteen kilos of mix," says the orchard keeper. "So, three of reed?"
>
> Pip draws five blocks in the dirt: one for reed, four for grass. "Fifteen in five blocks. Three in each block."
>
> "Three kilos of reed, twelve of grass," says the orchard keeper. She ties the sack and sets it aside. "Only if the town picks this plan. If it does, I'll miss those apple trees."

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> 1 : 4 makes 5 equal blocks. 15 ÷ 5 = 3 kg in each block. Reed: 3 kg. Grass: 4 × 3 = 12 kg. Working out a plan's needs is not choosing it. The plan and seed mix are made up.

## Teach cards

Each card is under 30 words. Worked steps appear one tap at a time.

**T1. For every** (A true thing about math)
> "2 white for every 3 blue" means groups. Each group has 2 white and 3 blue. More groups make more paint.
Worked example: 1. One group: 2 white, 3 blue. 2. Two groups: 4 white, 6 blue. 3. Same shade, twice the paint.

**T2. Multiply both, keep the shade** (A true thing about math)
> To make more of the same shade, multiply both amounts by the same number. Adding the same number to both changes the shade.
Worked example: 1. 2 white, 3 blue. 2. Add 2 to each: 4 white, 5 blue. 3. More white in each scoop: paler.

**T3. Ratio or difference?** (A true thing about math)
> A difference subtracts. A ratio asks "how many times?" Two mixes can have the same difference and different shades.
Worked example: 1. Mix A: 1 red, 2 yellow. Mix B: 3 red, 4 yellow. 2. Both: one more yellow. 3. A has 2 yellow per red; B has fewer.

**T4. Writing a ratio** (A true thing about math; recap of the Keeper's Desk's first case)
> Write 2 white for every 3 blue as 2 : 3. Keep the order. Simplify by dividing both numbers by the same number.
Worked example: 1. 6 white : 9 blue. 2. Both divide by 3. 3. 2 : 3.

**T5. A ratio is a "times" rule** (A true thing about math)
> In 2 : 3, blue is always 1.5 times white. White is always 2/3 of blue. A table shows the rule in every row.
Worked example: 1. White 2, 4, 6, 8. 2. Blue 3, 6, 9, 12. 3. Each blue ÷ white = 1.5.

**T6. Bars with equal blocks** (A true thing about math)
> Draw 2 : 3 as two blocks beside three blocks. Every block is the same size. Find one block, then read the rest.
Worked example: 1. White: 2 blocks. Blue: 3 blocks. 2. One block is 4 liters. 3. Blue: 3 × 4 = 12 liters.

**T7. Share a total in a ratio** (A true thing about math)
> Add the parts to count all the blocks. Divide the total by that. Then multiply for each share.
Worked example: 1. 20 liters, 2 : 3. 2. 2 + 3 = 5 blocks. 3. 20 ÷ 5 = 4. 4. White 8, blue 12.

**T8. Part to part, part to whole** (A true thing about math)
> 2 : 3 compares white with blue. White is 2 of all 5 blocks, so 2/5 of the paint.
Worked example: 1. 8 liters white, 12 liters blue. 2. Whole: 20 liters. 3. White: 8/20 = 2/5.

**T9. Three parts** (A true thing about math)
> A ratio can have three parts. Add all three to count the blocks.
Worked example: 1. 60 mL in 1 : 2 : 3. 2. 1 + 2 + 3 = 6 blocks. 3. 60 ÷ 6 = 10. 4. 10, 20 and 30 mL.

**T10. How much for one?** (A true thing about math)
> A unit rate is the amount for exactly one. Find it on a double number line, then scale to any amount.
Worked example: 1. 3 kg costs $6. 2. 1 kg costs $6 ÷ 3 = $2. 3. 5 kg costs 5 × $2 = $10.

**T11. A straight line through zero** (A true thing about math)
> Proportional amounts make a straight line through zero. No flour, no cost. A line that starts higher is not proportional.
Worked example: 1. 0 kg: $0. 1 kg: $2. 3 kg: $6. 2. The dots line up. 3. The line starts at zero.

**T12. More helpers, less time** (A true thing about math)
> Sometimes one amount goes up as the other goes down. Double the painters, half the hours. That is inverse proportion.
Worked example: 1. 2 painters: 6 hours. 2. 4 painters: 3 hours. 3. Both times, 2 × 6 = 4 × 3 = 12.

**T13. Words for proportion** (A true thing about math)
> Scale factor: what you multiply by. Unit rate: the amount for one. Constant of proportionality: the number one amount is always multiplied by.
Worked example: 1. Heron blue ×4: 8 white, 12 blue. 2. Scale factor: 4. 3. Blue = 1.5 × white. 4. Constant: 1.5.

**T14. Per: two units together** (A true thing about math)
> A compound unit joins two units with "per". Dollars per kilogram. Liters per minute. Kilometers per hour.
Worked example: 1. A vat fills 4 liters per minute. 2. 48 liters ÷ 4 = 12 minutes. 3. The Courier Yard's boat: 12 km in 1.5 hours is 8 km/h.

**T15. Compare the same amount** (A true thing about math)
> To find the better buy, compare the price for the same amount. The smaller total isn't always cheaper.
Worked example: 1. 2 kg for $5: $2.50 per kg. 2. 5 kg for $11: $2.20 per kg. 3. The big sack costs less per kg.

**T16. Changing units is proportion** (A true thing about math)
> 1 km = 1000 m, every time. A smaller unit needs a bigger number. Use a double number line.
Worked example: 1. 1 km: 1000 m. 2. 3 km: 3000 m. 3. 3.2 km: 3200 m.

**T17. Step by step** (A true thing about math)
> Change units in steps, with the number for each step. Hours to minutes, then minutes to seconds.
Worked example: 1. 2.5 hours × 60 = 150 minutes. 2. 150 minutes × 60 = 9000 seconds.

**T18. Square units** (A true thing about math; Keeper's Desk)
> A square meter is 100 cm along and 100 cm up. That is 100 × 100 = 10,000 small squares, not 100.
Worked example: 1. One row: 100 squares. 2. 100 rows. 3. 100 × 100 = 10,000 cm².

## Real-world and history cards

**H1. Grain swaps in old China** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> In China, about 2,000 years ago, a math book called *The Nine Chapters* listed rates for swapping grains. One rate: 50 of millet for 30 of hulled millet.
Keeper's note:
> The chapter is called *Millet and Rice*. It has many problems about swapping grains, beans and seeds. Its method: multiply by the rate you want, then divide by the rate you have. For 10 measures of millet: 10 × 30 ÷ 50 = 6 measures of hulled millet.

**H2. A meter from the whole Earth** (A true thing from history; Seedling and up)
> In 1791, French scientists proposed a new length, the meter: one ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the equator.
Keeper's note:
> In 1795 France set the kilogram as the mass of one liter of water. Today both units are defined in other ways. Their tens, hundreds and thousands make metric units easy to change: 1 km = 1000 m, 1 kg = 1000 g, 1 L = 1000 mL.

**H3. The inch, in centimeters** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> Since 1959, one inch has been exactly 2.54 centimeters. Six countries agreed, including the United States.
Keeper's note:
> The agreement fixed the yard at exactly 0.9144 m. A yard is 36 inches, so one inch is 0.9144 ÷ 36 = 0.0254 m, which is 2.54 cm.

## Bench (a): The Shade Table

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> In this game a shade depends only on its "for every". Real paints vary.

**Opening:** Make a pot that matches the sample.
**Buttons:** Add a white scoop · Add a blue scoop · Take a scoop back · Paint a stripe · Group the scoops · Empty the pot
**After a stripe:** Put your stripe beside the sample. Same shade?
**Sprout mode:** scoops shown as pictures in groups; no numbers on screen.
**Table mode (Seedling and up):** Rows: White · Blue. A rule strip under the table: "Blue ÷ white = ?"

**Guided item:** Heron blue is 2 white for every 3 blue. You have 6 blue.
- Step 1: How many groups of 3 blue? *(2 groups.)*
- Step 2: Each group needs 2 white. *(2 × 2.)*
- Step 3: 4 white for 6 blue. Paint a stripe and compare.

## Bench (b): The Bar Bench

**Model card:** Every block is the same size. Bars are a drawing of the amounts, not a picture of the pots.
**Buttons:** Add a block · Take a block · Label the total · Fill one block · Fill the rest · Check the sum
**Bar labels:** White · Blue · Total · ? (each bar can be named)

**Guided item:** Mix 20 liters of heron blue, 2 white : 3 blue.
- Step 1: How many blocks in all? *(2 + 3 = 5.)*
- Step 2: What is one block? *(20 ÷ 5 = 4 liters.)*
- Step 3: White: 2 × 4. Blue: 3 × 4. *(8 and 12.)*
- Step 4: Check: 8 + 12 = 20.

## Bench (c): The Rate Line

**Model card** (A made-up model): Made-up prices for practice.
**Views:** Double number line · Table · Graph (the three move together)
**Buttons:** Add a pair · Find one · Draw the line · Compare a line with a start fee
**Word drawer (Sapling and up):** ratio · rate · unit rate · scale factor · direct proportion · inverse proportion · constant of proportionality · compound unit. Each card shows a meaning, an example and a non-example.

| Word | Example | Non-example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ratio | 2 white : 3 blue | "3 more blue than white" (a difference) |
| rate | 3 jars for every 5 kg | 2 : 3 with no units at all |
| unit rate | $2 for each kg | $6 for 3 kg (not for one) |
| scale factor | × 4 makes a batch four times bigger | + 4 to each amount |
| direct proportion | twice the flour, twice the cost | a $2 pot fee plus $4 per liter |
| inverse proportion | twice the painters, half the hours | twice the painters, twice the paint |
| constant of proportionality | blue = 1.5 × white: the 1.5 | the 3 in "3 more" |
| compound unit | dollars per kilogram | kilograms on their own |

**Guided item:** 3 kg of chestnut flour costs $6. How much for 5 kg?
- Step 1: Find one: $6 ÷ 3 = $2 for 1 kg.
- Step 2: Scale: 5 × $2.
- Step 3: $10. Find the dot on the line.

## Problem sets

Fifteen three-choice items put the right answer at A five times, B five times and C five times.

### Set A. For every, and the same shade (Sprout and Seedling)

**A1 (Sprout, 3-choice, pictures)**
Prompt: The sample has one white scoop for every two blue. Which pot matches?
- A. Each white scoop has its two blue, three times over. *(right)*
- B. Three white scoops and three blue scoops. *(tests: equal scoops always match)*
- C. One white scoop and lots of blue. *(tests: more of one color keeps the shade)*
Hint: Look at each white scoop. How many blue go with it?
Explanation: Each white still has two blue, so the shade stays the same.
Feedback A: Yes. Every white scoop still has its two blue.
Feedback B: Now each white has one blue. That has more white, so it's paler.
Feedback C: Lots of blue with one white makes a darker blue.

**A2 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Bramble adds one more white scoop to the blue pot. What happens?
- A. The shade stays just the same. *(tests: more paint never changes the shade)*
- B. The blue gets paler. *(right)*
- C. The blue gets darker. *(tests: every addition darkens)*
Hint: Is there more white for each blue now?
Explanation: More white for each blue makes a paler blue.
Feedback A: More of both would keep it. She added only white.
Feedback B: Yes. More white for each blue: paler.
Feedback C: White makes paint lighter. Look at the stripe.

**A3 (Sprout, 3-choice, pictures)**
Prompt: Pot one: one yellow with one blue. Pot two: two yellow with two blue.
Question line: Same green?
- A. No. Pot two is bigger, so its green is different. *(tests: a bigger batch changes the shade)*
- B. No. Pot two has more yellow, so it's yellower. *(tests: looking at one color only)*
- C. Yes. Each yellow scoop still has one blue. *(right)*
Hint: Pair each yellow with a blue. Any left over?
Explanation: Every yellow has one blue in both pots, so the greens match.
Feedback A: Bigger is more paint. The shade depends on the pairs.
Feedback B: It has more blue too. Each yellow still has one blue.
Feedback C: Yes. Same pairs, same green.

**A4 (Seedling, number)**
Prompt: Heron blue is 2 white for every 3 blue. You have 9 blue. White?
Answer: 6 scoops of white.
Worked steps: 1. 9 blue is 3 groups of 3. 2. Each group needs 2 white. 3. 3 × 2 = 6.
Tests: 8 (adding 6 to both: 3 + 6 = 9, so 2 + 6 = 8); 13.5 (using the rate the wrong way: 9 × 3 ÷ 2).
Hint: How many groups of 3 blue are in 9?
Explanation: Three groups of blue need three groups of white: 6 white.

**A5 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Mix A: 1 red, 2 yellow. Mix B: 3 red, 4 yellow. Same orange?
- A. Yes. Both have one more yellow than red. *(tests: comparing by difference)*
- B. No. A has 2 yellow for each red; B has fewer. *(right)*
- C. We can't tell until both are mixed. *(tests: withholding: the amounts already tell)*
Hint: How many yellow go with each red?
Explanation: A has 2 yellow per red. B has 4 for 3 red, fewer than 2 each.
Feedback A: Same difference, different ratio. Count yellow for each red.
Feedback B: Yes. A is yellower, because each red has more yellow.
Feedback C: The amounts are enough. How many yellow for each red?

**A6 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Write 6 white : 9 blue in its simplest form.
- A. 2 : 3 *(right)*
- B. 3 : 6 *(tests: taking 3 from both)*
- C. 3 : 2 *(tests: swapping the order)*
Hint: What number divides both 6 and 9?
Explanation: Divide both by 3: 2 : 3. White first, as it was written.
Feedback A: Yes. Both divided by 3.
Feedback B: Taking away changes the ratio. Divide both by the same number.
Feedback C: Keep the order: white first, then blue.

### Set B. Bars and shares (Seedling and Sapling)

**B1 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: White has 2 blocks, blue has 3. Each block is 4 liters. Blue?
- A. 3 liters *(tests: counting blocks, not their size)*
- B. 12 liters *(right)*
- C. 20 liters *(tests: giving the whole, not the part)*
Hint: Three blocks of 4 liters each.
Explanation: 3 × 4 = 12 liters of blue.
Feedback A: That counts the blocks. Each block holds 4 liters.
Feedback B: Yes. Three blocks, 4 liters each.
Feedback C: That's all five blocks. Blue has three.

**B2 (Seedling, number)**
Prompt: Mix 20 liters of heron blue, 2 white : 3 blue. How much white?
Answer: 8 liters (and 12 liters of blue).
Worked steps: 1. 2 + 3 = 5 blocks. 2. 20 ÷ 5 = 4 liters in each block. 3. White: 2 × 4 = 8. 4. Check: 8 + 12 = 20.
Tests: 10 (sharing equally); 4 (stopping at one block).
Hint: Count all the blocks first.
Explanation: Five blocks share 20 liters, 4 each. White has two blocks.

**B3 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Heron blue is 2 white : 3 blue. What fraction of the paint is white?
- A. 2/3 *(tests: reading part to part as part of the whole)*
- B. 3/5 *(tests: choosing the other part)*
- C. 2/5 *(right)*
Hint: How many blocks make the whole pot?
Explanation: White is 2 blocks out of all 5, so 2/5.
Feedback A: 2 : 3 compares white with blue. The whole pot has 5 blocks.
Feedback B: That's the blue part. White has 2 blocks.
Feedback C: Yes. 2 out of 5 blocks.

**B4 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: Ribbon purple: 1 red : 2 blue : 3 white. Make 60 mL. Each amount?
Answer: 10 mL red, 20 mL blue, 30 mL white.
Worked steps: 1. 1 + 2 + 3 = 6 blocks. 2. 60 ÷ 6 = 10 mL each. 3. 1 × 10, 2 × 10, 3 × 10.
Tests: 20, 20, 20 (sharing equally between colors); 20, 40, 60 (dividing by the number of colors, not blocks).
Hint: Add all three parts.
Explanation: Six blocks, 10 mL each. Check: 10 + 20 + 30 = 60.

**B5 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: A green is 3 yellow : 2 blue. It used 12 liters of yellow. Blue?
Answer: 8 liters of blue; 20 liters in all.
Worked steps: 1. Yellow is 3 blocks: 12 ÷ 3 = 4 liters each. 2. Blue: 2 × 4 = 8. 3. All: 12 + 8 = 20.
Tests: 6 (taking half of the yellow); 14 in all (adding the 2 to 12).
Hint: 12 liters fills the yellow blocks. What is one block?
Explanation: One block is 4 liters, and blue has two blocks.

**B6 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: The pot had 10 liters white and 14 liters blue. Simplest ratio, white : blue?
Answer: 5 : 7.
Worked steps: 1. 10 : 14. 2. Both divide by 2. 3. 5 : 7.
Tests: 10 : 14 (not simplified); 7 : 5 (order swapped).
Hint: What divides both 10 and 14?
Explanation: Dividing both by 2 keeps the shade and gives 5 : 7.

### Set C. Tables, lines and words (Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**C1 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Tables: P: white 1, 2, 3 and blue 3, 6, 9. Q: white 1, 2, 3 and blue 5, 6, 7.
Prompt: Which table keeps the same shade in every row?
- A. Table P: blue is always 3 times white. *(right)*
- B. Table Q: blue is always 4 more than white. *(tests: a steady difference counts as a ratio)*
- C. Both, because both go up steadily. *(tests: any pattern is a ratio)*
Hint: Divide blue by white in each row.
Explanation: P's rows all give 3. Q's give 5, 3 and about 2.3.
Feedback A: Yes. The same "times" rule in every row.
Feedback B: The difference stays 4, but the ratio changes. Divide each row.
Feedback C: Going up steadily isn't enough. Is the "times" number the same?

**C2 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: 3 kg of chestnut flour costs $6. How much for 5 kg?
Answer: $10.
Worked steps: 1. 1 kg: $6 ÷ 3 = $2. 2. 5 kg: 5 × $2 = $10.
Tests: $8 (2 more kg, so $2 more); $11 (adding 5 to $6).
Hint: How much is 1 kg?
Explanation: The unit rate is $2 per kg, so 5 kg cost $10.

**C3 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which graph shows flour cost proportional to kilograms?
- A. A straight line that starts at $3 for 0 kg. *(tests: any straight line is proportional)*
- B. A curve that goes down. *(tests: mixing up direct and inverse)*
- C. A straight line through 0 kg and $0. *(right)*
Hint: What should no flour cost?
Explanation: Proportional means no flour, no cost, and a straight line.
Feedback A: Straight, but 0 kg costs $3. It misses zero.
Feedback B: Going down is inverse. Cost goes up with flour.
Feedback C: Yes. Straight, and through zero.

**C4 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Two painters paint the fence in 6 hours. Four painters, same speed?
- A. 12 hours *(tests: treating it as direct proportion)*
- B. 3 hours *(right)*
- C. 8 hours *(tests: adding 2 for 2 more painters)*
Hint: Twice the painters. More time, or less?
Explanation: Twice the painters share the work, so half the time: 3 hours.
Feedback A: More painters finish sooner. Double the painters, half the time.
Feedback B: Yes. 2 × 6 = 4 × 3. Inverse proportion.
Feedback C: Adding doesn't fit. Double the painters halves the time.

**C5 (Keeper's Desk, match)**
Prompt: Match each word to its example.
- unit rate → $2 for each kilogram
- scale factor → × 4 to make four times the paint
- constant of proportionality → the 1.5 in blue = 1.5 × white
- inverse proportion → more painters, fewer hours
- compound unit → liters per minute
Tests: unit rate confused with any rate; scale factor read as adding; inverse labeled direct.
Hint: Which one is "for one"? Which one says "per"?
Explanation: Each word names a different job in the same kind of problem.

**C6 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Paint: $4 per liter, plus $2 for the pot. Is cost proportional?
- A. No. 0 liters still costs $2, so it misses zero. *(right)*
- B. Yes. Its graph is a straight line. *(tests: straight means proportional)*
- C. No, because the cost goes up. *(tests: proportional means staying the same)*
Hint: Does 2 liters cost twice as much as 1 liter?
Explanation: 1 liter costs $6; 2 liters cost $10, not $12. Doubling doesn't double.
Feedback A: Yes. The pot fee stops it starting at zero.
Feedback B: Straight is not enough. Where does it start?
Feedback C: Proportional costs go up too. Check where it starts.

### Set D. Rates and the exchange board (Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**D1 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: 3 jars of honey for every 5 kg of flour. The miller brings 15 kg.
Question line: How many jars?
Answer: 9 jars.
Worked steps: 1. 15 kg is 3 groups of 5 kg. 2. 3 groups × 3 jars = 9 jars.
Tests: 13 (adding 10 to both); 25 (using the rate the wrong way: 15 × 5 ÷ 3).
Hint: How many groups of 5 kg in 15?
Explanation: Three groups of flour get three groups of jars.

**D2 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Chestnut flour: 2 kg for $5, or 5 kg for $11. Cheaper per kg?
- A. The 2 kg sack, because $5 is less. *(tests: comparing totals)*
- B. Can't tell; the sacks are different sizes. *(tests: withholding: a per-kg price settles it)*
- C. The 5 kg sack: $2.20 per kg, against $2.50. *(right)*
Hint: Find the price of 1 kg in each sack.
Explanation: $5 ÷ 2 = $2.50. $11 ÷ 5 = $2.20. Compare those.
Feedback A: $5 buys only 2 kg. Find the price per kg.
Feedback B: Different sizes are fine. Compare 1 kg from each.
Feedback C: Yes. Same amount, compared fairly.

**D3 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: The dye vat holds 48 liters. The pipe fills 4 liters per minute. How long?
Answer: 12 minutes.
Worked steps: 1. 4 liters each minute. 2. 48 ÷ 4 = 12. 3. 12 minutes.
Tests: 52 (adding); 192 (multiplying).
Hint: How many lots of 4 liters make 48?
Explanation: Liters per minute tells how much each minute. Divide to find the minutes.

**D4 (Keeper's Desk, number; history card H1)**
Prompt: Old rates: 50 of millet for 30 of hulled millet. 10 of millet gives?
Answer: 6 measures of hulled millet.
Worked steps: 1. Multiply by the rate you want: 10 × 30 = 300. 2. Divide by the rate you have: 300 ÷ 50 = 6.
Tests: about 16.7 (the rates swapped: 10 × 50 ÷ 30); 30 (copying the rate).
Hint: Hulled millet is less than the millet you start with.
Explanation: 50 becomes 30, so 10 becomes 6. Both are 3/5 as much.

**D5 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: How many jars of honey can 8 kg of chestnut flour buy?
Board: 4 kg chestnut for every 5 kg wheat; 3 jars for every 5 kg wheat.
Answer: 6 jars.
Worked steps: 1. 8 kg chestnut is 2 groups of 4 kg: 10 kg of wheat flour. 2. 10 kg wheat is 2 groups of 5 kg: 2 × 3 = 6 jars.
Tests: 4.8 (treating chestnut as wheat: 8 ÷ 5 × 3).
Hint: First change chestnut flour into wheat flour.
Explanation: Two rates in a chain: chestnut to wheat, then wheat to honey.

**D6 (Keeper's Desk, say why; spoken to a grown-up, not stored)**

Explanation: A rate carries two units with "per" between them.

- B. 200 g *(tests: using 100 in place of 1000)*

## "I'm stuck" worked examples

Step 2 of the ladder: an easier case, then how to start this one.

- **Shade items:** "One white for every one blue. Two groups: two white, two blue. Start: how many groups do you need?"
- **Ratio or difference items:** "2 and 4: the difference is 2, and 4 is 2 times 2. Start: subtract, or ask how many times?"
- **Simplest form items:** "4 : 6. Both divide by 2: 2 : 3. Start: what divides both of your numbers?"
- **Bar items:** "Share 12 shells 1 : 2. That's 3 blocks, 4 in each: 4 and 8. Start: how many blocks in all?"
- **Fraction-of-whole items:** "1 red : 1 blue. Red is 1 of 2 blocks: half. Start: count all the blocks."
- **Unit rate items:** "2 apples cost $4, so 1 apple costs $2. Start: what does one cost?"
- **Graph items:** "No apples cost $0. Start: does your line begin at zero?"
- **Inverse items:** "One friend digs a bed in 4 hours. Two friends: 2 hours. Start: more helpers, more time or less?"
- **Exchange items:** "1 jar for every 2 kg. 4 kg is 2 groups: 2 jars. Start: how many groups?"
- **Best-buy items:** "1 kg for $3 or 2 kg for $4: $3 and $2 per kg. Start: find the price of 1 kg."
- **Unit items:** "1 m = 100 cm, so 2 m = 200 cm. Start: is the new unit smaller or bigger?"
- **Chain items:** "1 hour = 60 minutes = 3600 seconds. Start: which step comes first?"

## Key terms (glossary)

- **ratio**: how much of one thing for every amount of another, written 2 : 3.
- **for every**: a group that repeats, the same each time.
- **simplest form**: a ratio with no number that divides both parts.
- **difference**: how much more, found by subtracting.
- **bar model**: a drawing with equal blocks for the parts.
- **part to part**: comparing one part with another.
- **part to whole**: comparing one part with all of it.
- **rate**: a ratio with two different units, such as 3 jars for every 5 kg.
- **unit rate**: the amount for exactly one.
- **scale factor**: the number you multiply by to scale up or down.
- **direct proportion**: both amounts are multiplied by the same number.
- **inverse proportion**: one amount goes up as the other goes down, and their product stays the same.
- **constant of proportionality**: the number one amount is always multiplied by to get the other.
- **compound unit**: two units joined by "per", such as dollars per kilogram.
- **double number line**: two number lines side by side, lined up at zero.
- **convert**: say the same amount in a different unit.
