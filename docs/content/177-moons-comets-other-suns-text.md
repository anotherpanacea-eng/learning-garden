# Moons, Comets and Other Suns' Planets: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 177](../specs/177-moons-comets-other-suns.md). Use as written, after independent review and a subject check. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion and is never gendered. Pip is a fox (he/him), Dot a ladybug (she/her), Bramble a girl witch (she/her) with a magpie, Ember a pocket-dragon, Hob Pennick he/him, Wren Halloway she/her, Léon a kingfisher (he/him), Nuria an otter (she/her). The librarian is they/them. The old Keeper is never named here. Nothing is scored.

**Labels used on every card:** *A story from Willowbank* (fiction), *A true thing about space* or *A true thing from history* (checked fact), *A story people told* (omens and myths), *A made-up model* (bench results).

## The story

### Scene 1. The late moon *(A story from Willowbank; Harvest Supper)*

**Short:**
> At Harvest Supper, Pip waits for the moon to rise over the hill. It came up at seven last night. Tonight it is late.

**Longer:**
> The Harvest Supper tables are out on the green. Pip has saved a seat facing the hill.
>
> "Last night the moon came up at seven," he says. "Right there. Like a big round pie."
>
> Seven comes. No moon. Pip eats a roll to pass the time. Then another.
>
> "Is it lost?" he asks.
>
> Hob Pennick checks his pocket watch. "Not lost. Late. It's late every night, a bit."
>
> "The Moon is moving too," says {guide}. "Along its path around Earth. So Earth has to turn a little extra to catch it up."
>
> At twenty-five past seven, a gold edge shows over the hill.
>
> "There," says Hob. "Less late than usual. Harvest moons are like that."
>
> Pip looks at his empty plate. "Tomorrow I'm bringing more rolls."

**Keeper's note:**
> On average the Moon rises about 50 minutes later each day. Near the autumn full moon, in northern lands, the delay shrinks to half an hour or less for a few nights. That is the harvest moon.

### Scene 2. The comet that came back *(A story from Willowbank, with a true thing from history)*

**Short:**
> Bramble says comets come by chance, as signs. The librarian opens an old record. One man used old records to say when a comet would return.

**Longer:**
> The *Gazette* wants a sky page. Bramble has written the headline already: COMETS: WHAT DO THEY MEAN?
>
> "People used to think comets were signs," she says. "They come whenever they like."
>
> Her magpie bobs in agreement.
>
> The librarian brings an Archive card to the table. "Edmond Halley read old reports of comets. Three of them, years apart, followed the same path."
>
> "So it was one comet," says {guide}. "Coming back."
>
> "About every 76 years. He said it would return around 1758. He didn't live to see it. But someone did, on Christmas night."
>
> Wren Halloway taps her ledger. "Old records, kept carefully, told him the future. That's what records are for."
>
> Bramble crosses out her headline and writes a new one: COMETS: WHEN DO THEY COME BACK?

**Keeper's note:**
> Comets as signs is a story people told for thousands of years. Halley's prediction showed a comet follows a path around the Sun, like a planet, only far more oval.

### Scene 3. Ember's dip *(A story from Willowbank, with a true thing from history; Act III letters, Lantern Night)*

**Short:**
> Léon writes that astronomers in France found a planet around another star. Nobody saw the planet. Ember shows how, by flying past a lantern.

**Longer:**
> Dot brings a letter from Les Saules. Léon writes: *Our teacher says astronomers at an observatory in the south of France found the first planet around a star like the Sun. In 1995! But they never saw the planet.*
>
> "Then how did they know?" asks Pip.
>
> On Lantern Night, {guide} takes everyone across the brook. One lantern glows by the bakery door.
>
> "Ember, fly in front of it," says {guide}. "Small circles."
>
> From across the water, the lantern dims a tiny bit each time Ember passes. Dim, bright. Dim, bright.
>
> "Ember's too small to see from here," says Dot. "But the lantern shows Ember's there."
>
> "That's one way," says the librarian. "Watch the star, not the planet."
>
> Bramble squints. "That dip is tiny."
>
> "Real ones are tinier," says the librarian.

**Keeper's note:**
> Léon's teacher is nearly right. That 1995 planet was found by a wobble in its star's light, not a dip. The dip method came in 1999. Both watch the star.

## Teach cards

### Moon Path

**Teach 1 (A true thing about space):** The Moon makes no light. Sunlight bounces off it. That is moonlight.

**Teach 2:** The Moon goes around Earth about once a month. One trip, one full set of shapes.
*Worked example, tap by tap:* 1. New moon: the lit side faces away. 2. A week on: half lit. 3. Two weeks: full. 4. About a month: new again.

**Teach 3:** The Moon spins once on each trip around. So the same side always faces us.
*Worked example, tap by tap:* 1. Find the flag on the Moon. 2. Step a week. 3. The flag still points at Earth. 4. Step again. Still there.

**Teach 4 (Seedling):** The far side gets sunlight too. We just never see it from Earth.

**Teach 5 (Sapling):** The Moon moves along its path while Earth spins. So it rises about 50 minutes later each day.

### Planet Gallery

**Teach 6:** Each planet has something special. Some have a reason you can find.

**Teach 7:** Mars is red because its dust has rust in it. Mars is cold, not hot.

**Teach 8:** Venus is farther from the Sun than Mercury, but hotter. Thick air traps its heat like a blanket.
*Worked example, tap by tap:* 1. Mercury has almost no air. 2. Its heat escapes at night. 3. Venus has thick air. 4. Its heat stays in.

**Teach 9:** All four giant planets have rings. Saturn's are just the brightest.

**Teach 10:** The Great Red Spot is a storm about as wide as Earth. Jupiter has no solid ground under it.

**Moon cards (A true thing about space):**
- **Phobos and Deimos:** Mars's two small moons, lumpy like potatoes.
- **Io:** a moon of Jupiter with hundreds of volcanoes.
- **Europa:** a moon of Jupiter. Scientists think an ocean lies under its ice.
- **Titan:** a moon of Saturn with thick air and lakes, but not of water.

### Comet Road

**Teach 11:** Asteroids are rocky. Comets are mostly ice and dust.

**Teach 12:** Near the Sun, a comet's ice turns to gas. It grows a glowing tail. It is not on fire.

**Teach 13:** One space rock gets three names, by where it is.
*Worked example, tap by tap:* 1. In space: a meteoroid. 2. Glowing through the air: a meteor. 3. Landed on the ground: a meteorite.

**Teach 14 (Sapling):** Comets travel on long oval paths. They go fastest when closest to the Sun.

### Falling Around

**Teach 15:** A space station is falling. It moves sideways so fast that it keeps missing Earth.

**Teach 16 (Sapling):** Once it's fast enough, its engines can stay off. It just keeps falling around.

**Teach 17 (Sapling):** Going up is the easy part. Going sideways fast is the hard part.
*Worked example, tap by tap:* 1. The station is about 400 km up. 2. It moves about 7.7 km every second. 3. That speed is what keeps it up.

**Teach 18 (Keeper's Desk):** A spacecraft can swing past a moving planet and borrow some speed. The planet slows by far too little to measure.

### Starlight Dip

**Teach 19:** Planets around other stars are too faint to see. So astronomers watch the star instead.

**Teach 20:** When a planet passes in front, the star dims a tiny bit. Then it brightens again.
*Worked example, tap by tap:* 1. Flat line: star alone. 2. A dip: planet crossing. 3. Flat again. 4. Same dip later: it went around.

**Teach 21 (Sapling):** A bigger planet makes a deeper dip. A longer gap between dips means a longer year.

**Teach 22 (Sapling):** A planet's pull makes its star wobble. The star's light shifts a little, back and forth.

**Teach 23 (Keeper's Desk):** The habitable zone is where liquid water could exist. It's a place to look, not a sign of life.

## History cards *(A true thing from history)*

- **Ceres, 1801.** On 1 January 1801, Giuseppe Piazzi, at Palermo in Sicily, found a moving dot. He first thought it was a comet. It was Ceres, the biggest thing in the asteroid belt.
- **Halley's comet, 1705 and 1758.** Edmond Halley studied old comet reports and said three were one comet. He predicted its return. He died in 1742. On Christmas night 1758, Johann Palitzsch, a farmer who watched the sky, saw it come back.
- **Pluto, 1930.** Clyde Tombaugh, at Lowell Observatory in Arizona, compared photos of the sky taken nights apart. One dot had moved: Pluto. A schoolgirl in England, Venetia Burney, suggested the name.
- **Voyager 2, 1977.** Launched in 1977, it swung past Jupiter, Saturn and Uranus, borrowing speed, and reached Neptune in 1989. No other spacecraft has visited Uranus or Neptune.
- **51 Pegasi b, 1995.** Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz, from Geneva, found the first planet around a Sun-like star, using an observatory in southern France. They shared a Nobel Prize in 2019.
- **The first dip, 1999.** Astronomers watched a star dim and brighten as a planet crossed it. Since then, thousands of planets have been found around other stars.

**Omen card (A story people told):** For thousands of years, many people told stories that comets were signs of coming events. Halley's prediction showed comets follow paths around the Sun.

## Key terms

- **Orbit:** the path one thing takes around another.
- **Phase:** how much of the Moon's lit half we can see.
- **Far side:** the side of the Moon that never faces Earth. It gets sunlight too.
- **Asteroid:** a rocky body going around the Sun, most between Mars and Jupiter.
- **Comet:** a ball of ice and dust that grows a tail near the Sun.
- **Meteoroid, meteor, meteorite:** a space rock in space, glowing in the air, and landed.
- **Ellipse:** an oval path.
- **Gravity assist:** borrowing speed by swinging past a moving planet.
- **Exoplanet:** a planet going around a star other than the Sun.
- **Transit:** a planet passing in front of its star.
- **Wobble:** a star's tiny back-and-forth, pulled by its planet.
- **Habitable zone:** the distance from a star where liquid water could exist.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches.** The Moon's monthly path and its one face; planet features with simple reasons; asteroids, comets and the meteor names; orbits as falling; how planets around other stars are found by watching the star; and what the habitable zone does and does not mean. Real history: Piazzi, Halley and Palitzsch, Tombaugh, Voyager 2, Mayor and Queloz.

**What it does not claim.** The benches are made-up models: not to scale, with drawn paths and dips far bigger than real ones. Moonrise "50 minutes later" is an average. No claim is made about life on other planets. Moon counts for the giant planets change, so none are given.

**Sources in brief.** NASA science pages and the NSSDC fact sheets; NASA's exoplanet program; Britannica and observatory histories for Halley, Ceres and Pluto; the Nobel Prize record for 1995's planet. Full list in the source audit.

**Off-screen idea (grown-up-led, optional, nothing recorded).** On a clear evening, note the Moon's place against a tree or roofline at the same time on two nights. It will have moved east and changed shape. Stay on the ground in a safe place, dress warmly, and **never look at the Sun**, even at sunset with binoculars or a telescope.
