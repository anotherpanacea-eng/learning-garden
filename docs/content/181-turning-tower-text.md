# The Turning Tower: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 181](../specs/181-turning-tower.md). Use as written, after independent spec review and a math check. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion and is never gendered; `{courier}` is the explorer's courier. The miller is she/her (with her cat), Pip he/him, Dot she/her, Bramble she/her (with her magpie), Nuria she/her, Hob Pennick he/him, the librarian they/them. Before the end of Act III nobody names the old Keeper. Real-world facts are in the [source audit](../source-audits/181-turning-tower.md); items still marked `[check]` stay off screen until cleared. Nothing here is scored or timed.

## Labels used on every card

- **A story from Willowbank**: the town, the mill, its sails and vane, and every character are made up.
- **A true thing about math**: checked math, explained simply.
- **A true thing from history**: something people really did, made or wrote.
- **A made-up model**: a simple game rule, shown on the card, that is not real measurement.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. The crooked sails (Act I, chapter 4: mend the village)

**Hook:** The storm knocked the mill's four sails crooked and spun the weathervane round.

**Short:**
> The storm bent the mill's sails. One gap is too wide, one too narrow. "Make them all quarter turns," says the miller.

**Longer:**
> The mill wheel is stuck in mud, and the storm has done worse up top. The four sails on the mill's tower hang crooked. One gap between sails is narrow. One is wide. Two look right.
>
> "The sails only turn well if every gap is the same," says the miller. "A quarter turn each."
>
> Pip measures the narrow gap with his paws spread wide. "This sail's longer than that one. So this gap is bigger."
>
> "Longer sails don't make a bigger gap," says {guide}. "Look at the turn at the middle."
>
> The miller hands you a card with one square corner. "The old Keeper used this. Fit it in each gap."
>
> The cat walks along a sail, unbothered, and sits exactly where the card should go.

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> The narrow gap is 70°, the wide gap 110°, and two gaps are 90°. Re-hung, all four gaps are quarter turns: 90° each. The sail lengths never changed. Only the turns did.

### Scene 2. The slanted poles (Act II, chapter 1: the weekly market)

**Hook:** The miller's flour stall goes up at the market. Bramble builds the awning frame.

**Short:**
> Bramble's two awning poles lean the same way. One is longer. "They can't be parallel," she says. "They're slanted."

**Longer:**
> The weekly market is back, and the miller wants a flour stall with an awning. Bramble offers to build the frame, mostly so she can tie ribbons to it.
>
> She sets up two poles. Both lean toward the brook, side by side. One is longer than the other.
>
> "Those aren't parallel," Bramble says. "Parallel lines are the same length. And they stand up straight."
>
> Dot slides a ruler between the poles, top to bottom. The gap stays the same all the way.
>
> "Same gap everywhere," says Dot. "If they went on forever, they'd never meet."
>
> Bramble looks at the poles for a long time. Her magpie lands on the longer one. "Fine," she says. "Parallel. But the ribbons are staying."

### Scene 3. The vane points upstream (Act III, chapter 3: the route by the high ford)

**Hook:** On the morning {courier} leaves for Vadoalto, the mill's weathervane swings round and points up the valley.

**Short:**
> The vane points toward Vadoalto. On the map, the river path and the high-ford path split at an angle.

**Longer:**
> The morning {courier} sets off, the wind blows down the valley from Vadoalto. The mill's weathervane swings round and holds steady, its arrow pointing upstream.
>
> "It's pointing the way you're going," says the miller. "Or the way the wind came. Same thing, today."
>
> {courier} spreads the map on the mill's workbench. At the first fork, the river path goes one way and the high-ford path another. Two straight lines from one point.
>
> "How sharp is that fork?" asks Hob Pennick, who has walked it. "Sharp enough that people miss the turn."
>
> "Guess first," says {guide}. "Then measure."
>
> The miller puts the protractor on the fork. Her cat puts a paw on the map, firmly, as if to hold the valley still.

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> The fork on the map makes a 35° angle: acute, a bit less than half a right angle. A sharp fork is easy to miss when you walk it. The map and the angle are made up.

### Scene 4. A letter about a vane (Act III, chapter 4: letters up and down the river)

**Hook:** Nuria writes from Vadoalto. The flood post there has a little vane on top too.

**Short:**
> Nuria writes: "¡Hola! Our vane's arms meet in an *ángulo recto*: a right angle. Does yours?"

**Longer** (Spanish lines for a fluent reader's check):
> *¡Hola desde Vadoalto!* (Hello from Vadoalto!)
>
> Dear friends at the mill,
>
> Our flood post has a little weathervane on top. Under the arrow is a cross with four arms, one for each way. The arms meet in an *ángulo recto*, a right angle. We check it with the corner of a book.
>
> The storm bent one arm. Now one corner is too wide and the corner next to it is too narrow. My cousin says it is fine because the arms are all the same length. I say length does not matter. Who is right?
>
> Write back. I am drawing a protractor in the mud.
>
> Nuria

## Teach cards

Each card is under 30 words. Worked steps appear one tap at a time.

**T1. An angle is a turn** (A true thing about math)
> Two arms meet at a point. The angle is how far one arm turns to reach the other. More turn, bigger angle.
Worked example: 1. Two sails meet at the hub. 2. Swing one sail open. 3. More turn between them: a bigger angle.

**T2. Longer arms, same angle** (A true thing about math)
> Making the arms longer does not change the angle. Only the turn at the point changes it. Look at the corner, not the ends.
Worked example: 1. Open the hinge a little. 2. Stretch both arms. 3. The turn stays the same, so the angle stays the same.

**T3. A right angle is a quarter turn** (A true thing about math)
> A square corner is a right angle: a quarter turn. Two make a half turn. Four make a full turn. Test with a corner card.
Worked example: 1. Fit the corner card into a sail gap. 2. It fits exactly. 3. That gap is a right angle.

**T4. Acute, obtuse, straight** (A true thing about math)
> Compare with the corner card first. Less open: acute. More open, but not flat: obtuse. A straight line is a half turn.
Worked example: 1. The vane gap is inside the card's corner. 2. Less open than a right angle. 3. Acute.

**T5. Reflex angles** (A true thing about math)
> A reflex angle is more than a half turn, less than a full turn. Find the small angle, then take it from 360.
Worked example: 1. The small angle is 90°. 2. A full turn is 360°. 3. 360 − 90 = 270°. Reflex.

**T6. Points, lines, segments, rays** (A true thing about math)
> A point is a spot. A line goes on forever both ways. A segment stops at both ends. A ray starts at a point and goes on.
Worked example: 1. The sail edge stops at both ends: a segment. 2. Sunlight from the window starts there and keeps going: a ray.

**T7. Parallel** (A true thing about math)
> Parallel lines stay the same distance apart. Make them longer and they still never meet. Length and tilt don't matter.
Worked example: 1. Two sail bars lean the same way. 2. Slide a ruler between them. 3. Same gap everywhere: parallel.

**T8. Perpendicular** (A true thing about math)
> Perpendicular lines meet at a right angle. Turn the picture any way. If the corner card fits, they are perpendicular.
Worked example: 1. A tilted brace meets a beam. 2. The corner card fits. 3. Perpendicular, even tilted.

**T9. Marks tell the truth** (A true thing about math)
> A small square means exactly 90°. Matching ticks mean equal lengths. Matching arcs mean equal angles. Arrows mean parallel. Trust marks over looks.
Worked example: 1. A corner looks wide. 2. It has a small square. 3. It is exactly 90°.

**T10. Naming an angle** (A true thing about math)
> ∠ABC is the angle at B, the middle letter. Its arms go from B to A and from B to C.
Worked example: 1. Read ∠ABC. 2. Find the middle letter: B. 3. Point to B, then trace to A and to C.

**T11. Estimate first** (A true thing about math)
> Before measuring, guess. Use benchmarks: 45° is half a right angle, 90°, 180°, 270°, 360°. Then measure to check.
Worked example: 1. A bit more than a right angle. 2. More than 90°, far from 180°. 3. Guess about 100°.

**T12. Degrees around a full circle** (A true thing about math)
> A full turn is 360 degrees. A quarter turn is 90°, a half turn 180°. A round protractor shows all of it.
Worked example: 1. Quarter turn: 90°. 2. Half turn: 180°. 3. Three quarters: 270°. 4. Full turn: 360°.

**T13. Two scales: start at zero** (A true thing about math)
> Put the center cross on the corner. Put zero on one arm. Read the scale that starts at zero on that arm.
Worked example: 1. The arm crosses 47 and 133. 2. Is it acute? Yes. 3. So it is 47°.

**T14. Draw an angle** (A true thing about math)
> Draw one arm. Put the center on its end and zero along it. Count to your angle. Dot it. Join the dot.
Worked example: 1. Draw 135°: obtuse. 2. Count from zero on your arm to 135. 3. Dot, join, then measure it again.

**T15. Angles add up** (A true thing about math)
> Two angles side by side make one bigger angle. Add the parts for the whole. Take a part from the whole to find the other.
Worked example: 1. Parts: 35° and 40°. 2. Whole: 35 + 40 = 75°. 3. Missing part: 75 − 35 = 40°.

**T16. Scale drawings multiply** (A true thing about math)
> On a scale drawing, each centimeter stands for a real length. Multiply to find the real size. Keep the units.
Worked example: 1. 1 cm stands for 50 cm. 2. The drawing shows 4 cm. 3. 4 × 50 = 200 cm.

**T17. Build a triangle from two sides and an angle** (A true thing about math)
> Draw one side. At its end, draw the angle. Measure the second side along it. Join the ends.
Worked example: 1. Draw 8 cm. 2. At one end, draw 60°. 3. Measure 5 cm along it. 4. Join: the third side is 7 cm.

**T18. Cut a segment in half, square on** (A true thing about math)
> Open the compass more than halfway. Draw arcs from both ends, same width. Join the two crossings. That line cuts it in half at a right angle.
Worked example: 1. PQ is 8 cm. Open to 5 cm. 2. Arcs from P and from Q cross twice. 3. Join the crossings.

**T19. Cut an angle in half** (A true thing about math)
> Draw an arc from the corner across both arms. From each crossing, draw equal arcs. Join the corner to where they meet.
Worked example: 1. The angle is 70°. 2. Make the arcs. 3. Join the line: two angles of 35°.

## Real-world and history cards

**H1. Why 360?** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> Babylonian sky-watchers counted in sixties. They split the Sun's yearly path among the stars into 12 signs, each with 30 parts. That makes 360.
Keeper's note:
> Greek astronomers later used 360 parts for every circle, and we still call them degrees. Nobody knows for sure why 360 was chosen. One idea: a year is nearly 360 days. Another: 360 splits evenly in many ways.

**H2. The astrolabe maker of Aleppo** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> In Aleppo, over 1,000 years ago, a woman called al-Ijliyyah made astrolabes for the city's ruler. An astrolabe measures angles in the sky.
Keeper's note:
> On the back of an astrolabe, a ring is marked in degrees. A pointer with two sights swings until a star lines up through both. Then you read the angle above the horizon from the ring. Never sight the Sun with your eye. Her life is known from one old book. It says she and her father learned from a maker in Baghdad.

**H3. The compass and the square** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> In China, more than 1,800 years ago, carvers showed two founders of the world, Fuxi and Nüwa. One holds a compass. One holds a carpenter's square.
Keeper's note:
> A compass draws circles. A carpenter's square checks right angles. Both are old tools for careful building. On the Millwright's Table you use a compass and a straight edge, and check corners with a square.

- Step 1: More or less than a right angle? *(More.)*
- Step 2: More or less than a half turn? *(Less.)*

## Key terms (glossary)

- **angle**: the amount of turn between two arms that meet at a point.
- **vertex**: the point where the two arms of an angle meet.
- **arm**: one of the two lines that make an angle.
- **right angle**: a quarter turn; a square corner; 90°.
- **acute angle**: less open than a right angle.
- **obtuse angle**: more open than a right angle, less than a straight line.
- **straight angle**: a half turn; 180°.
- **reflex angle**: more than a half turn, less than a full turn.
- **degree (°)**: one of 360 equal parts of a full turn.
- **point**: an exact spot.
- **line**: a straight path going on forever both ways.
- **line segment**: part of a line with two ends.
- **ray**: part of a line with one end, going on forever the other way.
- **parallel**: always the same distance apart; never meeting.
- **perpendicular**: meeting at a right angle.
- **protractor**: a tool for measuring and drawing angles in degrees.
- **estimate**: a careful guess, made before measuring.
- **not to scale**: drawn without true sizes; trust the marks.
- **scale drawing**: a drawing where each length stands for a real length.
- **compass**: a tool for drawing circles and arcs.
- **perpendicular bisector**: a line that cuts a segment in half at a right angle.
- **angle bisector**: a line that cuts an angle into two equal angles.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** an angle as an amount of turn, separate from arm length; right, acute, obtuse, straight and reflex angles; points, lines, segments and rays; parallel and perpendicular lines at any tilt; diagram marks and ∠ABC naming; estimating before measuring; measuring and drawing with a two-scale protractor; adding angles; scale drawings; building a triangle from two sides and the angle between; compass constructions for a perpendicular bisector and an angle bisector.

**What it does not claim:** it does not teach the angle-sum rules (angles on a line, round a point, in triangles); a separate pack does. It does not teach compass directions or turning on a grid. The screen protractor rehearses placement; a real one on paper builds the hand skill. The sails, vane and maps are made up.
