## Story scenes

### Scene 1. Ants with leaves (Act I, chapter 3: investigate and plant)

**Short:** Pip finds ants carrying crumbs across the garden path. Dot says her faraway cousins carry leaves, and never eat them.

**Longer:** Pip lay on his tummy by the bean rows. A line of tiny ants marched past his nose, each one carrying a crumb.

"Thieves," said Pip. "That was my toast."

Dot landed beside him and straightened her hat. "Ants are insects, like me. Six legs. Count them."

Pip counted. He got to six and lost his place.

"In the rainforest," said Dot, "some ants cut pieces of leaf and carry them home. But they don't eat the leaves."

"Then why carry them?" asked Pip.

"They feed the leaves to a garden," said {guide}. "A fungus garden. The fungus is the food."

Pip looked at the bean rows, then at the ants. "So ants are gardeners too," he said. "Like us."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** Leaf-cutter ants live in the warm forests of Central and South America. A big nest can hold millions of ants. Workers cut leaves and carry them underground. The leaves feed a fungus, and the fungus feeds the colony, especially the young ants.

### Scene 2. Where did the spoon come from? (Act II, chapter 1: the weekly market)

**Short:** Dot buys rubber boots at the market. "Where were these before the stall?" she asks. Nobody at the stall is quite sure.

**Longer:** The market was busy again. There was bread, honey, string, and a man who sold only spoons.

Dot bought a pair of rubber boots, very small, with room for a hat.

"Where were these before the stall?" she asked.

"In a box," said the stall keeper.

"And before the box?"

The stall keeper thought. "A ship, I suppose. And before that, a factory."

"And before the factory?" said {guide}. "A tree. A rubber tree, far away, in a warm forest."

The spoon seller held up a wooden spoon. "Mine came from a tree too. Just a nearer one."

Bramble's magpie stole the spoon. Nobody chased it.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** Natural rubber starts as latex, a milky sap from the rubber tree. The tree first grew wild in the Amazon rainforest. Today most natural rubber comes from farms in Southeast Asia.

### Scene 3. The first show needs a forest (Act II, chapter 4: the first show)

**Short:** The librarian painted a rainforest for the show. Bramble wants it bright and quiet. "Is a rainforest like that?" asks {guide}.

**Longer:** The Story Loft smelled of fresh paint. The librarian stepped back from a tall backdrop of trees, vines and leaves.

"It's a rainforest," they said. "For one scene. It needs lights and sounds."

"Bright lights," said Bramble. "Sunshine everywhere. And quiet, so people can hear the puppets."

{guide} looked up at the painted treetops. "In a real rainforest, the treetops block most of the light. The floor is dim, even at midday."

"And quiet?" said Bramble.

"Not quiet at all," said {guide}. "Frogs, birds, monkeys, insects. Rain dripping off the leaves."

Bramble folded her arms. "Fine. Dim and noisy. But the puppets still need sorting."

The magpie had already tipped out the puppet box.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** Under a thick rainforest canopy, only a tiny share of the sunlight reaches the ground. The air is warm and full of water, so skin feels damp and sticky.

### Scene 4. The biggest river (Act III, chapter 1: the floating market)

**Short:** The boats from Vadoalto tie up at the landing. "Is the brook a big river?" asks Pip. The Vadoalto baker laughs. "Let me tell you about a really big one."

**Longer:** Vadoalto's boats came down the brook with reed baskets, round loaves and jars of honey. The Vadoalto baker tied hers up at the landing.

"Is Willow Brook a big river?" asked Pip.

"It's a good river," said the baker. "But big? There's a river in South America so wide in places that it looks like the sea."

"Bigger than the brook?"

"More water than any river on Earth," said the baker. "¡Qué río! What a river! It's called the Amazon, and a great rainforest grows all around it."

Pip looked at the brook. The brook looked back, small and busy.

"Good rivers come in all sizes," said {guide}.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** The Amazon River carries more water than any other river. Which river is the longest is a different question, and experts still argue about it.

**Letter postscript (Act III, chapter 4: letters up and down the river).** Léon writes from Les Saules: "P.S. La France a un morceau de l'Amazonie ! France has a piece of the Amazon! It is called French Guiana."

### Scene 5. The show goes upstream (Act V, path 4: a traveling show)

**Short:** The rainforest scene goes to Vadoalto. You add a new act: a hungry bird looks for a snack. Who hides? Who warns?

**Longer:** The traveling show packed its puppets into baskets for the boat to Vadoalto.

"The rainforest scene needs something new," said the librarian. "Something that teaches as well as entertains."

"A hungry bird," said Bramble. Her magpie looked interested.

"The bird looks for a snack," said {guide}. "A brown stick insect sits on brown bark. A green frog sits on a green leaf. And a tiny frog sits there in bright red and blue."

"The bright one gets eaten first," said Pip.

"Watch the show," said {guide}, "and see."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** Many brightly colored frogs in Central and South American forests have poison in their skin. Their colors work like a warning sign. Birds and other hunters learn to leave them alone.

### Scene 6. A small slice, a big share (Act V, path 2: the Willowbank Gazette, Keeper's Desk)

**Short:** The librarian wants a Gazette page about rainforests. "Tell people why they matter," they say. "With numbers, and honestly."

**Longer:** The Gazette's printing room smelled of ink. The librarian set a blank page on the desk.

"Readers keep asking about the rainforest set," they said. "Can you write a page about real rainforests? Why do people say they matter so much?"

"Because they're big?" asked Pip.

"They're not that big," said {guide}. "Tropical rainforests cover only a small slice of the land. But a huge share of all known kinds of living things live in them."

"So a small patch holds a lot," said Pip.

"That's your headline," said the librarian.

## Teach cards

Each worked example reveals one step per tap.

**T1. Dim on the floor** (A true thing about science). The treetops block most of the light. Even at midday, the forest floor is dim.
Worked example: (1) Sunlight hits the treetops. (2) The leaves catch most of it. (3) Only a little reaches the floor.

**T2. Hot and sticky.** The air is warm all year. It is full of water too. Damp, sticky air is called humid.
Worked example: (1) Rain falls almost every day. (2) Warm air holds lots of water. (3) Skin would feel damp. That's humid.

**T3. Loud and dripping.** Hidden animals call and buzz all day and night. Rain and dew drip off millions of leaves.
Worked example: (1) The rain stops. (2) Water still runs off the leaves. (3) Drip, drip, drip, long after the rain.

**T4. Insects everywhere.** Insects are the most numerous animals in the rainforest. There are far more insects than all the other animals.
Worked example: (1) One tree. (2) A few birds, a monkey, some frogs. (3) Insects on every leaf and branch.

**T5. Ant farmers.** Leaf-cutter ants carry leaf pieces home. They feed the leaves to a fungus garden. The fungus feeds the colony.
Worked example: (1) Cut a leaf piece. (2) Carry it home. (3) Feed it to the fungus. (4) The fungus feeds the ants.

**T6. Six legs or eight?** Insects have six legs. Spiders have eight. Spiders are minibeasts, but they are not insects.
Worked example: (1) A beetle: count the legs. (2) Six. (3) It's an insect.

**T7. Sort by bodies.** Sort animals by body features: fur, feathers, scales, skin. Not by where they live or how they move.
Worked example: (1) A bat flies. (2) But it has fur and feeds its babies milk. (3) So it's a mammal.

**T8. Plants are a group too.** Plants make their own food from sunlight. Trees, ferns and orchids are all plants.
Worked example: (1) An orchid grows on a branch. (2) It has green leaves and makes its own food. (3) It's a plant.

**T9. Follow the key.** A key asks one yes-or-no question at a time. Each answer sends you down one branch.
Worked example: (1) Caiman: makes its own food? No. (2) Backbone? Yes. (3) Feathers? No. (4) Fur and milk? No. (5) Dry scales? Yes. A reptile.

**T10. Hide or warn.** Camouflage helps an animal hide on the right background. Warning colors do the opposite: they say "I taste bad."
Worked example: (1) Green frog on a green leaf: hard to see. (2) Red and blue frog: easy to see. (3) Birds leave it alone.

**T11. Grip and reach.** A gripping tail holds branches like a hand. A toucan's long beak reaches fruit on thin twigs.
Worked example: (1) The monkey's tail wraps the branch. (2) Both hands are free. (3) It reaches fruit without falling.

**T12. Awake at night.** Many rainforest animals are nocturnal: awake at night. Night can hide them, help them find food, or keep them cool.
Worked example: (1) Day: the bat sleeps. (2) Night: the bat flies out. (3) Fewer hunters see it.

**T13. Passed down, not chosen.** Animals don't choose their features. Features are passed down from parents, over many generations.
Worked example: (1) A frog is born bright. (2) Its parents were bright too. (3) It didn't decide to be bright.

**T14. Find the Amazon first.** The Amazon rainforest is in South America. It spreads across nine countries and territories. Most of it is in Brazil.
Worked example: (1) Find South America. (2) Find the big green patch. (3) Count the countries it touches: nine.

**T15. Biggest by water.** The Amazon River carries more water than any other river. "Biggest" here means the most water, not the longest.
Worked example: (1) Two rivers. (2) Which carries more water each second? (3) That one is bigger, by water.

**T16. One in ten kinds.** Scientists estimate that about 1 in 10 known kinds of living things lives in the Amazon. These are estimates.
Worked example: (1) Imagine all known kinds as 10 cards. (2) The Amazon holds about 1 card. (3) That's 1 in 10.

**T17. Many kinds, not just many animals** (Keeper's Desk). Biodiversity means how many different kinds live in a place. A thousand ants of one kind is not much biodiversity.
Worked example: (1) Jar A: 20 ants, one kind. (2) Jar B: 20 animals, 12 kinds. (3) Jar B has more biodiversity.

**T18. A small slice, a big share** (Keeper's Desk). Tropical rainforests cover about 6 in 100 parts of Earth's land. Over half of known kinds of plants and animals live there. Both numbers are estimates.
Worked example: (1) 100 squares of land. (2) About 6 are tropical rainforest. (3) About half of known kinds live in those 6.

**T19. From forest to shelf** (Keeper's Desk). Many things in a market began in a rainforest. Each passes many hands: farm, factory, ship, shop.
Worked example: (1) Latex from a rubber tree. (2) A factory makes boots. (3) A ship carries them. (4) The market stall sells them.

**T20. Hidden in plain sight** (Keeper's Desk). Palm oil hides in many things: some snacks, soaps and lipsticks. Most soy feeds farm animals, not people.
Worked example: (1) Soy beans grow on a farm. (2) Most become animal feed. (3) The animals become meat, milk and eggs.

**T21. Choices push back** (Keeper's Desk). Buyers pull goods along the chain. So buyers' choices can push back toward farms that protect forests.
Worked example: (1) The café wants chocolate. (2) It picks a farm that keeps its forest. (3) That farm sells more.

## Activity (a): The Canopy Stage

Opening: Make the forest floor look real. Set the treetops, then the sounds.
Treetop buttons: **No treetops** · **Thin treetops** · **Thick treetops**.
Floor readouts: **Bright** · **Dim** · **Dark and shady**.
Guided first run (2 steps): (1) Pick thick treetops. (2) Look at the floor. Is it bright or dim?
Floor lines:
- No treetops: Sunshine reaches the floor. That's a meadow, not a rainforest.
- Thin treetops: Some light gets through. Still too bright for a rainforest floor.
- Thick treetops: The treetops block the light. That's a rainforest floor.
Model line (A made-up model): Real treetops let through a little light, in patches. This stage has three settings.
Sound board opening: Tap each sound. What makes it?
Sounds: **Drip** (rain running off leaves) · **Buzz** (insects) · **Whoop** (howler monkeys) · **Squawk** (macaws) · **Peep chorus** (tree frogs).
Mist button: **Sticky air**. Line: This warm air holds lots of water. That's humid.
Contrast card (A story from Willowbank): Willowbank's willow wood is cooler, brighter and quieter. It is not a rainforest.
Sense and cause cards (tap to flip): **Dim** · the treetops catch the sunlight. **Sticky** · warm air holds lots of water. **Loud** · hidden animals call and buzz. **Dripping** · rain and dew slide off the leaves.

## History cards

**The first bouncing balls (A true thing from history).** Over 3,000 years ago, the Olmec people of Mexico made rubber balls. They mixed latex from a rubber tree with juice from a morning glory vine. The mix made the balls bounce.

**Merian's caterpillars (A true thing from history).** In 1699, Maria Sibylla Merian sailed from Amsterdam to Suriname with her daughter Dorothea. She watched caterpillars change into moths and butterflies. She painted each stage on the plant it ate. Her book of Suriname's insects and plants came out in 1705.

**Merian's helpers (A true thing from history, Sapling and up).** Merian had help finding plants and insects. Her helpers were Indigenous people and enslaved Africans. Their names were not written down in her book.

**Bates and the copycat butterflies (A true thing from history).** In 1848, Henry Walter Bates and Alfred Russel Wallace sailed from England to the Amazon. Bates stayed eleven years. He sent home thousands of kinds of insects, many new to European science.

**Bates's idea (A true thing from history, Sapling and up).** Bates saw that some harmless butterflies look like bad-tasting ones. Birds leave them alone too. Scientists still call this Batesian mimicry.

**Rubber travels (A true thing from history, Keeper's Desk).** In 1876, Henry Wickham carried about 70,000 rubber seeds from Brazil to Kew Gardens in London. Young trees were sent on to Asia. Today most rubber grows there, far from its first home.

## Problem sets

Right answers are spread across A, B and C (A 9, B 10, C 9 among the twenty-eight 3-choice items). *Say why* items are spoken to a grown-up and never stored.

### Set 1. Inside the forest (Sprout 1.1-1.4, Seedling 1.5)

**1.1 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: At midday, why is the forest floor dim?
- A. It is bright and sunny down there. *(misconception: floor-bright)*
- B. The treetops block the light. *(right)*
- C. Rainforests only have night. *(misconception: dim-means-night)*
Hint: Look up. What is above the floor?
Feedback: A: Look again. Most sunlight never reaches the floor. B: Yes. The treetops catch the light. C: Rainforests have day and night, like here.
Explanation: The thick treetops block most sunlight, so the floor stays dim.

**1.2 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Why does the rainforest air feel sticky?
- A. It is warm and full of water. *(right)*
- B. Honey drips from the trees. *(misconception: literal-sticky)*
- C. It is cool and dry. *(misconception: cool-dry-like-a-local-wood)*
Hint: What falls from the sky almost every day?
Feedback: A: Yes. Warm, wet air feels sticky. That's humid. B: Not honey. The sticky feeling comes from water in the air. C: Rainforests are warm and wet.
Explanation: Warm air full of water feels damp and sticky. We call it humid.

**1.3 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: The rainforest is loud. What makes the noise?
- A. Only the wind. *(misconception: quiet-forest)*
- B. Nothing. Forests are quiet. *(misconception: quiet-like-a-library)*
- C. Hidden animals calling and buzzing. *(right)*
Hint: Who lives in the trees?
Feedback: A: Wind helps, but listen for animals. B: Listen again. The forest is full of calls. C: Yes. Frogs, birds, monkeys and insects.
Explanation: Hundreds of hidden animals call and buzz, so the forest is loud.

**1.4 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: The rain has stopped. Do the leaves still drip?
- A. Yes. Water still runs off the leaves. *(right)*
- B. No. It's only wet while it rains. *(misconception: only-wet-when-raining)*
- C. No. The leaves drink it all at once. *(misconception: leaves-drink-it-all)*
Hint: Think of a wet umbrella after rain.
Feedback: A: Yes. Drip, drip, drip, long after the rain. B: The leaves stay wet and keep dripping. C: Leaves can't soak it all up at once.
Explanation: Water from rain and dew keeps sliding off the leaves, so the forest keeps dripping.

**1.5 (Seedling, match)**
Prompt: Match each feeling to its cause.
Pairs: dim → treetops block the light · sticky → warm air full of water · loud → hidden animals call and buzz · dripping → rain and dew run off leaves.
Misconception tested: senses as bare facts with no cause.
Hint: Ask "why?" for each one.
Explanation: Each feeling has a cause. That's what makes it a rainforest.

### Set 2. Six legs and more (Sprout 2.1-2.3, Seedling 2.4-2.6)

**2.1 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: What do leaf-cutter ants do with the leaves?
- A. They eat them for dinner. *(misconception: ants-eat-leaves)*
- B. They feed them to a fungus garden. *(right)*
- C. They use them as umbrellas. *(misconception: leaves-for-shelter)*
Hint: What grows in the ants' nest?
Feedback: A: They don't eat the leaves. They grow food with them. B: Yes. The fungus is their food. C: A fun idea, but the leaves go to the fungus.
Explanation: Leaf-cutter ants are farmers. The leaves feed the fungus, and the fungus feeds the ants.

**2.2 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which rainforest animals are there the most of?
- A. Jaguars, because they are big. *(misconception: big-animals-most-numerous)*
- B. Birds, because you hear them most. *(misconception: loudest-is-most)*
- C. Insects, small but everywhere. *(right)*
Hint: Look at every leaf and branch on one tree.
Feedback: A: Jaguars are rare. You might never see one. B: Birds are loud, but insects far outnumber them. C: Yes. Insects are the biggest crowd.
Explanation: Insects are the most numerous animals in the rainforest.

**2.3 (Sprout, 3-choice)**

**3.2 (Seedling, 3-choice)**

**3.3 (Sapling, 3-choice)**

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Rainforest:** a thick forest where it is warm and rains a lot.
- **Canopy:** the thick roof of treetops.
- **Forest floor:** the ground under the trees.
- **Understory:** the shady layer of small trees and bushes under the canopy.
- **Emergent layer:** the few giant trees that poke up above the canopy.
- **Dew:** tiny drops of water that form on leaves when warm, wet air cools.
- **Humid:** air that is damp and sticky with water.
- **Insect:** a small animal with six legs and three body parts.
- **Pollinate:** to carry pollen from flower to flower, so plants can make seeds.
- **Nectar:** a sweet juice inside flowers.
- **Minibeast:** a small animal with no backbone, like an insect or a spider.
- **Fungus:** a living thing, like a mushroom, that is not a plant or an animal.
- **Colony:** a big family of ants that live together.
- **Backbone:** the row of bones down an animal's back.
- **Mammal:** an animal with fur or hair that feeds its babies milk.
- **Bird:** an animal with feathers and a beak.
- **Reptile:** an animal with dry, scaly skin.
- **Amphibian:** an animal with moist skin, like a frog.
- **Key:** yes-or-no questions that lead you to a name or group.
- **Camouflage:** colors or shapes that help an animal hide.
- **Warning colors:** bright colors that tell hunters "stay away".
- **Mimicry:** when one kind of animal looks like another kind, which helps keep it safe.
- **Predator:** an animal that hunts other animals for food.
- **Gripping tail:** a tail that can hold on like a hand.
- **Nocturnal:** awake at night, asleep in the day.
- **Generation:** parents, then their children, then theirs.
- **Kind (species):** one sort of living thing, like the blue morpho butterfly.
- **Estimate:** a careful best guess, not an exact count.
- **Territory:** a place that belongs to a country but is far from it.
- **Biodiversity:** how many different kinds of living things live in a place.
- **Endemic:** found in one place and nowhere else.
- **Indigenous:** belonging to the people who first lived in a place.
- **Enslaved:** forced to work for others, with no pay and no freedom.
- **Latex:** a milky sap from some trees, used to make rubber.
- **Palm oil:** oil from the fruit of the oil palm tree.
- **Soy:** a bean plant grown on big farms.
- **Pasture:** a field of grass where farm animals eat.
- **Supply chain:** all the steps that bring a thing to a shop.
- **Label:** words or a sign on a package that tell you about it.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** what it feels like inside a rainforest, with a cause for each sense; that insects are its biggest crowd, including leaf-cutter ants that farm fungus; sorting rainforest life into groups by body features with a key; how features help animals survive (camouflage, warning colors, gripping tails, beaks, night activity), passed down over generations; the Amazon's place, river and range of life; biodiversity as variety, and why a small area can matter a lot; and, at the Keeper's Desk, how everyday goods trace back to rainforests through supply chains, and how buyers' choices can push back.

**What it does not claim:** the stage light, the one-tree tally, the spot bench and the 100-square bench are made-up models with stated rules, not measurements. Amazon species counts and the land and species shares are estimates that differ between sources. Labels and certification are described in general terms; the pack names no brand or scheme. Nothing here is shopping or diet advice, and nothing asks what your household buys or eats.

**Care:** The animals here are puppets and pictures. Real wild animals are watched from far away and never touched.
