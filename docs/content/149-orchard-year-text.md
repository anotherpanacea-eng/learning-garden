## Story scenes

### Scene 1. Is the orchard dead? (winter, Lantern Night)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** Pip walks through the orchard on Lantern Night. Every apple tree is bare. "They've died," he whispers. "All of them."

**Short:**
> Pip thinks the bare apple trees have died. The orchard keeper shows him fat buds on every twig. The trees are resting. The holly stays green.

**Longer:**
> Pip holds his lantern up to the first apple tree. No leaves. No apples. Just gray twigs.
>
> "They've died," he says. "The storm got them."
>
> The orchard keeper bends a twig down to his nose. At its tip sits a fat brown bud, tight as a fist.
>
> "Every twig has buds," she says. "Leaves and blossom are folded up inside. Apple trees drop their leaves each autumn and rest all winter."
>
> Pip points at the holly by the gate, shiny and green.
>
> "Holly keeps its leaves all year. It drops a few old ones at a time."
>
> Pip hangs his lantern on the gate, just in case the trees like the light.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about plants):**
> Trees that drop all their leaves for part of the year are deciduous, like apple trees. Evergreens such as holly and pine stay green but drop old leaves a few at a time. A larch is a cone tree that drops all its needles in autumn.

### Scene 2. Pip's bean (spring, Flood Boards and Planting)

**Hook:** Pip has a plan to make his bean grow faster. He plants it upside down, "so the shoot doesn't have to turn round."

**Short:**
> Pip plants his bean upside down in a glass jar. The root still grows down. The shoot still grows up. The bean knows which way is down.

**Longer:**
> The orchard keeper props a broad bean against the glass of a jar, on damp paper. "So we can watch the hidden part."
>
> Pip turns his bean upside down. "Now the shoot already points at the sky. Faster."
>
> Three days later a white root pokes out of Pip's bean, sideways. Then it curls and heads down.
>
> "It went the wrong way!"
>
> "It went the right way," says {guide}. "Roots grow down."
>
> Days later a pale shoot bends round and heads up.
>
> "Roots can feel which way is down," says the orchard keeper. "However you plant them."
>
> Pip labels his jar: CLEVER BEAN.

### Scene 3. Dot's blue hat (summer, Market Fair)

**Hook:** Dot wants blue flowers for her hat. The orchard stall only has white ones. She has a jar of blue-colored water and an idea.

**Short:**
> Dot stands white flowers in blue water. By the next day the petal edges have turned blue. The water climbed up the stems.

**Longer:**
> "White won't do," says Dot. "My hat is blue this summer."
>
> She stands three white flowers in water with blue food coloring. An hour later: still white. "Wait," says the orchard keeper.
>
> By morning every petal has blue edges, with thin blue lines like tiny rivers.
>
> "The flowers made blue!" says Pip.
>
> "The water brought the blue," says {guide}. "Up the stem, all the way to the petals."
>
> The orchard keeper snips a stem across. Inside: a ring of small blue dots. "Water tubes. My trees have them too."
>
> Dot pins a flower to her hat. "Science. Very becoming."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about plants):**
> Water moves up through fine tubes in the stem, called xylem. Water evaporates from tiny pores in the leaves, and more is drawn up from the roots to replace it. The dye travels with the water, so it shows the path. It takes hours.

### Scene 4. Did the tree eat the field? (autumn, Harvest Supper)

**Hook:** Pip looks at the biggest apple tree, then at the ground under it. "It must have eaten a lot of soil to get that big."

**Short:**
> Pip thinks the tree is made of soil. The librarian brings an old book. Someone weighed a tree and its soil, long ago. The soil hardly changed.

**Longer:**
> At the Harvest Supper, Pip sits under the oldest apple tree with a plate of apple cake.
>
> "This tree weighs more than the café. It must have eaten half the field."
>
> "Then where's the hole?" asks Bramble. There isn't one.
>
> "A man near Brussels asked your question, nearly four hundred years ago," says the librarian. "He weighed the soil in a pot, planted a small willow and waited five years. The willow grew huge. The soil weighed almost the same."
>
> "Then what is a tree made of?"
>
> "He thought: water. He was partly right. Come to the Keeper's Desk and find out which part."

**Keeper's note (A true thing from history):**
> Jan Baptist van Helmont lived near Brussels and died in 1644. His son published the willow account in 1648. He decided the tree was made of water alone. He did not know plants take in carbon dioxide, a gas from the air. Most of a tree's dry wood is built from that gas, with water.

### Scene 5. Pips for Vadoalto (Act III, the seed exchange)

**Hook:** Nuria writes from Vadoalto: they'd love some Willowbank apple seeds. The orchard keeper reads the letter twice and smiles.

**Short:**
> Nuria asks for apple seeds. Seeds from an apple won't grow the same apple. The orchard keeper sends twigs to join onto young trees instead.

**Longer:**
> Nuria's letter says: *¿Nos mandáis semillas de manzana?* Underneath: *Could you send us some apple seeds?*
>
> "They'd surprise her," says the orchard keeper. "An apple's seeds mix two trees: this one and the one the bee came from. Plant one and you get a brand-new variety. Lovely, or sour as a lemon. It takes years to find out."
>
> She holds up a twig from her best tree. "Join this onto the roots of a young tree and it grows the same apples. That's grafting."
>
> Pip adds a seed in an envelope anyway. "For the surprise."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about plants):**
> Apple trees don't grow "true" from seed: each seed mixes features from two parent trees. So named varieties are grown by grafting, joining a cut twig (the scion) onto a young rooted tree (the rootstock). A few seedlings become new varieties.

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer; worked-example steps appear one tap at a time.

**Card 1. Every plant has parts (A true thing about plants)**
> Plants have roots, a stem, leaves, and often flowers. A tree is a plant too. Its trunk is a big, woody stem.

Worked example: *A bean plant beside an apple tree.*
1. Under the soil: roots on both.
2. Holding it up: the bean's stem, the tree's trunk.
3. Leaves on both, and flowers in spring. Same parts, different sizes.

**Card 2. Bare or green in winter?**
> Deciduous trees drop all their leaves in autumn and rest. Evergreen trees keep green leaves all year, dropping old ones a few at a time.

Worked example: *The apple tree and the holly in January.*
1. The apple tree has no leaves, but its twigs have buds: resting.
2. The holly is green.
3. Apple: deciduous. Holly: evergreen.

**Card 3. A seed wakes up**
> A seed takes in water and swells. The root comes out first and grows down. Then the shoot grows up. Then leaves open.

Worked example: *The bean in the jar.*
1. Dry bean, add water: it swells.
2. A white root pushes out and turns down.
3. A shoot bends up, then two leaves open.

**Card 4. Every part has a job**
> Roots take in water and hold on. The stem carries water up. Leaves use sunlight to make food. Flowers make seeds.

Worked example: *Where does the apple tree's food come from?*
1. Roots take in water and a little mineral goodness from the soil.
2. The trunk carries the water up.
3. Leaves catch sunlight and make food.
4. So food is made in the leaves, not taken from the soil.

**Card 5. From blossom to apple**
> A bee carries pollen from one apple blossom to another. Then seeds can start. The petals fall and the flower swells into an apple.

Worked example: *One blossom in spring.*
1. A bee arrives with pollen from a different apple tree.
2. Pollen rubs off in the middle of the flower. Seeds start.
3. The petals drop. By autumn: an apple, seeds in its core.

**Card 6. Seeds on the move**
> Seeds travel away from the parent plant, so they have more room and light. A seed's shape shows how it travels.

Worked example: *A maple key.*
1. It has a thin wing.
2. Falling, it spins, so it drops slowly.
3. The wind carries it farther. Wing, so: wind.

**Card 7. Water goes up**
> Water goes in at the roots, up through tiny tubes in the stem, and out to the leaves and flowers. It takes hours.

Worked example: *Dot's white flower in blue water.*
1. Blue water at the bottom of the jar.
2. Next day: blue edges on the petals.
3. Cut the stem: blue dots inside. The water went up.

**Card 8. Where does a tree's wood come from? (Keeper's Desk)**
> A tree gains far more mass than its soil loses. So the wood must come from something else: water, and a gas from the air.

Worked example: *Van Helmont's willow (the balance below).*
1. Willow: 5 pounds, then about 169 pounds.
2. Soil: only about 2 ounces lighter.
3. So the tree did not come from the soil.

**Card 9. The leaf kitchen (Keeper's Desk)**
> Leaves use light energy to turn carbon dioxide and water into sugar. Oxygen goes out into the air. Green chlorophyll catches the light.

Worked example: *One leaf on a sunny day.*
1. In through tiny pores: carbon dioxide from the air.
2. Up from the roots: water.
3. Light is caught by chlorophyll. It brings energy. It isn't an ingredient.
4. Made: sugar, for the plant. Out: oxygen.

**Word equation (shown under card 9):** carbon dioxide + water → sugar (glucose) + oxygen, using light energy and chlorophyll.

## Blossom to Apple

**Model line:** A made-up model. *Lantern Red* and *Brook Gold* are invented apple varieties. Real orchards are more complicated.

**Bench:** *Lantern Red* and *Brook Gold* blossom side by side. Tap the bee, then a blossom. The bee picks up pollen at each visit and leaves some at the next.

| Bee's visit | Result line |
| --- | --- |
| From a Lantern Red blossom to a Brook Gold blossom (or the other way) | Pollen from the other variety. Seeds can start here. |
| From one Lantern Red blossom to another Lantern Red | Same variety. In our model, this blossom won't make an apple. |
| No visit | No pollen. This blossom won't make an apple. |

**Keeper's note (A true thing about plants):**
> Most apple trees need pollen from a different apple variety to make a good crop, so orchards plant two or more varieties that blossom together. Honeybees and wild bees such as bumblebees and mason bees carry it.

**Link line (shown under the bench):** Why do bees visit at all? Follow the Pollen Path in the Bee Meadow.

**Fast-forward to autumn:** Blossoms that got pollen from the other variety show as apples. Others show as bare stalks.

**Cut the apple (Seedling):** Cut across, an apple shows a star of five seed chambers. Tap each to count the seeds. *(Picture: 2, 2, 1, 2, 1.)*

**Line after cutting:** Five chambers. Each can hold up to two seeds. This apple has 8.

## The Keeper's Desk: the Willow in the Pot

**Header (A true thing from history):** Van Helmont's willow, as he reported it (published 1648).

**Units line (always shown):** His numbers are in the pounds and ounces of his time, which varied from town to town and were mostly a little heavier than today's.

**Balance pictures** (tap Before, then After):

| | Before | After five years |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Soil (dried) | 200 pounds | 200 pounds, less about 2 ounces |
| Willow | 5 pounds | about 169 pounds 3 ounces |

**What he did (card to read):** He dried and weighed the soil, planted a willow shoot, watered it with rain water and covered the pot against dust. After five years he weighed both again.

**Build the argument:** Choose one card for each slot.
- **Claim:** The tree did not come from the soil. ✔ · The tree slowly ate the soil it grew in (`plants-eat-soil`) · The scale must have been broken (`dismiss-data`)
- **Evidence:** The tree grew much taller and wider every year (`size-not-mass`) · Tree up about 164 pounds; soil down 2 ounces. ✔ · He gave it rain water whenever it needed it (`water-only`)
- **Reasoning:** Soil turns into wood very slowly, over many years (`plants-eat-soil`) · A big tree always needs a big, heavy pot (`irrelevant`) · Two ounces of soil can't make 164 pounds of tree. ✔

**Argument feedback (wrong cards):** Ate the soil: Then the soil would weigh far less. · Scale broken: Both soil weighings agree. · Taller: Height isn't mass. Use the weights. · Rain water: True, but it doesn't rule out soil. · Slowly: Five years used only 2 ounces. · Big pot: That doesn't say where the mass came from.

**What he got right, and what he missed (Sapling and Keeper's Desk):**
- Right: the tree was not made of soil.
- Missed: he thought it was water alone. Dry wood is about half carbon, and the carbon comes from carbon dioxide in the air.
- Missed: he did not weigh the leaves that fell each autumn.
- Missed: he had one pot. A second pot with no tree would be a fairer comparison.

**The detectives who came next (cards for the Long Line, spec 133):**
- 1648: van Helmont's willow account is published.
- 1771: Joseph Priestley finds a sprig of mint can "restore" air spoiled by a candle.
- 1779: Jan Ingenhousz shows that leaves do this only in light, and only their green parts.

## The Keeper's Desk: the Leaf Kitchen

**Prompt:** Sort each card. Where does it come from, or where does it go?

**Cards:** carbon dioxide · water · light · minerals · sugar · oxygen

**Places:** From the air · From the soil · From the Sun · Made in the leaf · Out into the air

**Answers and feedback:**
- Carbon dioxide → From the air: Yes. It comes in through tiny pores in the leaves.
- Water → From the soil: Yes. Roots take it in; the stem carries it up.
- Light → From the Sun: Yes. Light brings energy. It doesn't become part of the sugar.
- Minerals → From the soil: Yes, in small amounts. They keep the plant healthy. They aren't its food.
- Sugar → Made in the leaf: Yes. This is the plant's food. Some becomes wood and leaves.
- Oxygen → Out into the air: Yes. Leaves give out oxygen in the light.

**Night card (after sorting):** Plants also use oxygen and sugar all day and all night, to live. In good light, they make more than they use.

**E5 (true / not true).**

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Roots:** the parts under the soil. They take in water and hold the plant in place.
- **Stem:** holds the plant up and carries water. A **trunk** is a big woody stem.
- **Deciduous:** drops all its leaves for part of the year.
- **Evergreen:** keeps green leaves all year, dropping old ones a few at a time.
- **Seed:** a baby plant with a small food store, inside a coat.
- **Pollen:** fine dust from a flower. Moved to another flower, it helps a seed start.
- **Pollination:** moving pollen from flower to flower, by insects or wind.
- **Variety:** one named kind of apple, such as Lantern Red (made up) in our orchard.
- **Grafting:** joining a twig from one tree onto the roots of another.
- **Carbon dioxide:** a gas in the air. Plants build food from it.
- **Photosynthesis:** how leaves use light energy to make sugar from carbon dioxide and water.
- **Chlorophyll:** the green stuff in leaves that catches light.

## Grown-up panel

**What it does not claim:** every bench is a simplified picture. Model days, the 2 cm per hour dye speed, the seed counts and the varieties *Lantern Red* and *Brook Gold* are invented. Real apple pollination is more varied: some varieties set some fruit with their own pollen, and some are poor pollinators for others. The Bee Meadow (spec 150) teaches the bee's side: pollen must reach a flower of the same kind of plant. This pack adds the apple rule: from a different variety. No flower anatomy, xylem mechanics or chemical formulas. It builds on the light study at `#living/plants` without repeating it.
