# Act III. Beyond Willow Bridge

**Carried question:** What does upstream know?

**Hook:** A letter came from Vadoalto, up the river. The storm hit them too. The old path by the high ford is marked with faded ribbons.

**Opening** (Continue the story after the Act II celebration, or a later visit if paused):

**Short:** Vadoalto is a day's walk upstream, by the high ford. Nobody has walked it in years. The old fence posts have faded ribbons on them.

**Longer:** Upstream is closer than it sounds. Vadoalto is a day's walk by the high ford, or half a day by boat if the river is kind. Nobody in Willowbank has made the trip in years, and nobody can quite say why. "We just stopped," says the miller. "People used to come and go all the time. When I was small." On the old fence posts along the river path there are ribbons, faded almost white, tied in neat, careful knots. Pip holds one next to the bow Bramble tied on the parcel. They're the same knot.

**Ending opens when:** the explorer has made a contribution in any three of the four chapters.

## Chapter 1. The floating market

**Short:** Boats come down from Vadoalto with reed baskets and big round loaves. Everyone wants to trade. Nobody agrees on sizes.

**Longer:** One morning there are boats at the landing: flat, wide boats from Vadoalto, loaded with reed baskets, round loaves as big as cartwheels, and jars of dark honey. The traders call out prices in Spanish and English. *"¡Pan, cestas, miel!"* Bread, baskets, honey. Pip tries to trade one apple for one loaf. The baker from Vadoalto laughs so hard she has to sit down. "One of your apples," she says, "for one of my loaves? We'll need to talk about sizes."

**Keeper's note:** Trade between towns needs a rate everyone agrees on: how much of one thing is worth how much of another. A fair rate isn't always one-for-one.

**Jobs**

**Sprout · [Market Day coin counter]** A Vadoalto trader: "A reed basket is 6 coins. Can you pay exactly?"
Kept: "*¡Gracias!* Thank you! The trader gives you the basket with a little bow."

**Seedling · [Balance Stall]** The Vadoalto baker: "Is one of my loaves really as heavy as five of your apples? Let's put them on the scale."
Kept: "The scale settles it. The two bakers shake hands, flour everywhere."

**Sapling · [new: the reed-for-apples rate]** The orchard keeper: "Vadoalto will trade 2 bundles of reed for 3 baskets of apples. I have 9 baskets. And 12 next week."
Kept: "A fair trade, written down so nobody has to argue next week. The orchard keeper argues anyway, for fun."

**Keeper's Desk · case idea:** When the brook rises at Vadoalto's post, the water reaches Willowbank about 6 hours later. If it rises at 9 in the morning, when should Willowbank's bell ring? What could make the water arrive sooner?

## Chapter 2. The seed exchange

**Short:** Vadoalto grows a tall reed that loves wet ground. Willowbank grows apples. Each wants what the other has.

**Longer:** Nuria comes down on one of the boats, carrying a bundle of tall green reeds taller than she is. "*Carrizo*," she says. "Reed. We grow it by the river, where the ground is always wet. It's good for baskets and roofs, and it doesn't mind a flood." The orchard keeper looks at the reeds, then towards the low corner of her orchard, then back at the reeds. Vadoalto wants apple seedlings; their orchard was flattened in the storm. Willowbank wants reed for baskets and roofs. Everyone wants honey.

**Keeper's note:** Reeds are wetland plants. Some kinds grow in standing water and can slow water moving through them *(check: the claim and the species before naming one)*. The orchard keeper has just noticed something about her low corner.

**Jobs**

**Sprout · [Equal Groups: packing bundles]** Nuria: "We need the apple seedlings in bundles of 4, so each family upstream gets the same."
Kept: "Bundles packed. Nuria counts them in Spanish. *Uno, dos, tres...* She lets you count the last one."

**Seedling · [Seed Stall]** The seed seller: "This bag has reed seeds and apple pips mixed up. If you take one without looking, which is more likely?"
Kept: "A good guess, and a better reason. The seed seller labels her bags, finally."

**Sapling · [Living Garden: plant investigation]** {guide}: "Which plants do best in wet ground, and which in dry? The low corner of the orchard might care."
Kept: "Your notes go to the orchard keeper. She reads them twice, then goes to look at the low corner."

**Keeper's Desk · case idea:** Vadoalto's flood post and Willowbank's, side by side. Nuria has copied Vadoalto's marks for the same storm. Which town's water rose higher, and why might that be?

## Chapter 3. The route by the high ford

**Short:** {courier} sets off for Vadoalto by the old path. At every fork, there's a faded ribbon. Someone tied them to show the way.

**Longer:** The first letter to Vadoalto goes by land, by the old path through the high ford, because Dot says a courier should know the way and not just the river. {courier} sets off with a map, a sandwich and a parcel of seedlings. At the first fork there's a post with a faded ribbon on it. Blue, on the left. At the second, gold, on the right. "Bramble's grandmother," says {guide}. "She marked the whole way." Halfway there, the ribbons stop. A new path has been cut straight across the old one, and nobody tied anything to it.

**Keeper's note:** A path, a timetable and a map all describe the same journey in different ways. A map shows where; a timetable shows when; a scale tells you how far one step on the map is in the real valley.

**Jobs**

**Sprout · [Map Reader Dispatcher]** {courier}: "The parcel has to reach the ford before it can cross. Which way?"
Kept: "Across the ford, feet wet, parcel dry."

**Seedling · [Courier Clock Walk]** Dot: "If {courier} leaves at 8 and the walk to the ford takes 45 minutes, when do they get there?"
Kept: "On time. Dot writes the time in the post office book."

**Sapling · [Same Route, Different Scale / Cartographer's Table]** The librarian: "The old map and the new map show the same path at different scales. Is the path really longer now, or just drawn bigger?"
Kept: "The librarian redraws the route for the post office wall, at one scale, with your notes."

**Keeper's Desk · case idea:** The new straight path saves time but floods in spring; the old ribboned path is longer and stays dry. Which should Willowbank mark as the main Way, and for which seasons?

## Chapter 4. Letters up and down the river

**Short:** Letters go up the river to Nuria in Vadoalto and down the river to Léon in Les Saules. Some are in Spanish. Some are in French.

**Longer:** Once the path is open, letters start to travel. Nuria writes from upstream, half in Spanish and half in English, with drawings of her flood post in the margins. Then a letter arrives from the other direction: Léon, who keeps the ferry at Les Saules, where Willow Brook meets the big river. His letters are in French and English and always slightly damp. "Willowbank is in the middle," says Dot, sorting the post. "Everyone's letters come through us." She says it as if she's just been given an important job. She has.

**Keeper's note:** Spanish and French are cousins: both grew from Latin. *Río* and *rivière*, *agua* and *eau*, *puente* and *pont*. Some words are easy to guess across the family. Some are not.

**Jobs**

**Sprout · [Letter reader: Nuria or Léon]** Dot: "A letter from upstream! Shall we read it together?" (letters.md, letters 1 and 2, short versions, with read-aloud)
Kept: "Read. Dot pins it up next to the others."

**Seedling · [new: a reply with word cards]** Dot: "We should write back. Use some of their words, if you like."
Kept: "Your reply goes in the post bag. Dot says your Spanish (or French) handwriting is very elegant."

## Ending: Both Ways Across Willow Bridge

**Short:** Seedlings go up the river. Reeds and honey come down. Nuria comes to visit, and the third page comes free. She knows the handwriting.

**Longer:**

> A parcel of apple seedlings goes up the river path. A bundle of reeds and a jar of Vadoalto honey come back down. Nuria comes with them, to see the mill and the famous Willowbank flood post. She stands in front of it for a long time, reading the marks.
>
> "Ours has marks like this," she says. "Somebody taught me to read them."
>
> While she's reading the drying pages over your shoulder, the third one comes free.

> "Hollis taught me," says Nuria. "Every spring they came up the river path to check our post. Then one year they said their legs wanted the seaside, and they went. They never said they were the Keeper of anything." She laughs. "They wouldn't. Hollis didn't like titles."
