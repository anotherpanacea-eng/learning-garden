# One Seed, Many Words: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 254](../specs/254-one-seed-many-words.md). Use as written, after review. `{guide}` is never gendered. Pronouns: Pip, Hob he/him; Dot, the miller, Bramble, the orchard keeper she/her. Sources: [audit](../source-audits/254-one-seed-many-words.md). Nothing is scored, timed or locked.

**Read-aloud rule:** at Sprout and Seedling a grown-up or the device reads every prompt, hook and card aloud. Spelling choices are shown, not read aloud, because they sound alike. No sound is taught here; the grown-up panel explains the sounds.

**Root tags:** where an item needs a root word, it appears on a seed tag beside the prompt, never inside the prompt.

**Labels used throughout:**
- **A story from Willowbank** marks the town, its people, the signs and labels, and Hob's note (fiction).
- **A true thing about language** marks real facts about English spelling and words.

## Arriving

**Place title:** One Seed, Many Words

**First visit** (A story from Willowbank):
> Along the Word Garden wall, five new beds wait, with a quiet corner at the end. Each has a little sign: Plurals. Endings. Changes. Odd Ones. Roots.
>
> "Same seed, different flowers," says the orchard keeper. "Words do that too. One word, many endings."

**What you already know (recall card, links to `#words`):** In the Word Garden, cuttings made new words. Here, endings change a word's form: one seed, two seeds.

**Every bed screen (always shown):** Spelling rules usually work. A dictionary has the last word.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. Two boxs of beans (Spring, Flood Boards and Planting)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** The flood boards are in. Now the beds want seeds. Pip is in charge of the labels. He writes very fast.

**Short:**
> Pip's labels say BOXS, BUSHS and BERRYS. Dot fixes them: boxes, bushes, berries. Some words need more than an s.

**Longer:**
> The flood boards are in the millrace. Now the beds want seeds.
>
> "I'm on labels," says Pip. He writes very fast.
>
> *3 BOXS OF BEANS. 2 BUSHS. 6 SEEDS.*
>
> Dot reads them at the potting bench. "Seeds is right."
>
> "They're all right," says Pip. "More than one gets an s."
>
> "Most words do," says Dot. "But box ends in x. Words like that usually take -es. Boxes."
>
> "And bush?"
>
> "Ends in sh. Bushes."
>
> Pip fixes them. Then he finds one more label. *2 BERRYS.*
>
> "Oh no," he says.
>
> "Berry ends in a consonant and y," says {guide}. "Swap the y for i. Then add -es. Berries."
>
> Pip writes it and holds it up. "Labels," he says, "are harder than they look."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> Plural means more than one. Most English naming words add -s. Words ending in s, x, z, ch or sh usually add -es. A few words, like mouse, change in other ways.

### Scene 2. Frogs hoping (Act II, a garden across the seasons)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** A new sign stands by the lettuce bed. It says FROGS HOPING. Dot would like to know what they are hoping for.

**Short:**
> Pip's sign says the frogs are hoping. He meant hopping. Hop doubles its p. Hope drops its e. One letter changes the word.

**Longer:**
> Pip pushes a sign into the lettuce bed. *MIND THE FROGS. FROGS HOPING.*
>
> Dot reads it. "What are they hoping for?"
>
> "Lettuce, mostly," says Pip. "Also rain."
>
> "Your sign says hoping. Like wishing."
>
> Pip looks at the frogs. The frogs look busy. "They're jumping. Hop. Hopping."
>
> "Then you need two p's," says {guide}. "Hop has one vowel, then one consonant at the end. So the p doubles before -ing."
>
> "And hoping?"
>
> "That grows from hope. Hope ends in e. Drop the e, then add -ing. One p."
>
> Pip makes a second sign and puts both up. *FROGS HOPPING. GARDENER HOPING.*
>
> The miller walks past and reads the pair. "Both true," she says.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> Doubling and dropping keep words apart in writing. Hoping and hopping. Taped and tapped. Change one letter and you change the word.

### Scene 3. Three mouses (Act II, a garden across the seasons, summer)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** Something has nibbled the seed sacks in the mill shed. Pip crouches by the door with his notebook to count the nibblers.

**Short:**
> Pip counts three mouses. The miller says mice. Pip tries "two hice" for houses. Some words change in odd ways. House isn't one.

**Longer:**
> The seed sacks in the mill shed have small round holes in them.
>
> Pip crouches by the door with his notebook. "Three mouses," he whispers. "And two gooses by the pond."
>
> "Three mice," says the miller. "Two geese. Those words change inside, not at the end."
>
> Pip writes it down. He likes a rule. "So one house, two hice."
>
> The miller laughs so hard she has to sit on a sack.
>
> "Houses," says Dot. "House just adds -s."
>
> "But mouse and house rhyme!"
>
> "They do," says {guide}. "Odd plurals don't follow rhymes. You learn each one."
>
> Pip writes a list: *mouse, mice. goose, geese. house, houses.* Under it he writes *NOT HICE.*
>
> The three mice go on eating.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> Mice, geese, feet and teeth are very old plurals. Long ago, more English words made plurals by changing a vowel inside. Only a few still do.

### Scene 4. The bigest pumpkin (Act II, a garden across the seasons, late summer)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** Two pumpkins sit on the potting bench, each with a sign. Bramble reads Pip's sign and raises one eyebrow.

**Short:**
> Pip's sign says BIGEST. Bramble's says BIGGEST. Bramble is right: big doubles its g. Then the scale shows Pip's pumpkin is heavier.

**Longer:**
> Two pumpkins sit on the potting bench, each with a sign.
>
> Pip's says *BIGEST PUMPKIN.* Bramble's says *BIGGEST PUMPKIN.*
>
> Bramble taps Pip's sign. "You've lost a g."
>
> "I didn't need it," says Pip.
>
> "You did. Big has one vowel, then one consonant. So the g doubles. Bigger. Biggest."
>
> Pip looks at {guide}. {guide} nods. "She's right."
>
> "Tall doesn't double," says Pip. "Taller."
>
> "Tall already ends in two l's," says Bramble. "Nothing to double."
>
> Pip fixes his sign. The orchard keeper lifts both pumpkins onto the scale, one and then the other.
>
> Pip's is heavier.
>
> "Heavy, heavier," says Dot. "The y turned into an i."
>
> Bramble reads the scale twice. "Fine," she says. "Your spelling is better now. So is your pumpkin."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> -er compares two things: bigger, heavier. -est picks out the most of three or more: biggest. The -er in baker or farmer is a different ending with the same spelling. It means a person who does something.

### Scene 5. Rows dibbled (Spring, Flood Boards and Planting)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** Hob has pinned a note to the seed shed door. Pip reads it and frowns at one word.

**Short:**
> Hob's note says the rows are dibbled. Pip doesn't know dibble. But -ed tells him it is already done. Then Hob shows him the tool.

**Longer:**
> A note is pinned to the seed shed: *Rows dibbled. Plums unripe. Two trowels in the box. Hob.*
>
> "Dibbled," says Pip. "That's not a word."
>
> "Look at the end," says Dot. "-ed. Whatever dibbling is, it's done."
>
> "And the root is dibble," says {guide}. "Drop the e, add -ed."
>
> "Unripe," Pip goes on. "Here un- means not. Not ripe. Don't eat the plums."
>
> "Trowels has an s," says Dot. "More than one trowel."
>
> "What's a trowel?"
>
> "Still don't know," says Dot. "But there are two."
>
> Hob comes round the corner with a pointed wooden stick. He pokes a neat hole in the soil and drops a bean in. "Dibble," he says. "Dibbled. Dibbling. Done."
>
> Pip looks down the rows. Every hole is ready.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> Word parts give clues before you know a word. -s says more than one. -ed says it already happened. -ing says it is going on. Un- often says not. Then check your guess with the picture, the story or someone who knows.

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer; worked-example steps appear one tap at a time. Every card is **A true thing about language**; the example sentences are **A story from Willowbank**.

**Card 1. More than one**
> Plural means more than one. Most naming words add -s to make a plural: one seed, two seeds. One pot, three pots.

Worked example: *One bed, four ___.*
1. Bed names a thing.
2. Four means more than one.
3. Add -s: four beds.

**Card 2. Sometimes -es**
> Words that end in s, x, z, ch or sh usually add -es: box, boxes; bush, bushes; bench, benches.

Worked example: *One fox, two ___.*
1. Look at the last letter: x.
2. x is on the -es list.
3. Two foxes.

**Card 3. A y at the end**
> If a word ends in a consonant and y, usually change y to i and add -es: berry, berries. A vowel and y just adds -s: tray, trays.

Worked example: *One daisy, many ___.*
1. The last letter is y.
2. The letter before it is s, a consonant.
3. Change y to i, add -es: daisies.

**Card 4. Endings on doing words**
> Doing words take endings: -s or -es, -ing and -ed. Most just add the ending: pick, picks, picking, picked. Most show already done with -ed.

Worked example: *fill*
1. Dot fills the can.
2. Dot is filling the can.
3. Dot filled the can. No change to fill.

**Card 5. Same letters as plurals**
> Doing words use the plural letters too. After s, x, z, ch or sh, usually add -es: fix, fixes; wish, wishes; mix, mixes.

Worked example: *Dot ___ the gate every week.* Seed tag: fix.
1. Fix ends in x.
2. x is on the -es list.
3. Dot fixes the gate.

**Card 6. Comparing with -er and -est**
> -er compares two things: taller. -est picks the most out of three or more: tallest. Many short describing words just add the ending.

Worked example: *Three sunflowers.*
1. Pip's sunflower is tall.
2. Dot's is taller than Pip's.
3. Bramble's is the tallest of all.

Small line under the card: In the Word Garden, -er on painter means a person who paints. Same letters, different job.

**Card 7. Snip the e**
> If a word ends in a consonant and e, usually drop the e before an ending that starts with a vowel: hope, hoping. Usually keep it before a consonant: hopeful.

Worked example: *rake + ing*
1. Rake ends in k, then e.
2. -ing starts with i, a vowel.
3. Drop the e: raking.

Small line under the card: You met this in the Word Garden. Care plus -er made carer.

**Card 8. Swap y for i**
> If a word ends in a consonant and y, usually change y to i before -ed, -er, -est or -es: carry, carried. Before -ing, keep the y: carrying.

Worked example: *muddy + er*
1. Muddy ends in d, then y.
2. The ending is -er, not -ing.
3. Swap y for i: muddier.

**Card 9. Double it**
> A word with only one vowel letter, then one consonant at the end, usually doubles that consonant before -ing, -ed, -er or -est: hop, hopping.

Worked example: *big + est*
1. Big has only one vowel letter, i.
2. Then one consonant at the end, g.
3. Double the g: biggest.

**Card 10. When not to double**
> After two vowels, usually don't double: rain, raining. After two consonants, don't double: jump, jumped. Never double x, w or y: mixing, snowing, spraying.

Worked example: *weed + ing*
1. Weed ends in d.
2. Two vowels, e and e, come before it.
3. No doubling: weeding.

**Card 11. Odd plurals**
> A few words make plurals their own way: mouse, mice; goose, geese; child, children; sheep, sheep. Learn them one by one. Rhymes don't help.

Worked example: *One house, two ___.*
1. House rhymes with mouse.
2. Rhymes don't help. Is house odd?
3. No. It just adds -s: houses.

**Card 12. Group words**
> Some naming words name a whole group: a flock of geese, a bunch of carrots, a swarm of bees. One group, many inside.

Worked example: *A ___ of sheep came down to the ford.*
1. Many sheep move together.
2. A group of sheep is a flock.
3. A flock of sheep.

**Card 13. Find the root**
> A root word is the word before any ending: pick in picks, picked and picking. To find it, take the ending off and undo any change.

Worked example: *hopping*
1. Take off -ing: hopp.
2. The p was doubled. Undo it.
3. The root is hop.

**Card 14. Word parts are clues**
> Endings and beginnings give clues to a new word. -s: more than one. -ed: already happened. -ing: going on. Un-: often not.

Worked example: *unwashed carrots*
1. Root: wash.
2. -ed: washing is something done to them.
3. Un-: not. Unwashed means not washed yet.

**Card 15. Longer words (Keeper's Desk)**
> A longer word ending in one vowel, then one consonant, usually doubles only if its last beat is strong: begin, beginning. Garden's first beat is strong: gardening.

Worked example, with a grown-up: *forget and visit*
1. Clap forget: for-GET. Strong beat last. Forgetting.
2. Clap visit: VIS-it. Strong beat first. Visited.
3. Only forget doubles.

### (a) The Plural Bed (Sprout, Seedling; first slice)

**Header:** Doing words and describing words grow endings too. Pick a word, then an ending.

**Doing words:** fill, fix, pick, weed, water. Endings: -s or -es, -ing, -ed.

**Describing words, on three sunflowers:** tall, long, fresh. Endings: -er, -est.

- -er under the tallest of three: Three things need -est.

### (c) The Changing Bed (Seedling, Sapling)

**Header:** Some roots change when an ending joins. Pick a tool, then an ending.

**Tools:** No change · Snip the e · Swap y for i · Double it

**Tool reach cards (shown when a tool doesn't fit):**
- Snip the e: Fits a word ending in a consonant and e, before a vowel ending.
- Swap y for i: Fits a consonant and y. Not before -ing.
- Double it: Fits a word with only one vowel letter, then one consonant. Never x, w or y.
- No change: This root changes. Look at its last two letters.

### (d) The Odd Plot (Sapling)

**Header:** These plants grow their own way. Tap a card to see its plural.

**Picture pairs:** mouse, mice · goose, geese · child, children · a rake's tooth, teeth · a goose's foot, feet · leaf, leaves · loaf, loaves · sheep, sheep

**Rhyme trap card:** Mouse, mice. House, houses. They rhyme, but only mouse is odd.

**Group-word labels:** a flock of sheep · a bunch of carrots · a swarm of bees

### (e) The Root Path (Sprout to Sapling)

**Header (A story from Willowbank):** Hob's note: Rows dibbled. Plums unripe. Two trowels in the box.

**Clue stones:** Tap a word part. Its clue lights up.
- -ed in dibbled: Already happened.
- un- in unripe: Not.
- -s in trowels: More than one.

**Root finder header:** Take off the ending. Undo any change. What is the root?

**Root finder cards:** hopping → hop · hoping → hope · cried → cry · bigger → big · berries → berry · raking → rake

### (f) The Long-Word Corner (Keeper's Desk)

**Header:** Clap each word with a grown-up. Where does the strong beat fall?

**Words:** begin, beginning · forget, forgetting · garden, gardening · visit, visited · water, watering

**Honest exceptions shelf (A true thing about language):**
- Hoeing keeps its e. Words ending in oe or ee keep it before -ing: toeing, seeing. But argue drops it: arguing.
- Being keeps its e. It is a true exception.
- Argue drops its e in argument, though -ment starts with a consonant.
- Say, said and pay, paid change the y after a vowel.
- Stomach ends in ch, but adds just -s: stomachs.
- Potatoes and tomatoes add -es. Pianos and photos add -s. Check o-words in a dictionary.
- Leaf, leaves, but roof, roofs. Check f-words too.
- Label, labeled. Some countries spell it labelled. This garden uses the American spelling.

## Problem sets

### Set A. More than one (Sprout, Seedling)

- Hint: What letter does box end with?
- Explanation: Box ends in x, so it takes -es: boxes.

**A2 (Sprout; picture: one bucket, then three buckets).**

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Plural:** more than one. Seeds is the plural of seed.
- **Naming word:** a word for a person, place or thing, like seed or fox. Also called a noun.
- **Doing word:** a word for an action, like pick or hop. Also called a verb.
- **Describing word:** a word that tells what something is like, like tall or muddy. Also called an adjective.
- **Root word:** a word before any ending is added, like hop in hopping.
- **Ending:** letters added at the end of a word, like -s, -ed or -ing. Also called a suffix.
- **Vowels:** the letters a, e, i, o and u.
- **Consonants:** the other letters. Y can work either way, so these rules look at the letter before it.
- **Beat (Keeper's Desk):** one part of a word you can clap with a grown-up. Garden has two beats: gar, den.
- **Group word:** a word for a whole group, like flock.
- **Odd plural:** a plural made its own way, like mice.
- **Clue:** a part of a word that helps you guess its meaning.
- **Dibble:** a pointed tool for making holes to plant seeds. To dibble is to plant with one.
- **Trowel:** a small hand spade for digging.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** regular plurals with -s and -es, and consonant-y plurals with -ies; -s or -es, -ing and -ed on verbs and -er and -est on adjectives with no change to the root; the three root changes (drop a final e, change y to i, double a final consonant) and where each one stops; common irregular plurals and collective nouns; finding a root inside a changed word; and -s, -ed, -ing and un- as clues to a new word's meaning. At Keeper's Desk: doubling in longer words, and honest exceptions.

**About sounds:** the English national curriculum states the plural rule by sound. If the ending sounds like s or z it is spelled -s; if it adds an extra beat it is spelled -es. The regular past ending can sound like "id" (hunted), "d" (buzzed) or "t" (jumped), but is always spelled -ed. This pack shows the letter version on screen, which gives the same spelling for every word here except stomachs, which is on the exceptions shelf. Words already ending in e, like house, add a beat but just -s. Doubling on screen uses a letter test (only one vowel letter) where the curriculum says one syllable; they agree for every word here. Beats and stress appear only at Keeper's Desk, clapped with a grown-up. Sound teaching and recorded audio are for the separate phonics review.

**About doubling:** the double-letter rule keeps a short vowel short (hopping, not hoping). In longer words, it depends on stress: be-GIN-ning doubles, GAR-den-ing does not. American spelling keeps one l on unstressed endings (labeled, traveled); British spelling doubles it (labelled, travelled).

**About mouses and foots:** forms like these show a child has learned the -s pattern and is using it widely. They are a normal stage and usually pass. A gentle recast ("Yes, three mice!") works better than a drill.

**Home and regional forms:** some families and regions use different plurals in speech. This pack teaches the written forms and never marks a child's own speech wrong.

**What it does not claim:** these are usual patterns, not laws, and a dictionary settles any doubt. Reading these endings aloud is taught in Pip's Library Cushions; which verb form agrees with which subject in The Puppet's Sentence Cards; past tense and odd past forms like went in Then, Now and Next; -ly and other suffixes in Market Signs; word families and Latin and Greek roots in The Grafting Bench.
