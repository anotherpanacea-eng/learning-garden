# The Old Keeper's Spellings: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 268](../specs/268-old-keepers-spellings.md). Use as written, after review. `{guide}` is never gendered. Pronouns: Pip he/him; Dot, Bramble, Nuria she/her; the baker he/him; the librarian they/them; Léon he/him; Hollis they/them (named only in scene 4 and after it, once page 3 is free). Sources: [audit](../source-audits/268-old-keepers-spellings.md). Nothing is scored, timed or locked.

**Page rule:** scenes 1 to 3 and every bench except Pip's Copy use page 1's map labels (free since the end of Act I) or lines newly written for this pack, and say "the old Keeper". Scene 4, scene 5 and Pip's Copy read page 3 and open only after page 3 comes free at the end of Act III. In standalone play without that page, Pip's Copy is not offered and nothing else changes.

**Read-aloud rule:** spelling choices are shown, never read aloud, because homophones and silent letters sound alike. Scenes, cards and prompts have read-aloud.

**Labels used throughout:**
- **A story from Willowbank** marks the town, its people, the map tracing and every letter or copy in it (fiction).
- **A true thing about language** marks real spelling patterns and real word histories.
- **A made-up model** is not used: nothing here is a model.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. The stained labels (Act III ch. 4, letters up and down the river)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** Nuria asked what the valley looked like long ago. The librarian traces the old Keeper's map to send her. Some labels are stained.

**Short:**
> The librarian traces the old map for Nuria. Water has stained some labels. The missing letters are ones you can't hear.

**Longer:**
> The librarian copies the old Keeper's map onto thin paper, label by label. "Nuria asked what our valley looked like before," they say. "So she gets the whole thing."
>
> Some labels have stains. Hi▢▢ Ford. ▢noll Wood. The I▢land.
>
> Pip squints. "The water took the letters nobody says. That's suspicious."
>
> "Those letters were spoken once," says {guide}. "Long ago, people said the k in knoll."
>
> "Not all of them," says Bramble, leaning in the doorway. "My grandmother said someone just stuck the s into island."
>
> The librarian opens the thick dictionary. They read for a while.
>
> "She's right," they say. "That s was never said in English."
>
> Bramble looks very pleased. Her magpie looks pleased too, on principle.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> Island was once written iland. Writers added an s about 500 years ago, because they thought it came from isle. It doesn't. The two words only look like family.

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer; worked-example steps appear one tap at a time. Every card is **A true thing about language**.

**Card 1. Silent letters**
> A silent letter is written but not said. In knoll, the k is silent. You have to remember it, because you can't hear it.

Worked example: *▢noll Wood on the map.*
1. Say knoll. You hear n first.
2. The word-history note says: once written cnoll, with the k said.
3. Restore the k: Knoll Wood.

**Card 2. Some silent letters were once said**
> Long ago, people said the k in knife, the w in write, the g in gnaw and the b in lamb. The sound left. The letter stayed.

Worked example: *Gnat Corner.*
1. The note says: people once said the g in gnat.
2. Speech changed. Spelling didn't.
3. So the g stays: Gnat Corner.

**Card 3. Some were added by writers**
> Some silent letters were never said in English. Writers added them to look like an older word. Island, debt and doubt got letters this way.

Worked example: *Debt.*
1. Old spelling: dette, no b.
2. Writers added b to match the Latin word debitum.
3. The b was never said in English.

**Card 4. Hear it in the family**
> A silent letter is often heard in a family word. In sign, the g is silent, but signal says it. In muscle, the c is silent, but muscular says it.

Worked example: *Bomb.*
1. Find a family word: bombard.
2. Say bombard. You hear the b.
3. So bomb keeps its b.

**Card 5. -cious or -tious?**
> If the root word ends in -ce, use -cious: space, spacious. If a -tion word is in the family, -tious is usual: caution, cautious.

Worked example: *Grace.*
1. The root is grace. It ends in -ce.
2. Swap -ce for -cious.
3. Gracious.

**Card 6. -cial or -tial?**
> After a vowel letter, -cial is common: official, special. After a consonant letter, -tial is common: partial, essential. Learn the odd ones: initial, financial.

Worked example: *Confidential.*
1. The letter before the ending is n.
2. n is a consonant letter.
3. So -tial: confidential.

**Card 7. -ant or -ent?**
> If c or g sounds hard, as in cat or go, use -ant: significant, elegant. If it sounds soft, as in city or gem, use -ent: innocent.

Worked example: *Elegant and intelligent.*
1. In elegant, the g sounds hard, like go.
2. Hard g, so -ant: elegant.
3. In intelligent, the g sounds soft, like gem. So -ent.

**Card 8. -able or -ible?**
> If a whole word comes before the ending, -able is usual: depend, dependable. If not, -ible is usual: visible, terrible. Learn the odd ones: sensible.

Worked example: *Comfortable.*
1. Cover the ending. Is comfort a whole word? Yes.
2. So -able is the likely ending.
3. Comfortable.

**Card 9. Keep the e to keep it soft**
> Notice ends in -ce. Before -able, keep the e, or the c turns hard. Noticeable. Change and changeable work the same way.

Worked example: *Manageable.*
1. Manage ends in -ge.
2. Without the e, ga would sound hard, like gap.
3. Keep the e: manageable.

**Card 10. From -able to -ably**
> To make a word that tells how, change the final e to y: comfortable becomes comfortably, terrible becomes terribly.

Worked example: *Possible.*
1. Possible ends in -ble.
2. Change the e to y.
3. Possibly.

**Card 11. Double the r when the beat stays on fer**
> Clap the word with a grown-up. If the strong beat stays on fer, double the r: preferring. If it moves away, don't: preference.

Worked example: *Refer.*
1. Clap re-FER-ring. The strong beat stays on fer.
2. So double the r: referring.
3. Clap REF-er-ence. The beat moved, so one r.

**Card 12. Hyphens that change the meaning**
> A hyphen can keep two meanings apart. Recover means get well. Re-cover means cover again. Resign means leave a job. Re-sign means sign again.

Worked example: *Hob's hens.*
1. A coop is a hen house.
2. A co-op is a shop its members own together.
3. The hyphen tells you which one.

**Card 13. ei after c**
> When it sounds like ee right after c, write ei: receive, ceiling. Learn the odd ones: seize, protein. In science, ie doesn't sound like ee.

Worked example: *Receipt.*
1. After c, the sound is ee.
2. So write ei: rec-ei-pt.
3. The p is silent too. Writers added it.

**Card 14. Homophones need meaning**
> Homophones sound the same but have different spellings and meanings. Need and knead. Ways and weighs. Read for meaning, then choose.

Worked example: *Advice or advise?*
1. Advice is a noun, a thing you give.
2. Advise is a verb, an action you do.
3. "Dot gave good advice." A thing, so advice.

**Card 15. One letter string, many sounds (ough)**
> The letters ough spell several sounds: though, through, thought, tough, cough, bough. Learn each word by its meaning and its family.

Worked example: *Dough.*
1. The baker kneads dough.
2. Dough sounds like though.
3. Same letters, same sound: d-ough.

**Card 16. Long words, one part at a time (Keeper's Desk)**
> Split a long word into parts. Spell each part by a rule you know. Then check the whole word in a dictionary.

Worked example: *Photosynthesis.*
1. Parts: photo, syn, thesis.
2. Greek parts often spell f as ph and i as y.
3. Check the dictionary: photosynthesis.

### Set D. Homophones (Seedling, Sapling)

**D1 (Seedling, guided).** Steps shown first: 1. Pressing and folding dough is kneading. 2. Kneading has a silent k. 3. Choose the one with k.
- Prompt: The baker presses and folds the dough. He ___ it each morning.
- Choices: needs (`sound-picks-spelling`) · kneeds (`silent-letter-only`) · kneads ✔
- Feedback: needs: That means he must have it. He's pressing it. · kneeds: The k is right. The vowels are ea.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Silent letter:** a letter that is written but not said, like the k in knee.
- **Root word:** the main part of a word, before endings or beginnings are added.
- **Suffix:** an ending added to a word, like -able or -ous.
- **Prefix:** a beginning added to a word, like re- or co-.
- **Word family:** words that share a root and part of their meaning, like sign and signal.
- **Word history:** where a word came from, and how its spelling changed.
- **Vowel letter:** a, e, i, o or u.
- **Consonant letter:** any letter that isn't a vowel letter.
- **Hard and soft c or g:** hard as in cat and go; soft as in city and gem.
- **Beat:** one part of a word you can clap, like re-fer.
- **Strong beat:** the beat you say a little louder.
- **Hyphen:** a short line that joins parts of a word, as in re-cover.
- **Homophone:** a word that sounds like another but has a different spelling and meaning.
- **Noun:** a word for a thing, person or place.
- **Verb:** a word for an action.
- **Glossary:** a list of words with their meanings.
- **Catchment:** the land that drains into one river.
- **Permeable:** letting water soak through.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** Years 5 and 6 spelling patterns from England's national curriculum, written in US spelling: silent letters and their histories, -cious and -tious, -cial and -tial, -ant and -ent, -able and -ible with their -ably and -ibly forms, doubling after -fer, hyphens that keep meanings apart, ei after c, ough, homophones, words from the Years 5 and 6 list sorted by pattern, and, at Keeper's Desk, applying all of these to long subject words.

**US and UK spelling:** the game uses US spelling. The English curriculum teaches *practice* (noun) and *practise* (verb), and *licence* and *license*, in British spelling; in US spelling *practice* and *license* serve as noun and verb, so this pack never asks children to choose between them. *Advice* and *advise*, *device* and *devise* work the same in both. British spelling hyphenates *co-ordinate*, *co-operate* and *re-enter*; US dictionaries usually close them (*coordinate*, *cooperate*, *reenter*), so the pack keys hyphens only where meaning changes. The statutory list's *criticise*, *marvellous*, *neighbour*, *programme* and *recognise* appear here as *criticize*, *marvelous*, *neighbor*, *program* and *recognize*, and the curriculum's *plough* appears as *bough*.

**About sounds:** spelling choices are never read aloud. Stress is clapped with you, never played or recorded. Accents differ: for many speakers *which* and *witch* sound alike, and some still say the h.

**What it does not claim:** rules here "usually" work, and the odd ones are named. Willowbank, Hollis, the map tracing, the librarian's letter, Pip's postcard and the glossary card are invented. Word histories come from dictionaries, named in the source audit.
