Companion to [spec 270](../specs/270-archive-reading-room.md). Use as written, after review. `{guide}` and `{courier}` are never gendered. Pronouns: the librarian they/them; the miller she/her; Bramble, Nuria and Dot she/her; Pip he/him; Hollis they/them. Sources: [audit](../source-audits/270-archive-reading-room.md). **Hollis and page rule:** Hollis is named only in Act IV and Act V scenes (sections marked *Act IV* or *Act V*). The Act III scene and every standalone screen say "the old Keeper". ### Folder 1. Paris, 1910

**Text 1A. Why the Seine Rose** (A true thing from history; cause and effect)

**Short:**
> In late 1909, a lot of rain fell on the land around the river Seine. In January 1910, heavy rain fell again. The ground was too full to soak it up, so the water ran into the rivers. The Seine rose through Paris.

**Longer:**
> The autumn of 1909 was very wet around the Seine. Month after month, rain fell on the river's land, and the ground filled up with water. Then, from 18 to 21 January 1910, heavy rain fell for four days. The ground was soaked, and in places it was frozen, so the rain could not sink in. As a result, it ran off into streams and rivers. Several rivers that flow into the Seine rose at the same time, and their water reached Paris together. On 28 January, the gauge at the Austerlitz bridge read 8.62 meters, the highest of that flood.

**Keeper's length** adds:
> Rivers that flow into a bigger river are called tributaries. The Yonne and the Marne are two of the Seine's. In 1910 their high water came at about the same time, so the Seine had to carry it all at once. More rain fell from 23 to 25 January and kept the rivers high. While the water was still high, the government set up a commission to study why it happened and how to lessen future floods.

**Text 1B. The Flood on a Line** (A true thing from history; a timeline and a gauge picture)

**Timeline cards (left to right):**
- **Autumn 1909:** A very wet autumn. The ground fills with water.
- **18 to 21 January 1910:** Four days of heavy rain.
- **28 January 1910:** The Seine is highest at the Austerlitz gauge: 8.62 meters.
- **9 February 1910:** The government sets up a flood commission.
- **15 March 1910:** The Seine is back in its bed.

**Gauge picture caption:** A gauge is a tall ruler beside a river. This simplified picture shows the 8.62-meter mark on 28 January 1910.

**Timeline note:** The cards are spaced in order, not by length of time.

### Folder 2. Valencia, 1957

**Text 2A. The Day the Turia Overflowed** (A true thing from history; time order)

**Short:**
> In October 1957, heavy rain fell on Valencia, in Spain, and on the hills upstream. On 14 October, the river Turia overflowed. Water filled the city streets. Afterward, the government planned a new path for the river.

**Longer:**
> In October 1957, storms brought heavy rain to Valencia, a city on the east coast of Spain, and to the hills upstream. First, rain fell for several days. Then, on 14 October, the river Turia overflowed its banks where it ran through the city. Water filled streets, homes and shops. After the flood, the government chose a plan. In 1961 a law made it official: dig a new channel, about 12 kilometers long, to carry the river around the south of the city. Finally, after years of digging, the river had a new path.

**Keeper's length** adds:
> Many people died in the flood. The 1961 law also set the planned size of the new channel: about 200 meters wide.

**Text 2B. A River Moved, a Garden Made** (A true thing from history; problem and solution)

**Short:**
> The problem: the river ran through the middle of Valencia. The solution: move it. A new channel took the river around the city. The old riverbed was left dry. Neighbors asked for a park there, and they got one.

**Longer:**
> **The problem.** The river Turia ran straight through the middle of Valencia. When it flooded, the city flooded.
>
> **The solution.** The government moved the river. A new channel now carries it around the south of the city.
>
> **A new problem.** That left the old riverbed dry: a long, empty strip through the city. A city plan proposed building a road along it. Many neighbors wanted something else.
>
> **Their solution.** They campaigned for a park. The city agreed. In 1986 the Turia Gardens opened. Today the park runs for about 9 kilometers through the city, where the river used to be.

**Keeper's length** adds:
> The neighbors' slogan was in Valencian, a language many people in Valencia speak alongside Spanish: *El riu és nostre i el volem verd.* In English: "The river is ours, and we want it green." *(Valencian line: fluent reader needed.)*

**Folder 2 timeline (shown beside both texts):** 1957 flood · 1961 law · new channel dug · neighbors' campaign · 1986 gardens open.

### Folder 3. Room for the River (the Netherlands)

**Text 3. Two Ways to Keep a Town Dry** (A true thing from history; compare and contrast, then problem and solution)

**Short:**
> Much of the Netherlands is low, flat land beside big rivers. Long banks of earth called dikes keep the water out. After high water in 1993 and 1995, the country also gave its rivers more room.

**Longer:**
> **Way one: a higher dike.** A dike is a long bank of earth beside a river. A higher dike protects the land behind it. But the water it holds back still has to go somewhere.
>
> **Way two: more room.** Move a dike back from the river, lower the land beside it, or dig a side channel. Then high water can spread out and flow past. But it needs land, and people may already be using that land.
>
> **What the Netherlands did.** In 1993 and 1995, the big rivers rose very high. In 1995, more than 200,000 people had to leave their homes for a while. Afterward, the country did not only raise its dikes. In many places it also gave its rivers more room. The program was called Room for the River.

**Keeper's length** adds:
> In the city of Nijmegen, a dike was moved back about 350 meters, and a side channel about 3 kilometers long was dug beside the river Waal. The land between them became an island. Work there finished around 2016.

**Glossary box:** **dike:** a long bank of earth that keeps a river or the sea out. **side channel:** a second path dug for high water.

### Folder 4. Marks on the Wall (France)

**Text 4. Marks on the Wall** (A true thing from history; laid out with headings, a caption and a key word in bold)

**Heading: What a flood mark is**
> In many French towns, small marks on walls and bridges show how high the water came in a great flood. Most have a line and a date. In French, a flood mark is a **repère de crue**. *(French term: fluent reader needed.)*

**Heading: What the law asks**
> A French law from 2003 says towns in flood areas must do three things. First, find and list their old flood marks. Next, put up marks for great floods. Then, look after the marks and keep them where people can see them.

**Heading: Why marks matter**
> Great floods are rare. People forget, and newcomers may never know. A mark shows how high the water once came, long after the flood has gone.

**Picture caption:** A simplified drawing of a flood mark on a wall: a line, a date and an arrow pointing to the line.

### Folder 5. Stairs into the Nile (Egypt)

**Text 5. Stairs into the Nile** (A true thing from history; description; facts from [spec 134](../specs/134-nile-year.md))

**Short:**
> In ancient Egypt, the Nile flooded every year. Farmers needed the flood: it left rich new soil. People read the flood's height on stone gauges called nilometers.

**Longer** adds:
> On Elephantine Island at Aswan, a stone stair runs down to the river, with level marks on its walls. A low flood could mean hunger. A very high flood could damage crops and buildings. A flood that was just right meant a good harvest.

**Link line:** More about the Nile year is in the Nile drawer (`#archive/nile`).

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer; worked-example steps appear one tap at a time. Cards are **A true thing about language**.

**Card 1. Features help you find and understand**
> Headings tell you what a part is about. A caption explains a picture. Bold type marks a key word. Each feature helps a reader.

Worked example: *Marks on the Wall.*
1. You want to know what the law asks.
2. Find the heading "What the law asks".
3. Read only that part.

**Card 2. Pictures and charts can add**
> A chart, timeline or diagram can show what words leave out: exact numbers, how far apart, or what something looks like.

Worked example: *The Paris gauge picture.*
1. The Short text says the Seine rose.
2. The gauge picture shows how high: 8.62 meters.
3. The picture added a number.

**Card 3. Time order**
> Some texts tell events in the order they happened. Look for dates and words like first, then, after and finally.

Worked example: *The Day the Turia Overflowed.*
1. First: rain for several days.
2. Then: the river overflowed.
3. After: a plan, a law, a new channel.

**Card 4. Cause and effect**
> Some texts explain why something happened. One thing leads to another. Look for because, so and as a result.

Worked example: *Why the Seine Rose.*
1. The ground was full, and frozen in places.
2. So the rain could not sink in.
3. As a result, it ran into the rivers.

**Card 5. Problem and solution**
> Some texts name a problem, then what people did about it. A solution can bring a new problem too.

Worked example: *A River Moved, a Garden Made.*
1. Problem: the river ran through the city.
2. Solution: move the river.
3. New problem: an empty riverbed.

**Card 6. Compare and contrast**
> Some texts set two things side by side. Look for both, but, unlike and however.

Worked example: *Two Ways to Keep a Town Dry.*
1. Way one: a higher dike.
2. Way two: more room.
3. Both aim to keep towns dry. They work differently.

**Card 7. What happened, and why**
> To explain an event, say what happened, then the causes that led to it. Use details from the text, not guesses.

Worked example: *Paris, 1910.*
1. What happened: the Seine rose very high.
2. Why: a wet autumn, then four days of rain.
3. Detail: the tributaries rose together.

**Card 8. A point needs reasons and evidence**
> A point is what the writer wants you to believe. Evidence is a fact that backs it up. Good evidence fits the point and is enough.

Worked example: *Two Ways to Keep a Town Dry.*
1. Point: higher dikes alone were not enough.
2. Evidence: in 1995, more than 200,000 people left home.
3. It fits the point.

**Card 9. Fact or opinion?**
> A fact can be checked. An opinion is what someone thinks. Some claims could be checked but have not been yet. Sounding sure doesn't make them facts.

Worked example: *Two sentences about the Turia Gardens.*
1. "The gardens opened in 1986." Can we check it? Yes. Fact.
2. "They are the best park anywhere." Opinion.
3. "Nobody ever floods there now." Could be checked, but has it been? Not yet. An unchecked claim.

**Card 10. Main ideas and a summary**
> A summary gives the main ideas in a few sentences. It leaves out small details and your own opinion.

Worked example: *A River Moved, a Garden Made.*
1. Main idea one: Valencia moved its river.
2. Main idea two: neighbors turned the dry bed into a park.
3. Summary: both, in two sentences.

**Card 11. Two texts, one topic**
> Reading two texts on one topic, ask three things. What do both say? What does only one say? Do they disagree?

Worked example: *Folder 1.*
1. Both: the Seine was highest on 28 January.
2. Only the timeline: back in its bed by 15 March.
3. They don't disagree.

**Card 12. Bring sources together**
> To combine sources, say each fact in your own words. Put the source in brackets after it, so a reader can check.

Worked example: *A fact card.*
1. Source says: "A new channel now carries it around the south of the city."
2. Own words: Valencia gave its river a new path.
3. Add: (Folder 2).

## Benches

### (a) Features and Pictures

**Header:** Build the chain. Put each cause before the thing it led to.

**Paris chain cards:** a very wet autumn · the ground is full, and frozen in places · four days of heavy rain · rain runs off into the rivers · the tributaries rise together · the Seine is highest on 28 January.

### (c) Text Shapes

**Header:** Tap the signal words. Then choose the text's shape.

**Signal-word sort:** time order (first, then, after, finally) · cause and effect (because, so, as a result) · problem and solution (the problem, the solution) · compare and contrast (both, but, unlike, however).

**Side by side (Keeper's Desk):** Both Valencia texts open in two columns. Tap one sentence in each that shows its shape.

**Side-by-side line:** Same flood, two shapes. Each shape helps a reader see something different.

### (d) Point and Proof

**Header:** Find the point. Then check each piece of evidence: does it fit, and is it enough?

**Evidence sorter (the Gazette page; after page 4 comes free):** each sentence goes in one tray: *checked fact* · *claim not checked yet* · *opinion* · *evidence for the point*. A sentence can be a fact and evidence at once; tap both trays.

**Bramble's card (A story from Willowbank):** "Both pieces have a point. Neither one is enough on its own. That's not the same as both being wrong."

**What would help?** Choose a record that could test both pieces (see D5).

### (e) Two Texts Together (Keeper's Desk)

**Header:** Read both texts in a folder. Sort each fact card: in both, only the first, only the second.

**Disagree tray:** Do the texts disagree anywhere? (Folder 1 and Folder 2: no. The tray stays open for a reader to check.)

**Summary chooser:** Three summaries. Choose the one with the main ideas and no opinion.

**Headings:** What happened · What people did · What changed afterward.

### Set D. Point and proof (Sapling, Keeper's Desk; D3 to D5 after page 4 comes free)

**D1 (Sapling, guided sort).** Steps shown first: 1. Can someone check it? 2. Or is it what someone thinks? 3. Look for clearly or best.
- Prompt: Fact or opinion? Sort each sentence.
- Cards: "The Turia Gardens opened in 1986." (fact) · "The Turia Gardens are the best park in Spain." (opinion) · "The old map shows marsh where the water now sits." (fact, A story from Willowbank) · "The new footbridge is the prettiest place in Willowbank." (opinion, A story from Willowbank)
- Feedback for the last card in fact (`confident-is-fact`): Prettiest is what someone thinks. Nobody can check it.
- Hint: Could you check it in a record?
- Explanation: Facts can be checked. Opinions can be argued for, but not checked.

**D2 (Sapling; uses the Longer text).**
- Prompt: Which detail is evidence that higher dikes alone were not enough?
- Choices: Dikes are long banks of earth built beside rivers (`evidence-any-fact`) · High water made over 200,000 people leave home ✔ · The Netherlands is a lovely country (`opinion-as-evidence`)
- Feedback: Banks of earth: True, but it describes a dike. It doesn't back the point. · Lovely: That's an opinion, and off the point.
- Hint: Which detail shows dikes alone were not keeping people safe?
- Explanation: Evidence must fit the point, not just be true.

**D3 (Sapling; after page 4 comes free).**

**D4 (Keeper's Desk; after page 4 comes free).**

## "I'm stuck" ladder

**Step 1, hint:** the item's own hint.

**Step 2, an easier case and how to start:**
- Feature items: "Try this one first: a recipe card. Its heading says *Pancakes*. Its bold word is **whisk**. Now find this text's features."
- Picture items: "Try this one first: a note says *the hen laid more on Monday*. A chart shows 5 on Monday, 2 on Tuesday. The chart adds numbers."
- Why items: "Try this one first: a lawn already soaked, then more rain. So puddles form. Now find this text's *so*."
- Shape items: "Try this one first: *First we mixed. Then we baked. Finally we ate.* Time order. Now find this text's signal words."
- Fact or opinion items: "Try this one first: *The café opens at eight* can be checked. *The buns are the best* can't. Now test yours."
- Two-text items: "Try this one first: one note says *Dot came at noon*. Another says *Dot came at noon with a hat*. The second adds the hat."

**Step 3:** Skip for now. You can come back any time.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Text feature:** a part of a page that helps a reader, like a heading, caption or bold word.
- **Caption:** words beside a picture that say what it shows.
- **Timeline:** dates set in order along a line.
- **Gauge:** a tall ruler beside a river for reading how high the water is.
- **Signal word:** a word that shows how ideas connect, like then, because or however.
- **Cause:** what makes something happen. **Effect:** what happens because of it.
- **Tributary:** a river that flows into a bigger river.
- **Channel:** a path dug or worn for water to flow along.
- **Dike:** a long bank of earth that keeps water out.
- **Commission:** a group of people chosen to study a problem.
- **Campaign:** working together to ask for a change.
- **Point:** what a writer wants you to believe.
- **Evidence:** facts that back up a point.
- **Fact:** something that can be checked. **Unchecked claim:** something that could be checked but hasn't been yet. **Opinion:** what someone thinks or feels.
- **Main idea:** what a whole text or part is mostly about.
- **Summary:** the main ideas, short, without your opinion.
- **Source:** where a piece of information came from.

## Off-screen card (adult-led)

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** reading nonfiction for structure (time order, cause and effect, problem and solution, compare and contrast), text features and pictures, explaining what happened and why, reasons and evidence, fact and opinion, summary, and bringing two or more texts together with their sources.

**UK terms:** the English national curriculum says "non-fiction", "presentational features" and "summarise". US spelling is used on screen.

**The real floods:** every history passage is newly written from the sources in the audit. Paris 1910, Valencia 1957, the Netherlands' Room for the River program, France's flood-mark law and the Nile are real. Willowbank, the Gazette, the flood posts and the two-post chart are made up. The pack does not cover recent floods. Valencia flooded again in 2024; this pack is about 1957 only.

**About loss:** the Valencia flood of 1957 killed many people. The pack says so once, in the longest reading, and gives no number. Children who want to know more can explore it with you.

**What it does not claim:** no text here says which flood plan is right. The two Gazette pieces each have a fair point and neither is proof. Willowbank's meeting decides its own plan.
