## Story scenes

### Scene 1. Six hats on the pegs (Act II ch. 3, Ember's birthday)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** The party is over. Six hats hang on the café pegs. Dot's note says FOUND: A BLUE HAT. Two people come to collect it, and Dot's own hat is blue too.

**Short:**
> Six hats are left after Ember's party. "A blue hat" fits four of them. Dot needs more words, in the usual order.

**Longer:**
> The café is quiet. Crumbs, ribbons, one sleeping Puff. On the pegs by the door hang six hats nobody took home.
>
> Dot pins up a note: *FOUND: a blue hat.*
>
> By lunchtime, two people have come for it.
>
> "Mine's blue," says the baker.
>
> "So is mine," says the orchard keeper.
>
> "Mine too," says Dot, a little stiffly. "I never take my hat off. I took it off to dance. It was a special occasion."
>
> A fourth blue hat belongs to Puff, who is still asleep.
>
> Pip squints at the pegs. "There are two small blue ones."
>
> "One is old and one is new," says Dot. "Mine is my small old blue hat."
>
> "Why not your blue old small hat?" asks {guide}.
>
> Dot opens her mouth, then shuts it. "It sounds wrong. I don't know why."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> English speakers usually put size before age, and age before color: a small old blue hat. Most people never learn this as a rule. They just hear "blue old small" and frown. Grammar books list the full order in slightly different ways, so treat it as a strong habit, not a law.

### Scene 2. Lost: my patience (Act II ch. 3)

**Hook:** Pip has waited all afternoon for cake. He pins a note to Dot's shelf: LOST: MY PATIENCE. Dot is not sure it counts.

**Short:**
> Pip's note says he lost his patience. Bramble says nobody can hand that back. Patience is a noun, but not one you can touch.

**Longer:**
> The cake is not ready. It has not been ready for some time.
>
> Pip writes a note and pins it up: *LOST: my patience. Last seen before lunch.*
>
> Dot reads it twice. "This shelf is for things."
>
> "Patience is a thing," says Pip. "It's a noun."
>
> "It's a noun," says Bramble, arriving with a basket of ribbons. "But nobody can hand it back. You can't see it. You can't hold it."
>
> "Then where did it go?" says Pip.
>
> The cake arrives. Pip waits, very still, while Ember blows out the candles, all of them, twice.
>
> "Found it," says Dot, and takes the note down.

### Scene 3. Ember's thank-you notes (Act II ch. 3)

**Hook:** Ember brings a stack of thank-you notes to the post office, slightly singed at the corners. They have to go in the post, so Dot reads them first.

**Short:**
> Ember's notes mix up describing words. Dot helps: the song was beautiful, and Puff sang beautifully.

**Longer:**
> The day after the party, Ember lays a stack of thank-you notes on the counter. Each one is a little singed.
>
> "Can you read them before they go?" asks Ember.
>
> Dot reads the first. *Dear Puff, thank you for singing so beautiful.*
>
> "Your song was beautiful," says Dot. "But Puff sang beautifully. One word tells about the song. The other tells how Puff sang."
>
> Ember frowns. "And the baker's? I wrote that the cake was lovely."
>
> "Lovely is fine," says Dot. "It ends in -ly, but it tells about the cake. Not every -ly word tells how."
>
> Ember fixes the note, then adds a line: *P.S. You sang more beautifully than the magpie.*
>
> "Kind," says Dot. "And mostly true."

### Scene 4. The magpie's shelf (Act II ch. 3)

**Hook:** After the party, a spoon, a button and a ribbon go missing. They turn up in a row on Bramble's ribbon gate. Bramble says the magpie only borrowed them.

**Short:**
> Dot writes notes to return what the magpie took. Bramble points out that one note could mean two people. Bramble is right.

**Longer:**
> By evening the magpie has collected a spoon, a button and one of Bramble's own ribbons. It lines them up along the ribbon gate.
>
> "Borrowed," says Bramble. "It always gives things back. Eventually."
>
> Dot writes the return notes. She hands one to Bramble: *Dot gave the miller the button because she found it.*
>
> Bramble reads it. "Who found it? You, or the miller?"
>
> "Me," says Dot. "Obviously."
>
> "Not obviously. She could be either of you." Bramble taps the word. "Say who."
>
> Dot writes it again: *Dot found the button, so she gave it to the miller.*
>
> "Better," says Bramble. She lifts her ribbon off the gate. "This one is mine, by the way."
>
> The magpie looks at the ribbon, then at Bramble, and says nothing at all.

**Longer:**

>

>

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer; worked-example steps appear one tap at a time. Every card is **A true thing about language**.

**Card 1. Adjective or adverb?**
> An adjective tells about a noun: a quick magpie. An adverb tells about a verb: the magpie hopped quickly. Adverbs can tell about adjectives too: very small.

Worked example: *The tired fox ate slowly.*
1. Tired tells about the fox, a noun. Adjective.
2. Slowly tells how the fox ate. Ate is a verb. Adverb.
3. Many adverbs end in -ly.

**Card 2. After is, seems and tastes**
> After words like is, was, seems, looks and tastes, an adjective often describes the noun: the cake tastes sweet. Tastes links the cake to sweet.

Worked example: *Ember's cake was ___. Delicious or deliciously?*
1. Was links the cake to a describing word.
2. We are describing the cake, a noun.
3. The cake was delicious.

**Card 3. Not every -ly word is an adverb**
> Friendly, lovely and lonely end in -ly, but they describe nouns: a friendly wave. Some adverbs have no -ly at all: fast, hard, soon, well.

Worked example: *The magpie gave a friendly hop and flew off fast.*
1. Friendly tells about the hop. Adjective.
2. Fast tells how it flew. Adverb, with no -ly.

**Card 4. Good and well, careful and carefully**
> Good is an adjective: a good song. Well is usually the adverb: Puff sang well. Looked careful means seemed careful. Looked carefully tells how someone looked.

Worked example: *Dot looked careful. Dot looked carefully.*
1. Looked careful: Dot seemed careful.
2. Looked carefully: Dot studied something closely.
3. One small ending, a different meaning.

**Card 5. Comparing two, or picking one from many**
> Compare two things with -er or more: bigger, more colorful. Pick one from three or more with -est or most: the biggest, the most colorful.

Worked example: *Two buns, then three buns.*
1. Two buns: this one is bigger than that one.
2. Three buns: this one is the biggest.
3. Bigger is a comparative. Biggest is a superlative.

**Card 6. Vowel letters and consonant letters**
> A, e, i, o, u are vowel letters; the rest are consonant letters. Short word ending in one consonant letter after just one vowel letter? Double it: big, bigger.

Worked example: *hot, wide, tiny, new*
1. Hot: one vowel letter, one consonant letter. Hotter.
2. Wide ends in e. Just add r: wider.
3. Tiny: the y turns to i. Tinier.
4. New ends in w. W, x and y are never doubled: newer.

**Card 7. Words with their own forms**
> Some words change completely: good, better, best; bad, worse, worst. Adverbs compare too: fast, faster, fastest. Most -ly adverbs use more and most: more carefully.

Worked example: *Pip's first cake, second cake, third cake.*
1. The first was good.
2. The second was better.
3. The third was the best.

**Card 8. Abstract nouns**
> Some nouns name things you can see or touch: a hat, a spoon. Abstract nouns name ideas, qualities and feelings: patience, kindness, honesty. They are still nouns.

Worked example: *Pip lost his patience.*
1. Can you see patience? No. Touch it? No.
2. Can his or the go before it? Yes.
3. Patience is an abstract noun.

**Card 9. Word families**
> A word family shares a root and a meaning: patient, patiently, patience. Each member does a different job: adjective, adverb, noun.

Worked example: *brave, bravely, bravery*
1. Brave describes someone. Adjective.
2. Bravely tells how. Adverb.
3. Bravery names the quality. Noun.

**Card 10. Noun phrases and the head noun**
> A noun phrase is a group of words built around one noun, the head noun. Determiners like the, a, my and Dot's come first.

Worked example: *Dot's small old hat*
1. What thing is it? A hat. Hat is the head noun.
2. Dot's comes first. It is a determiner.
3. Small and old describe the hat.

**Card 11. Growing a noun phrase**
> Grow a noun phrase with adjectives before the noun, a noun that describes it, like party hat, and a prepositional phrase after it, like with a gold ribbon.

Worked example: *the hat*
1. Add an adjective: the big hat.
2. Add a describing noun: the big party hat.
3. Add a phrase after: the big party hat with a gold ribbon.

**Card 12. The usual order**
> English usually puts opinion first, then size, then age, then color: a lovely small old blue hat. It is a strong habit, not a law. Other orders still make sense.

Worked example: *blue, small, old*
1. Size first: small.
2. Then age: old.
3. Color comes last, just before hat: the small old blue hat.

**Card 13. Prepositional phrases**
> A prepositional phrase is a preposition plus a noun phrase: under the bench. It can say which one, or where, when or how something happened.

Worked example: *The scarf under the bench. Puff slept under the bench.*
1. Which scarf? The one under the bench. It describes a noun.
2. Where did Puff sleep? Under the bench. It tells about the verb.

**Card 14. Adverbials**
> An adverbial does an adverb's job: it tells when, where or how. It can be one word, like soon, a phrase, like after the party, or a whole clause.

Worked example: *After the party, Dot sorted the hats carefully.*
1. When? After the party.
2. How? Carefully.
3. Both are adverbials.

**Card 15. Pronouns point back**
> A pronoun stands in for a noun: Dot found her hat. It points back to a noun already named. If two nouns could fit, the pronoun is unclear.

Worked example: *Pip told the baker that he was hungry.*
1. He could be Pip or the baker.
2. Name the hungry one first: Pip was hungry. He told the baker.
3. Now only one reading fits.

**Card 16. Reflexive pronouns**
> Myself, himself, herself, itself, ourselves and themselves point back to the doer: Pip helped himself to cake. Pip helped him would mean someone else got cake.

Worked example: *Dot warmed ___ by the stove.*
1. Who did the warming? Dot.
2. Who got warm? Dot.
3. Same one, so: Dot warmed herself.

**Card 17. Possessive pronouns**
> My, your, her and their come before a noun: her hat. Mine, yours, hers and theirs stand alone: the hat is hers. Those are possessive pronouns. His does both.

Worked example: *The small new hat is the baker's. It is ___.*
1. Nothing comes after the gap.
2. We need a word that stands alone.
3. It is his.

**Card 18. Clauses and conjunctions**
> A clause has a subject and a verb: the party ended. A subordinate clause leans on a main clause: when the party ended. Conjunctions like when and because join clauses.

Worked example: *When the party ended, Dot sorted the hats.*
1. Dot sorted the hats: a main clause. It can stand alone.
2. When the party ended: a subordinate clause.
3. When is the conjunction that starts it.

**Claim cards (one owner points at a peg; you describe it):**

**Fits counter (after each tile):**
- This fits {n} hats. Which word would rule some out?
- This fits 1 hat. Nobody can take the wrong one.
- This fits no hats. Check each word against the pegs.

**Lovely tile:** Lovely is an opinion. It doesn't rule any hat out.

- Usual order: Small, old, blue. That's the order most speakers use.
- Other order: Everyone can still find the hat. We usually say size, then age, then color.
- Lovely not first: Opinions like lovely usually come first: a lovely small hat.
- Determiner not first: Words like the and Dot's usually go at the very front.

**Kept:** "{Owner} takes {description} home. Dot crosses it off."

**Keeper's Desk, The finished notice:** Dot's notice for the last hat. Tap a word or group of words, then its name.
- Notice: When the party ended, a big old blue hat with a gold ribbon was left on the top peg. Is it yours?

- (Not shown: accept every true name. When the party ended is a subordinate clause and also an adverbial; on and with are both prepositions; a and the are both determiners.)

### (d) Bigger and Biggest (Seedling, Sapling)

**Header:** Market signs compare things. Two things, or three or more?

**Stalls (A story from Willowbank):** the orchard keeper's apples (red) · the baker's buns (big) · Bramble's ribbons (colorful) · two rolls of string (long)

**Compare:** Tap two, or tap three or more. Then choose the sign.
- Two apples: This apple is redder than that one.
- Three apples: This is the reddest apple.
- Two ribbons: This ribbon is more colorful than that one.
- All the ribbons: This is the most colorful ribbon.

**Compare feedback:**
- Two picked, -est chosen: Only two things. Use -er or more.
- Three picked, -er chosen: Three or more things. Use -est or most.
- More with a short word: Short words usually take -er. Long words take more.

**Spelling bench (Sapling):** Add -er to each word: big · hot · wide · tiny · new · good.

**Spelling feedback:**
- Big, hot: One vowel letter, one consonant letter at the end. Double it.
- Wide: It already ends in e. Just add r.
- Tiny: Change the y to i, then add -er.
- New: W is never doubled. Just add -er.
- Good: Good has its own forms: better, best.

**How they moved (adverbs, Sapling):** The magpie flew fast. Pip walked slowly. Pick the sign for each.
- The magpie went ___ than Pip. (faster ✔ · more faster · fastest)
- Pip went ___ than the magpie. (more slowly ✔ · most slowly · slowlier)
- (Not shown: slower is also correct for Pip, so it is never offered as a wrong choice.)

### (e) Where Exactly? (Sapling)

**Header:** Things left on the café floor. Say exactly where each one is.

**Build a phrase:** preposition tiles (under · on · by · behind) plus noun phrase tiles (the bench · the windowsill · the door · the big plant).
- The scarf is ___. (under the bench)
- The cup is ___. (on the windowsill)
- The spoon is ___. (by the door)

- The scarf under the bench is Puff's. (which one)
- Puff slept under the bench. (where it happened)
- The cup on the windowsill is cold. (which one)
- Ember danced on the windowsill. (where it happened)

- Which one: Here the phrase tells which thing. It describes a noun.

### (f) Whose Is It? (Sapling, Keeper's Desk)

**Header:** The magpie borrowed things. Help Dot give them back clearly.

**Who is she?** Tap any pronoun that could point to two people. Then pick the clearer note.
- The miller gave Dot the spoon after she cleaned it. (unclear: she) → The miller cleaned the spoon, then gave it to Dot.
- Bramble told Dot that she had lost her ribbon. (unclear: she, her) → Bramble had lost her ribbon. She told Dot.
- The baker thanked Pip when he found the button. (unclear: he) → When Pip found the button, the baker thanked him.

**Who is she? feedback:**
- Unclear pronoun found: Yes. Two people could be meant. Who is it?
- Clear pronoun tapped: Only one person fits there. That pronoun is clear.

**Mine, yours, hers:** Finish each return note.
- The button is the miller's. It is ___. (hers)
- The spoon is Pip's. It is ___. (his)
- The ribbon is Bramble's. She says it is ___. (hers)
- "These cups are ours," say Dot and Pip. (no gap; read only)

**Himself or him?** Two pictures: Pip helped himself to cake (Pip takes a slice) · Pip helped him to cake (Pip passes a slice to the baker). Tap the sentence for each.

## Problem sets

Every item has the "I'm stuck" ladder. Choices appear in the order written; ✔ marks the right one. Tags in backticks name the misconception a wrong choice tests.

### Set A. Which hat? (Seedling, Sapling, Keeper's Desk)

**A1 (Seedling, guided).** Steps shown first: 1. Look at the six pegs. 2. Count the hats each description fits. 3. Pick the one that fits only Dot's.
- Sentence: Dot's hat hangs on peg 1. The six pegs are shown.
- Prompt: Which description fits only Dot's hat?
- Choices: the small blue hat (`too-little-detail`) · the small old blue hat ✔ · the small old blue hat with a gold ribbon (`detail-that-doesnt-fit`)
- Feedback: Small blue: Two hats are small and blue. One is new. · Gold ribbon: Dot's hat has no ribbon. Now no hat fits.
- Hint: Count the hats each description fits.
- Explanation: Small, old and blue together fit one hat. That's Dot's.

**A2 (Seedling).**
- Sentence: Ember's hat is ___.
- Prompt: Which says the words in the usual order?
- Choices: a new big red hat (`order-any-way`) · a red big new hat (`order-any-way`) · a big new red hat ✔
- Feedback: New big red: Clear, but it sounds odd. We usually say size before age. · Red big new: Clear, but color usually comes last, just before hat.
- Hint: Say each one aloud. Size, then age, then color.
- Explanation: Big is size, new is age, red is color. That's the usual order.

**A3 (Sapling).**
- Phrase: the big old blue hat with a gold ribbon
- Prompt: Which word is the head noun of this phrase?
- Choices: ribbon (`last-noun-is-head`) · blue (`adjective-is-head`) · hat ✔
- Feedback: Ribbon: The ribbon only tells us which hat. The phrase is about a hat. · Blue: Blue is an adjective. It tells about the hat.
- Hint: What thing is the whole phrase about?
- Explanation: Every other word tells about the hat. Hat is the head noun.

**A4 (Sapling).**
- Prompt: In "the party hat," what job does "party" do?
- Choices: It tells what kind of hat ✔ · It is the head noun (`modifier-is-head`) · It tells how something happened (`noun-modifier-is-adverb`)
- Feedback: Head noun: The phrase is about a hat. Party says which kind. · How: Nothing happens here. Party describes the hat.
- Hint: Is it a party, or a hat?
- Explanation: A noun can sit before another noun and describe it: party hat, stone bridge.

**A5 (Sapling).**
- Prompt: Which is the usual order for these words?
- Choices: small my old hat (`determiner-after-adjective`) · small old my hat (`determiner-after-adjective`) · my small old hat ✔
- Feedback: Small my: My is a determiner. Determiners come first. · Small old my: My goes before all the describing words.
- Hint: Words like the, my and Dot's go at the very front.
- Explanation: Determiners such as the, my and Dot's come before any adjectives: my small old hat.

**A6 (Keeper's Desk).**
- Prompt: Which is true about the usual order of adjectives?
- Choices: It is a law, and any other order is always a mistake (`order-is-law`) · It is a strong habit that writers sometimes break ✔ · Order never matters in English at all (`order-never-matters`)
- Feedback: Law: People understand other orders. Writers sometimes break it on purpose. · Never matters: "A red big hat" sounds odd to most speakers. Order matters.
- Hint: Would "a blue small hat" sound odd? Would you still understand it?
- Explanation: The usual order is a strong pattern, not a law. Breaking it sounds odd unless it's done on purpose.

### Set B. Can it go on the shelf? (Seedling)

**B2 (Seedling).**

**B3 (Seedling).**
- Sentence: Pip waited all afternoon. Dot thanked him for his ___.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Noun:** a word that names a person, place, thing or idea.
- **Abstract noun:** a noun for something you can't see or touch, like patience or kindness.
- **Adjective:** a word that tells about a noun, like small or red.
- **Adverb:** a word that tells about a verb or an adjective, like slowly or very.
- **Comparative:** the form that compares two things, like bigger or more colorful.
- **Superlative:** the form that picks one from three or more, like biggest or most colorful.
- **Vowel letter:** a, e, i, o or u.
- **Consonant letter:** any other letter, like b, g or t.
- **Word family:** words that share a root and a meaning, like patient, patiently and patience.
- **Noun phrase:** a group of words built around one noun.
- **Head noun:** the main noun in a noun phrase. The other words tell about it.
- **Determiner:** a word at the front of a noun phrase, like the, a, my or Dot's.
- **Preposition:** a word like under, on, with or after that links a noun to the sentence.
- **Prepositional phrase:** a preposition plus a noun phrase, like under the bench.
- **Adverbial:** a word, phrase or clause that tells when, where or how.
- **Pronoun:** a word that stands in for a noun, like she, it or they.
- **Possessive pronoun:** a pronoun that shows whose and stands alone, like mine, yours or hers.
- **Reflexive pronoun:** a pronoun that points back to the doer, like myself or himself.
- **Clause:** a group of words with a subject and a verb.
- **Subordinate clause:** a clause that leans on a main clause, often starting with when, because or if.
- **Conjunction:** a joining word, like and, when or because.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** describing a thing so exactly that only one fits: adjective or adverb, comparing, abstract nouns, longer noun phrases in the usual order, prepositional phrases, clear pronouns, and the English national curriculum's Year 3 and 4 grammar terms.

**Terms:** this pack uses US spelling and terms. England's curriculum calls a prepositional phrase a "preposition phrase." It treats my, your and her before a noun as determiners and keeps "possessive pronoun" for mine, yours and hers; many American books call my and her possessive pronouns or possessive adjectives. Both are in use.

**What it does not claim:** the adjective order here is a strong habit of English, not a law; grammar books list the full order differently (some put shape before age), and writers break it on purpose. "Two things: -er; three or more: -est" is the school convention; people sometimes say "the best of the two" in speech. Some adverbs without -ly are standard (fast, hard, and slow in "go slow"). Two-syllable adjectives vary: quieter and more quiet are both used. The senses test for abstract nouns is a rule of thumb; some nouns sit on the line. The items practice the forms most used in writing; feedback names what writing usually uses and never calls a spoken or regional form bad. "Dot and myself went" is common in speech; writing usually keeps myself for pointing back (Dot and I went; I helped myself), and nothing here scolds the spoken form. Willowbank and its party are invented.

**Talk and writing:** "Say why" answers are spoken to you and never stored. Nothing the child writes or says is recorded.
