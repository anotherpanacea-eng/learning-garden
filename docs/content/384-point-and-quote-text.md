## Common teaching and copy

**Point and Quote** · **A reader should be able to see what your story words show.**

The librarian wants a short paragraph for someone who has not read the story. Build Dot's paragraph, or choose any of the other drafts.

- A point says what you want to say about a text. A quotation uses the text's exact words. An explanation connects those words to your point.
- Point, quotation, explanation is one helpful pattern. It is the pattern Dot asks for in this draft, not the only good way to write a paragraph.
- A line can be in a story without showing the point you are making. Read the words around it too: who is speaking or acting?
- When a point is about two texts, bring relevant words from both. Our short extracts do not tell you everything about a whole book or character. You may have met some of these extracts before.
- These texts and sentence strips are provided. A person reads your own paragraph; selecting or ordering these words does not assess independent writing.

Separate worked example, not any task's story: A tiny story says, “Nell counted the umbrellas twice before she left.” Point: Nell checks the umbrellas carefully. Quote: “counted the umbrellas twice”. Explanation: Counting again suggests she wants to avoid leaving one behind. This is an interpretation supported by that action, not proof she always behaves that way.

**Tell someone, or write on paper:** Make a point about a story, choose exact words that help show it, and explain the connection. Ask a reader whether the words support your point. You can choose a different interpretation.

**Return to a kept paragraph:** Reopen your kept paragraph. Follow its quotation back to the story, then use or revise the paragraph for someone who has not read it. This is a new purpose for familiar text, not an unfamiliar-reading test.

### quote-build · Build Dot's paragraph · order · default entry

Family quote-goat. Reader: Someone who has not read The Goat in the Well. Prompt: Put Dot's sentences in her requested order: point, quotation, explanation. You can keep any order.

Initial parts, in this exact order:
1. **explain**, role Explanation: This shows she jumped before she knew how to get out.
2. **point**, role Point: The goat doesn't think ahead.
3. **quote**, role Quotation: The story says she "jumps without a second thought."

Fitting key for this requested pattern: point,quote,explain. Only quote has a source link: goat/g2. The point/explanation are Dot's supplied interpretation, not words quoted from the fable.

Hint: Find the point first. "The story says" introduces the words. "This shows" explains what they show.

General non-fit: Dot asked for point, quotation, explanation in this draft. Your sentences are still here to move or keep; this is not a rule for every good paragraph.

### quote-goat · Find the goat's words · choice

Family quote-goat. Reader: A reader checking the point that the goat does not pause to think ahead. Prompt: Which of these lines most directly shows that point? Read the extract too.

Choices, stable order:
- **look:** A thirsty goat looks over the edge. → goat/g2.
- **jump:** The goat jumps without a second thought. → goat/g2. **Fitting key: jump.**
- **fox-out:** The fox hops onto the goat's back, then her horns, and springs out. → goat/g3.

Hint: Look at what the goat does after looking over the edge.

Fit: The words "without a second thought" directly support this point about the goat's action here. It does not prove what she always does.

Different look: Looking over the edge is in the story. By itself it does not show the goat acting without a pause to think.

Different fox-out: That line describes the fox getting out. The requested point is about the goat's earlier action.

General: The selected words stay yours. Compare them with the whole extract; you can change or keep this choice.

### quote-fox · The fox's hundred ways · choice

Family quote-fox. Reader: A reader checking whether the fox chooses in time. Prompt: Which of these lines shows that the fox's many possible escapes do not help him choose in time?

Choices:
- **boast:** "I know a hundred ways to escape," says the fox. → cat/c1.
- **climb:** The cat is up the tree in a flash. → cat/c2.
- **choosing:** The fox is still choosing when the hounds reach him. → cat/c3. **Fitting key: choosing.**

Hint: A boast tells what he claims. Which words show what happens when the hounds arrive?

Fit: The fox is still choosing when the hounds arrive. That supports the point about not choosing in time, rather than just repeating his boast.

Different boast: This is the fox's claim about his options. It does not show whether he chooses in time.

Different climb: This shows the cat acting quickly. The requested point is about the fox's delay.

General: The selected words stay yours. Compare them with the whole extract; you can change or keep this choice.

### quote-mole · Mole's confidence · choice

Family quote-mole. Reader: A reader checking what Mole believes before he takes the oars. Prompt: Which words directly show that Mole thinks he can row as well as Rat?

Choices:
- **as-well:** he could do it every bit as well → mole/m1. **Fitting key: as-well.**
- **sculling:** sculling so strongly and so easily along → mole/m1.
- **laughing:** he was evidently laughing → mole/m4.

Hint: Read who is rowing, whose pride whispers, and who is laughing.

Fit: The words about what his pride whispers show Mole's belief. They do not show that his rowing skill equals Rat's.

Different sculling: In this sentence the strong, easy rowing describes Rat, whom Mole is watching.

Different laughing: This "he" refers to Rat after Rat grips Mole. It does not state Mole's belief before he takes the oars.

General: The selected words stay yours. Compare them with the whole extract; you can change or keep this choice.

### quote-two · Two stories, one point · choice

Family quote-two. Reader: Someone comparing an action in two short extracts. Point: Both extracts describe a jump by a character. Prompt: Which pair supplies words about a jump from BOTH extracts? This point is about these actions, not each character's whole personality.

Choices:
- **descriptions:** "The walls are smooth and wet." + "sculling so strongly and so easily along" → goat/g1 and mole/m1.
- **both-jumps:** "The goat jumps without a second thought." + "He jumped up and seized the sculls" → goat/g2 and mole/m2. **Fitting key: both-jumps.**
- **one-jump:** "The goat jumps without a second thought." + "his pride began to whisper" → goat/g2 and mole/m1.

Hint: Find words about a jump in each extract. One relevant half is not evidence from both.

Fit: Each half names a jump in its own extract. This pair supports that narrow comparison; it does not establish that the stories have the same ending or moral.

Different descriptions: The first half describes the walls; the second describes Rat's rowing. Neither half gives the two jumps requested.

Different one-jump: The first half gives the goat's jump. The second half is about a thought, not Mole's jump.

General: Both source links remain available even when a pair does not fit the stated point. Inspect the words around each quotation.
