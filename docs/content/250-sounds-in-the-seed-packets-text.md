# Sounds in the Seed Packets: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 250](../specs/250-sounds-in-the-seed-packets.md). Use as written, after review. `{guide}` is never gendered. Pronouns: Pip he/him; the orchard keeper and Wren Halloway she/her; Hob Pennick he/him. Sources: [audit](../source-audits/250-sounds-in-the-seed-packets.md). Nothing is scored, timed or locked. No Hollis, no pages and no parcel appear here.

**Brief 55 rule (binding):** text and audio requests only; nothing plays until [brief 55](../specs/55-phonics-content-audio-brief.md)'s review approves real recordings, so every bench runs in **adult-read mode**. Child text names a sound by a key word ("the sound at the start of ship"), never by slashes; slashes appear only in the audio table.

**Labels used throughout:**
- **A story from Willowbank** marks the town, its people and its seed shed (fiction).
- **A true thing about language** marks real facts about English letters and sounds.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. The blank packets (Act I, mend the village)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** The storm got into the orchard keeper's seed shed. Every packet still has its picture. Every name has run.

**Short:**
> The storm washed the names off the seed packets. The pictures are still there. You and Pip help the orchard keeper read them again.

**Longer:**
> The seed shed smells of wet sacks. Packets lie drying on the bench, curled at the corners.
>
> "The pictures held," says the orchard keeper. "The names didn't."
>
> Pip finds a label with one faint word left on it. He sounds out every letter. "Sss. Huh. Ed?"
>
> "Try the first two letters as one sound," says {guide}.
>
> Pip tries again. "Shed!" He looks around. "Why does a seed packet say shed?"
>
> "It doesn't," says the orchard keeper. "That's the shelf label. It came unstuck in the flood."
>
> Pip puts it back on the shelf, very carefully, and wipes his paws on his coat.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> English has more sounds than letters. So some sounds are written with two letters working as a team. Grown-ups call a team like this a digraph. Sh, ch and th are three of them.

### Scene 2. Pip's chips (Act I, mend the village)

**Hook:** One packet shows thin green stalks. Pip hopes very hard that it says chips.

**Short:**
> Pip hopes a packet says chips. It says chives. The e before the s is quiet, but it changes the i.

**Longer:**
> Pip holds up a packet. Thin green stalks on the front, and one word: chives.
>
> "Chips," he says. "Someone grows chips."
>
> "Look near the end," says the orchard keeper.
>
> Pip looks. "There's an e before the s. It doesn't say anything."
>
> "It doesn't," says {guide}. "But it changes the i. Like pin and pine."
>
> "Chives," Pip reads slowly. He sniffs the packet. "Smells like onions."
>
> "They're in the onion family," says the orchard keeper.
>
> Pip puts the packet down. He had really hoped for chips.

### Scene 3. Sow, or sow? (Spring, Flood Boards and Planting)

**Hook:** Planting day. The boards are in the millrace and the packets are dry. Pip reads the first one out loud.

**Short:**
> A packet says sow in spring. Pip reads sow like cow, which means a pig. Hob grins. Here it is sow like snow: plant.

**Longer:**
> Planting day. The flood boards are in, the beds are raked, and the packets sit in a row on the wall.
>
> Pip reads the first one out loud. "Sow in spring." He says sow like cow.
>
> Hob Pennick grins. "My sow is in her pen," he says. "She can stay there."
>
> "A sow is a mother pig," says {guide}. "But this is a seed packet."
>
> "Sow like snow," says the orchard keeper. "It means put seeds in the ground."
>
> Pip reads it again. He looks at the packet, then at the pig pen. "Same letters," he says. "Not fair."
>
> "Try one sound, then the other," says {guide}. "Keep the one that fits."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> The letters ow have two common sounds, as in cow and as in snow. A few words, like sow and row, can be read either way, and each way means something different. The sentence tells you which one to choose.

### Scene 4. Wren's quiet w (Act I, mend the village)

**Hook:** Wren Halloway brings the town's seed list to the shed. Pip reads her name on the top line and stops.

**Short:**
> Pip asks why Wren's name starts with w. Wren says the w is quiet, like the k in knot. Long ago, people said it.

**Longer:**
> Wren Halloway brings the town's seed list. Her name is at the top: Wren.
>
> Pip reads it twice. "Why is there a w? You say Ren."
>
> "The w is quiet," says Wren. "Like the k in knot."
>
> "Then why is it there?"
>
> "Long ago, people said it," says Wren. "The sound went away. The letter stayed."
>
> Pip reads down the list. "Celery." He frowns at the c. "Kel? No. Sel-ery?"
>
> "That's the one," says the orchard keeper.
>
> "English," says Pip, "is full of tricks."
>
> "Not tricks," says Wren. "History. Lots of odd letters have a story."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> In Old English, the w in words like wren and write was said out loud. It went quiet hundreds of years ago, but the spelling kept it. The k in knot has a similar story.

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer; worked-example steps appear one tap at a time. Every card is **A true thing about language**. A grown-up says each key word aloud until reviewed audio exists.

**Card 1. Two letters, one sound**
> Sometimes two letters work as a team and make one sound. The s and h in ship make the sound at the start of ship.

Worked example: *shed.*
1. Find the two letters side by side: s and h.
2. Say them as one sound, the sound at the start of ship.
3. Then the rest: shed.

**Card 2. ch and th**
> The c and h in chip make one sound. The t and h in thin make one sound. In the word this, th sounds a little different. Both are th.

Worked example: *chick and thin.*
1. chick starts with the ch team.
2. thin starts with the th team.
3. Read the team first, then the rest.

**Card 3. ck, tch and ng**
> Some teams sit at the end. ck sounds like the k in kit, as in duck. tch sounds like ch, as in patch. ng ends ring.

Worked example: *patch.*
1. Find the end team: t, c, h.
2. Three letters, one sound, like the start of chip.
3. Read it: patch.

**Card 4. ph, wh and k**
> ph in photo sounds like the f in fin. wh often sounds like the w in wet, as in wheat. Before e or i, you often see k, not c.

Worked example: *photo.*
1. Find the team at the start: p and h.
2. It sounds like the start of fin.
3. Read it: photo.

**Card 5. Vowel teams**
> Two vowels can team up and make one sound. ai in rain and ay in day sound the same. ee in tree is another team.

Worked example: *rain.*
1. Find the vowels side by side: a and i.
2. Read them as one sound, as in day.
3. Read it: rain.

**Card 6. More vowel teams**
> oa in boat and oe in hoe sound the same. oi in coin and oy in boy sound the same.

Worked example: *boat.*
1. Find the team: o and a.
2. One sound, as in hoe.
3. Read it: boat.

**Card 7. Teams with two sounds**
> Some teams have two sounds. oo sounds one way in moon and another in book. ea sounds one way in leaf and another in bread.

Worked example: *thread.*
1. Try ea as in leaf. Does that fit a sewing thread?
2. Try ea as in bread.
3. That's it: thread.

**Card 8. The end e**
> An e at the end is often quiet. Often it changes the vowel before it. cap becomes cape. kit becomes kite.

Worked example: *cape.*
1. Read cap.
2. Now an e sits at the end, after the p.
3. The a changes: cape.

**Card 9. The end e with other vowels**
> The end e works with every vowel. pin, pine. hop, hope. cub, cube. In the word these, it changes an e.

Worked example: *hope.*
1. Read hop.
2. Add the end e.
3. The o changes, as in home: hope.

**Card 10. Not every end e**
> Some words have an end e that changes nothing. give and have keep their short vowels. If one way doesn't make sense, try the other.

Worked example: *give.*
1. Try i as in bike: it doesn't make a word that fits.
2. Try i as in pin.
3. give. That fits.

**Card 11. Two-way teams**
> ow sounds one way in cow and another in snow. ie sounds one way in pie and another in field. Try one, then the other.

Worked example: *a bowl of seeds.*
1. Try ow as in cow. Does that fit?
2. Try ow as in snow.
3. bowl. That fits the seeds.

**Card 12. More teams to know**
> ou in out sounds like ow in cow. ew in grew and ue in blue sound the same. aw in saw and au in haul sound the same.

Worked example: *cloud.*
1. Find the team: o and u.
2. It sounds like the ow in cow.
3. Read it: cloud.

**Card 13. y at the end**
> At the end of a word, y can sound like the y in happy or the y in my. At the start, as in yes, it sounds different again.

Worked example: *sky and sunny.*
1. sky is short. Its y sounds like the y in my.
2. sunny is longer. Its y sounds like the y in happy.
3. Not always, so check that the word fits.

**Card 14. r changes a vowel**
> When r comes after a vowel, the vowel changes. cat and cart sound different. ar is in car, or is in fork, ore is in more.

Worked example: *barn.*
1. Find the vowel with r after it: a and r.
2. Read them together, as in car.
3. Read it: barn.

**Card 15. Three spellings, one sound**
> er in her, ir in bird and ur in turn all make the same sound.

Worked example: *fern, birch and turnip.*
1. Find the vowel and r in each.
2. er, ir, ur: one sound.
3. Read them: fern, birch, turnip.

**Card 16. Teams of three**
> igh makes one sound, as in night. air in chair, ear in hear and are in care are teams with r.

Worked example: *light.*
1. Find i, g, h together.
2. One sound, as in night.
3. Read it: light.

**Card 17. Soft c and soft g**
> Before e, i or y, c often sounds like the s in sun, as in city. g often sounds like the j in jam, as in giant.

Worked example: *celery.*
1. Look after the c: an e.
2. Try c as in sun.
3. celery. It fits the packet.

**Card 18. Quiet letters and other spellings**
> In knot and gnat, the k and g are quiet. In wren, the w is quiet. dge in badge sounds like j. The s in treasure is buzzy.

Worked example: *knot.*
1. Find k and n together at the start.
2. The k is quiet.
3. Read it: knot, like the one in a string.

**Card 19. Try one sound, then the other**
> Some letters can make more than one sound. Try the usual one. If it doesn't make a word that fits, try the other.

Worked example: *giant sunflower.*
1. Try g as in gum. That doesn't make a word that fits.
2. Try g as in jam.
3. giant. A very big sunflower.

## Benches

### (a) Two Letters, One Sound (Sprout, Seedling)

**Header:** Each packet has a letter team. Find it, then read the name.

**Find the team:** Each packet names one place in its word, the start or the end. The child taps two or three letters.
- Prompt on a start packet: Tap the letters that make the sound at the start.
- Prompt on an end packet: Tap the letters that make the sound at the end.
- First slice, the start: shed (sh) · ship (sh) · chip (ch) · chick (ch) · chest (ch) · thin (th) · this (th)
- First slice, the end: fish (sh) · bath (th)
- Later, the start: photo (ph) · wheat (wh)
- Later, the end: duck (ck) · ring (ng) · patch (tch, three letters)
- Each word has exactly one fitting team at its named place. chick asks only about the start.
- Feedback, wrong letters: Say the word slowly. Which letters make that one sound?
- Feedback, c and h tapped in patch: Close. Here t, c and h make one sound together.

**Name the packet:** Tap the name that goes with this picture. Uses the cards and feedback of items A2 and A3; no other card sets are written yet.

**Fix the packet:** The team washed off. Pick the team that finishes the word.
- shed: tiles sh · ch · th. Fitting tile: sh.
- fish: tiles th · sh · ch. Fitting tile: sh.
- chest: tiles sh · th · ch. Fitting tile: ch.
- bath: tiles ch · th · sh. Fitting tile: th.
- Feedback for bash on the bath packet: That makes bash. The picture shows a bird bath.
- Any other wrong tile: Say the picture's name slowly. Which team do you hear?

**Recap card:** sh, ch and th: three letter teams. Listen to the sounds they can make.

### (b) Vowel Teams (Seedling)

**Header:** Two vowels side by side often make one sound. Read the team as one.

**Team trays:** Put each packet on its team's tray: rain · day · tree · leaf · boat · hoe · coin · boy.

**Two-way shelf:** Is the oo like moon or like book? moon · spoon · zoo · book · wood · hook.

**Two-way shelf:** Is the ea like leaf or like bread? leaf · bean · pea · bread · thread · spread.

**Shelf feedback:** Try it the other way. Which one makes a word you know?

### (c) The End e (Seedling)

**Header:** Slide the e onto the end. Read the new word.

**Pairs:** cap, cape · kit, kite · pin, pine · cub, cube · hop, hope · mat, mate.

**Each pair, after the slide:** The e is quiet. It changed the vowel.

**Odd-ones shelf:** give · have. These end e's change nothing. Read them with a short vowel.

### (d) Two-Way Teams (Sapling)

**Header:** Some teams sound two ways. Try one, then the other.

**ow trays:** Like cow or like snow? cow · town · owl · brown · snow · grow · bowl · slow.

**Sentence packets:** Read each one. Which ow fits?
- Sow in spring. Fits: as in snow.
- A row of beans. Fits: as in snow.
- The cow is brown. Fits: as in cow, both times.

**ie tray:** Like pie or like field? pie · tie · cried · field · chief · thief.

**y tray:** Like happy or like my? happy · sunny · poppy · my · fly · sky.

**Same-sound pairs:** Match each pair: out, cow · grew, blue · saw, haul.

### (e) Teams with r, Teams of Three (Seedling, Sapling)

**Header:** When r follows a vowel, read them together.

**r packets:** car · barn · chard · corn · fork · more · store.

**One sound tray:** fern · birch · turnip. Three spellings, one sound.

**Teams of three:** night · light · high · chair · pair · hear · year · care · share.

**ear shelf:** Like hear or like bear? hear · near · year · bear · pear · wear.

### (f) The Odd Packets (Sapling)

**Header:** These packets use other spellings for sounds you know.

**Soft c and g:** city · celery · giant · ginger · cage · sage.

**Honest shelf (always shown):** get · give · girl. Here g keeps the sound in gum, even before e or i. That's why we say "often".

**Quiet letters:** knot · knife · gnat · gnaw · wren · write.

**Other spellings:** badge · hedge · treasure · measure.

**Try-both card:** Try the usual sound. If it doesn't make a word that fits, try the other.

## Problem sets

Every item has the "I'm stuck" ladder. Choices appear in the order written and are shown, not read aloud; the grown-up reads only the prompt. ✔ marks the fitting choice. Tags in backticks name the misconception a wrong choice tests.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Letter team:** two or three letters that make one sound together, like sh or igh.
- **Vowel:** the letters a, e, i, o and u. Sometimes y works like one.
- **Consonant:** any letter that isn't a vowel, like b, s or t.
- **Short vowel:** the vowel sound in cat, shed, pin, top or cub.
- **End e:** an e at the end of a word that is quiet and often changes the vowel before it.
- **Team with r:** a vowel team and an r read together, like air in chair.
- **Two-way team:** a letter team with two common sounds, like ow in cow and snow.
- **Quiet letter:** a letter you don't hear, like the k in knot.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** reading consonant and vowel digraphs, split digraphs, two-way vowel teams and final y, r-controlled vowels, igh with air, ear and are, and other spellings for known sounds (soft c and g, dge, kn, gn, wr, s in treasure). UK schools say "split digraph"; many US programs say "silent e" or "VCe". This page says "end e".

**How sounds are shown:** until reviewed recordings exist, you are the audio. Please say the sound, not the letter name: for ch, say the sound at the start of chip, not "see aitch". Sounds like p, t, k, b, d, g and ch are short; say them crisply without adding "uh". Read the prompt aloud but not the answer choices, so your child does the reading. For ages 4 to 6, sit together, read aloud, and help freely. Help is never counted against anyone.

**The reading model:** the key words assume a General American reading. Your own accent is fine, and so is your child's. Where accents differ:
- **r after a vowel.** General American says the r in car, fork and bird. Many speakers in England, Australia and parts of the US Northeast and South do not; the vowel still changes, so ar, or, er, ir and ur still read as one chunk.
- **air, ear and are.** In English school phonics these are taught as three letters for one sound. In General American the r is still said, so this page calls them teams with r.
- **aw and au.** Many Americans say saw and haul with the same vowel as top; others keep them different. No item asks your child to tell those apart.
- **wh.** Most Americans say wheat like "weet". Some say a breathy "hweet". Both are fine.
- **u-e, ew and ue.** After j, r and l, almost everyone says "oo" (June, rule, blue). After t, d and n, General American usually says "oo" too (tube, new), where much British English says "yoo". After c, f, m and p, both say "yoo" (cube, few). The key words here don't depend on this difference.
- **bath, path.** General American has the vowel of cat; much of southern England has the vowel of car.

**Letter names and the end e:** many programs say the end e "makes the vowel say its name". That works for most words, but this page keeps letter names separate from sounds, so it uses key words (cake, bike, home, cube) instead. The odd-ones shelf shows give and have, where the e changes nothing.

**What it does not claim:** this is practice, not a reading level or test. Choosing a word under a picture is not the same as reading it alone. Brief 55's twelve short-vowel words come first in the Word Workshop plan; this page builds beyond them. Spelling these sounds in writing is pack 263's job; reading longer words is pack 252's.
