# Parts of Parts: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 198](../specs/198-parts-of-parts.md). Use as written, after independent spec review and a math check. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion and is never gendered; `{courier}` is the explorer's courier. Pip is he/him, Dot she/her, Bramble she/her (with her magpie), the baker he/him, the orchard keeper she/her, Nuria she/her. Real-world facts are in the [source audit](../source-audits/198-parts-of-parts.md); items still marked `[check]` stay off screen until cleared. Nothing here is scored or timed.

## Labels used on every card

- **A story from Willowbank**: the town, the café, the orchard, the traders and every character are made up.
- **A true thing about math**: checked math, explained simply.
- **A true thing from history**: something people really did or wrote.
- **A made-up model**: a simple game rule, shown on the card, that is not real measurement.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. Supper for the traders (Act III, chapter 1: the floating market)

**Hook:** Vadoalto's traders stay for supper. The baker's card makes enough for 4. There are more mouths than that.

**Short:**
> The traders stay for supper. "One batch feeds four," says the baker. "I need one and a half batches." Bramble says multiplying always makes more.

**Longer:**
> The boats from Vadoalto are tied up at the landing, and the traders are hungry. The baker pins his oat-cake card to the bench. "One batch feeds four," he says. "Tonight there are six. So, one and a half batches."
>
> "Easy," says Bramble. "Multiplying makes things bigger. Everyone knows that."
>
> "This time it does," says the baker. He measures 2 1/3 cups of flour, then half as much again.
>
> Pip arrives with an empty bowl. "Can I have a small batch? Just for me? Half a batch?"
>
> The baker turns the dial on the card to ½. Every amount on the card shrinks.
>
> Bramble stares. "You multiplied. And it got smaller."
>
> "Times a half," says {guide}. "That's half as much."
>
> Bramble's magpie steals an oat cake. Nobody writes that on the card.

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> Times a number bigger than 1 gives more. Times 1 keeps it the same. Times a number less than 1 gives less. The card's 2 1/3 cups of flour times 1 1/2 is 3 1/2 cups. Times 1/2 it is 1 1/6 cups.

### Scene 2. Half of the three strips (Act III, chapter 2: the seed exchange)

**Hook:** Vadoalto wants apple seedlings. The orchard keeper gives them part of her field to grow them in.

**Short:**
> "Three of my four strips are orchard," says the orchard keeper. "Half of that is for Vadoalto's seedlings." Pip asks, "So, half the field?"

**Longer:**
> The orchard keeper's square field is cut into four strips. Three strips hold the orchard. The last one is the low corner, which is often wet.
>
> "Vadoalto's orchard was flattened in the storm," she says. "They need seedlings. I'll grow them in half of my orchard."
>
> "Half the field," says Pip, helpfully.
>
> "Half of the orchard," says the orchard keeper. "The orchard is three-quarters of the field."
>
> Dot flies up and looks down. "From up here," she says, "you'd cut the orchard in half the other way. Then count the little pieces."
>
> The field now has eight pieces. Three of them are seedling beds.
>
> "Three-eighths," says the orchard keeper. "Less than half. Still a lot of digging."
>
> Pip offers to supervise.

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> Half of three-quarters is 1/2 × 3/4. Cutting both ways makes 2 × 4 = 8 pieces, and 1 × 3 = 3 are shaded both ways: 3/8. A part of a part is smaller than either part.

### Scene 3. Packs for the high ford (Act III, chapter 3: the route by the high ford)

**Hook:** {courier} needs oat packs for the walk to Vadoalto. The baker has 3 cups of oats and a quarter-cup scoop.

**Short:**
> "Three cups of oats," says the baker, "a quarter cup in each pack. How many packs?" Bramble says dividing makes it smaller. She'll need to count.

**Longer:**
> {courier} is walking to Vadoalto by the high ford, and the baker is packing oats. He has 3 cups of oats and a quarter-cup scoop.
>
> "Three divided by a quarter," says Bramble. "Dividing makes things smaller. So, less than three packs."
>
> "Let's scoop and see," says {guide}.
>
> One cup fills four scoops. Two cups, eight. Three cups, twelve.
>
> "Twelve packs," says the baker. "That's a lot of oats for one courier."
>
> "Some are for Hob at the ford," says {courier}. "He always makes tea."
>
> Then the baker cuts a third of a loaf and shares it between two travelers. Each piece is smaller than the third.
>
> "So sometimes dividing makes it smaller," says Bramble slowly, "and sometimes the answer is bigger."
>
> "It depends on the question," says {guide}. "Sharing out, or how many fit?"

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> 3 ÷ 1/4 asks how many quarter cups fit in 3 cups: 12. Check: 12 × 1/4 = 3. 1/3 ÷ 2 shares a third between two: each gets 1/6. Check: 1/6 × 2 = 1/3.

### Scene 4. Feeding the show (Act V, path 4: a travelling show)

**Hook:** The travelling show goes upstream by boat. Six players need supper on the way, and the order slip must be right.

**Short:**
> Six players, one boat, one order slip. The card feeds four. "Write it so I can't mix it up," says the baker.

**Longer:**
> The travelling show is going to Vadoalto by boat, and six players will need supper on the way. The baker hands you a blank order slip.
>
> "The card feeds four," he says. "Write me the oats for six. And write what the number means. Last time someone wrote 'one and a bit' and I made porridge for a bear."
>
> You draw the card's 3/4 cup of oats as a bar. Six is one and a half times four. You draw one bar and half a bar more.
>
> 3/4 × 1 1/2 = 9/8. One and an eighth.
>
> "One and an eighth what?" says the baker.
>
> "Cups of oats. For six players."
>
> "Now that," says the baker, "I can bake."

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> A number is not finished until it says what it counts. 1 1/8 alone could be cups, cakes or players. "1 1/8 cups of oats for six" is an order the baker can use.

## Teach cards

Each card is under 30 words. Worked steps appear one tap at a time.

**T1. Lots of a part** (A true thing about math)
> 2/5 is two of the fifths. Three lots of 2/5 is six fifths. The part stays a fifth. Only the count grows.
Worked example: 1. 2/5 + 2/5 + 2/5. 2. 2 + 2 + 2 = 6 fifths. 3. 3 × 2/5 = 6/5.

**T2. Past one whole** (A true thing about math)
> When the top passes the bottom, you have passed one whole. Take out the wholes. What's left is the fraction.
Worked example: 1. 4 × 3/8 = 12/8. 2. 8/8 is one whole. 3. 4/8 left: 1 4/8, which is 1 1/2.

**T3. Copy every part** (A true thing about math)
> Three lots of 2 1/3 is three 2s and three thirds. Copy the whole number and the fraction.
Worked example: 1. Three 2s: 6. 2. Three thirds: 3/3 = 1. 3. 6 + 1 = 7.

**T4. A part of a part** (A true thing about math)
> To find 1/2 of 3/4, shade 3/4 of the field one way. Then shade half of that the other way. Count the double-shaded pieces.
Worked example: 1. Four strips, three shaded. 2. Cut across in half. 3. Eight pieces, three double-shaded: 3/8.

**T5. Tops times tops, bottoms times bottoms** (A true thing about math)
> Multiply the tops. Multiply the bottoms. The bottoms multiply because the field is cut both ways.
Worked example: 1. 2/3 × 4/5. 2. Tops: 2 × 4 = 8. 3. Bottoms: 3 × 5 = 15 pieces. 4. 8/15.

**T6. Times is different from plus** (A true thing about math)
> Adding fractions needs matching bottoms. Multiplying does not. When you multiply two fractions, the bottom changes too.
Worked example: 1. 2/3 of 1/3. 2. Cut a third into three. 3. Take two: 2/9 of the whole.

**T7. Smaller numbers, same amount** (A true thing about math)
> If the top and bottom share a factor, divide both by it. The amount stays the same.
Worked example: 1. 3/4 × 2/3 = 6/12. 2. 6 and 12 share 6. 3. 6/12 = 1/2.

**T8. Bigger, same or smaller?** (A true thing about math)
> Times more than 1: bigger. Times exactly 1: the same. Times less than 1: smaller. Multiplying can make things smaller.
Worked example: 1. Flour: 2 1/3 cups. 2. Times 1 1/2: more. 3. Times 1/2: less. 4. Times 4/4 is times 1: the same.

**T9. "Of" means times** (A true thing about math)
> 3/4 of 200 means 3/4 × 200. Find one quarter. Then take three of them.
Worked example: 1. 200 ÷ 4 = 50. 2. 3 × 50 = 150. 3. 3/4 of 200 is 150.

**T10. Percent is a fraction too** (A true thing about math)
> Percent means out of 100. 25% is 25/100, which is 1/4. So 25% of 12 is 1/4 of 12.
Worked example: 1. 25% = 1/4. 2. 12 ÷ 4 = 3. 3. 25% of 12 is 3.

**T11. Mixed times mixed** (A true thing about math)
> Change mixed numbers to top-heavy fractions first. Or draw the rectangle and count all four pieces.
Worked example: 1. 2 1/3 = 7/3 and 1 1/2 = 3/2. 2. 7/3 × 3/2 = 21/6. 3. 21/6 = 3 1/2.

**T12. Sharing a part** (A true thing about math)
> 1/3 shared by 2: cut the third in two. Cut every third the same way. Count the pieces in the whole.
Worked example: 1. 3 thirds, each cut in 2. 2. 3 × 2 = 6 pieces. 3. Each share is 1/6. Check: 1/6 × 2 = 1/3.

**T13. How many fit?** (A true thing about math)
> 3 ÷ 1/4 asks how many quarter cups fit in 3 cups. Each cup holds four. So twelve.
Worked example: 1. 1 cup: 4 scoops. 2. 3 cups: 3 × 4 = 12. 3. Check: 12 × 1/4 = 3.

**T14. Share or scoop?** (A true thing about math)
> Sharing an amount out gives smaller pieces. Counting how many small scoops fit gives a bigger count. Ask which question it is.
Worked example: 1. "1/2 cup shared by 3": sharing. 2. "How many 1/3 cups in 2 cups": scooping. 3. 1/6 cup; 6 scoops.

**T15. Match the bottoms, then count** (A true thing about math)
> To divide fractions, give them the same bottom. Then ask how many of one fit in the other.
Worked example: 1. 3/4 ÷ 3/8. 2. 3/4 = 6/8. 3. How many 3 eighths in 6 eighths? 2.

**T16. Why flip the second fraction?** (A true thing about math)
> One whole holds 8/3 lots of 3/8. So dividing by 3/8 is the same as multiplying by 8/3. Keep the first. Flip only the second.
Worked example: 1. 8 eighths ÷ 3 eighths = 8/3. 2. 3/4 ÷ 3/8 = 3/4 × 8/3. 3. 24/12 = 2.

**T17. Say what the number means** (A true thing about math)
> Draw the story. Work it out. Then say the number with its unit and what it counts.
Worked example: 1. Oats for 6: 3/4 × 1 1/2. 2. 9/8 = 1 1/8. 3. "1 1/8 cups of oats for six."

## Real-world and history cards

**H1. Loaves shared in Egypt** (A true thing from history; Seedling and up)
> An Egyptian scroll, the Rhind papyrus, is about 3,500 years old. Its first problems share loaves of bread among 10 people.
Keeper's note:
> A scribe named Ahmose copied the scroll around 1550 BCE. Sharing 1 loaf among 10 gives each person 1/10. For 9 loaves, the scribe wrote each share as 2/3 + 1/5 + 1/30. That is still 9/10 of a loaf. Egyptian scribes wrote most fractions as sums of different unit fractions.

**H2. A book that starts with fields** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> In China, about 2,000 years ago, a book called The Nine Chapters gathered math problems. Its first chapter is about fields. It works with fractions.
Keeper's note:
> The first chapter shows how to find a field's area from its width and length. It also gives ways to work with fractions. A later scholar, Liu Hui, wrote notes in the 200s explaining why the methods work.

**H3. Flip the divisor** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> Around the year 850, the Indian mathematician Mahavira wrote a rule: to divide by a fraction, swap its top and bottom, then multiply.
Keeper's note:
> His book is called the Ganita Sara Sangraha. He gave a second way too. For 3/4 divided by 3/8, multiply each top by the other fraction's bottom: 3 × 8 = 24 and 3 × 4 = 12. Then 24 divided by 12 is 2, the same answer as T15 and T16.

## Bench (a): The Cup Line

## Bench (e): The Fit Board

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> Two bars on one ruler. Lay the short bar along the long one and count.

**Opening:** How many of these fit in that? Match the bottoms first.
**Buttons:** Match the bottoms · Lay it along · Show the leftover · Flip and multiply

- Step 2: How many 3 eighths in 6 eighths? *(2.)*

## Key terms (glossary)

- **fraction**: a number for equal parts of a whole, like 3/4.
- **numerator**: the top number; how many parts.
- **denominator**: the bottom number; what size the parts are.
- **unit fraction**: one part, like 1/3 or 1/8.
- **mixed number**: a whole number and a fraction, like 2 1/3.
- **top-heavy fraction (improper fraction)**: a fraction with the top bigger than the bottom, like 7/3.
- **factor**: a number that goes into another with nothing left over, like 3 into 12.
- **simplest form**: the same fraction with the smallest numbers.
- **scale**: make every amount bigger or smaller by the same multiplier.
- **of**: in math, "3/4 of 200" means 3/4 × 200.
- **percent**: out of 100; 25% is 25/100.
- **share out**: cut an amount into equal shares.
- **how many fit**: count how many of one amount fit in another.
- **reciprocal**: a fraction flipped over; the reciprocal of 3/8 is 8/3.
- **divisor**: the number you divide by.
- **unit**: what a number counts or measures, like cups or meters.
- **scribe**: a person whose job was writing and copying.
