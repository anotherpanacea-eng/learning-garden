# The Cold Shelf: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 153](../specs/153-cold-shelf.md). Use as written after review, a subject check and (book 6) review by Inuit and Sámi readers. Pip he/him, Bramble she/her, the librarian they/them; `{guide}` is never gendered. Nothing is scored.

## Labels

- **A story from Willowbank** (every scene and character line; the town and its people are made up)
- **A true thing about the poles** (science cards)
- **A true thing about people today** (Inuit and Sámi cards)
- **A true thing from history** (the race, Shackleton, the Treaty)
- **A simplified picture** (Which pole?, the Sun dial, the chain board)
- **A made-up model** (the keep-warm bench, the made-up chart in book 9)

## Arriving

**Place title:** The Cold Shelf

**First visit:**
> A shelf by the library stove holds nine thin books, a globe and a small table. A card on the shelf says: *The coldest places on Earth. Read by the warm.*

**Index button:** All the books
**Bench buttons:** Which pole? · The Sun dial · The keep-warm bench · The chain board

## The story scene (Lantern Night, winter)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** The first snow falls on Willowbank. The librarian makes a new shelf by the stove, for books about the coldest places on Earth.

**Short:**
> Snow falls. The librarian moves the cold-places books by the stove. Pip asks if lanterns anywhere stay lit all day. Bramble thinks polar bears and penguins are neighbors.

**Longer:**
> The first snow comes down on Lantern Night. In the library, the librarian carries an armful of thin books to the shelf beside the stove.
>
> "The Cold Shelf," they say. "Books about the coldest places on Earth. Best read somewhere warm."
>
> Pip warms his paws. "We light lanterns because the nights are long," he says. "Is there anywhere the night just keeps going? Do they leave the lanterns on all day?"
>
> "There's a book for that," says the librarian, and taps the second one.
>
> Bramble arrives with snow on her hat and a picture book under her arm. On the cover, a polar bear and a penguin share one ice floe. "Neighbors," she says. "Obviously."
>
> {guide} looks at the globe, then at the cover. "Top and bottom," {guide} says slowly. "I'm not sure they've ever met."
>
> "Then we'll find out," says the librarian. "That's what the shelf is for."

**Keeper's note:**
> Willowbank and its library are made up. The poles are real, and so are places like Tromsø in Norway, where people live today. Bramble's cover is made up too: in the wild, polar bears live in the far north and penguins in the south, with warm oceans between.

**After book 1 (Bramble):** "Fine. They live at opposite ends of the world. It's still a nice cover."
**After book 2 (Pip):** "Months without a sunrise. I'd want a lot of lanterns. And snacks."

## Book 1. Top and bottom: the Arctic and the Antarctic

**Globe card (A true thing about the poles):** Earth has a North Pole at the top and a South Pole at the bottom. The places around them are some of the coldest on Earth.

**Worked example (tap to reveal):** Find the poles on the globe.
1. Put a finger on the very top. That is the North Pole.
2. Turn the globe over. The very bottom is the South Pole.
3. They are as far from the equator, Earth's middle line, as you can go.

**Teach:** The Arctic is an ocean with land around it. The Antarctic is land with ocean around it.

**Worked example:**
1. Look down on the Arctic. Blue ocean in the middle, land all around.
2. Look down on the Antarctic. White land in the middle, ocean all around.
3. So the North Pole is on sea ice. The South Pole is on land.

**Teach 2:** Antarctica's ice sits on rock, about 2 kilometers thick on average. High and icy, Antarctica is the colder pole.

**Teach 3:** Polar bears live in the Arctic. Wild penguins live in the south. Warm oceans lie between them.

**Contrast card:** People have lived in the Arctic for thousands of years. Nobody lives in Antarctica all the time. Scientists come and go.

**Guided (Sprout):** Which pole has land in the middle?
Steps shown: 1. Look at the two globes. 2. Find the white land. 3. Is it top or bottom?
Answer: the Antarctic, at the bottom.

### Which pole? text

**Instruction:** Pick a card. Then tap where it belongs.
**Buttons:** Arctic · Antarctic · Both
**Why? chips:** It lives on sea ice · It lives on land by the sea · People live here · Scientists visit here · It's in the south · It's in the north
**Card captions:** Polar bear · Emperor penguin · Arctic fox · Adélie penguin · Walrus · Narwhal · Leopard seal · An Inuit town · Sámi reindeer herders · A research station · Ice on rock · Ice on water
**Read-back for a penguin placed in the Arctic:** This puts penguins in the Arctic. Check the penguin card.
**Research station:** Both is right. Scientists work near both poles.
**Model line:** A simplified picture: real coastlines are more complicated, and some Arctic land has thick ice on rock.

## Book 2. The Sun that stays up: midnight sun and polar night

**Teach (Sprout):** Near the poles, summer has days when the Sun never sets. Winter has days when it never rises.

**Worked example:**
1. In Willowbank, every day has a sunrise and a sunset.
2. Near the North Pole in summer, the Sun circles the sky all night.
3. In winter there, the Sun stays below the horizon all day.

**Teach 2:** The Sun is never switched off. In polar night, it is shining on the other half of Earth.

**Teach 3:** Midnight sun is still cold sun. It stays low, so its light spreads thin.

**Teach 4:** Polar night is not pitch black. Stars, the Moon and sometimes the aurora light the sky.

**Teach (Sapling, the lean):** Earth's axis leans. Each pole leans toward the Sun for half the year, then away.

**Worked example (Sapling):**
1. Earth's axis always points the same way in space.
2. In June, the north half leans toward the Sun. The Arctic turns but stays in the light.
3. In December, the north half leans away. The Arctic stays in the dark.

**Guided (Seedling):** At the North Pole in June, does the Sun set?
Steps shown: 1. June is summer in the north. 2. The north leans toward the Sun. 3. So does it set?
Answer: no; the Sun stays up all day and night.

### Sun dial text

**Instruction:** Pick a place and a month. Will the Sun set today?
**Prediction buttons:** It will set · It won't set · Not sure
**Play button:** Play a day
**Places:** Willowbank's valley · Tromsø, Norway · The North Pole · The South Pole
**Results:** The Sun set and rose today. · The Sun stayed up all day and night: midnight sun. · The Sun stayed below the horizon all day: polar night.
**After a mismatch:** Not this time. Try the same month at another place.
**Sapling button:** Show the lean
**Model line:** A simplified picture: one day in each month. Real dates shift by a day or so with weather, hills and the year.

## Book 3. Keeping warm: adaptations

**Teach:** Polar animals have features that help them keep their body heat. These are called adaptations.

**Teach 2 (wrap up):** Fur, feathers and blubber slow down heat leaving the body. They don't make heat.

**Worked example (the keep-warm bench):**
1. Three jars of warm water sit in ice.
2. One has no wrap, one a thin fat layer, one a thick fat layer.
3. After 10 minutes, the thick-layer jar is warmest. The fat slowed the heat leaving.

**Animal gallery (A true thing about the poles):**
- **Polar bear:** thick fur, a thick fat layer, and black skin under see-through hairs that look white.
- **Seal:** a thick layer of blubber under its skin.
- **Emperor penguin:** fathers hold the egg on their feet and huddle together in the winter dark.
- **Arctic fox:** small ears, short legs, furred paws, and a coat that turns white in winter.

**Contrast card:** Pip is a red fox. His ears are big. An Arctic fox's ears are small and round.

**Teach 3 (huddle):** Emperor penguins huddle. They shuffle round, so every penguin gets turns in the warm middle.

**Teach 4:** Adaptations grow over many generations. An animal can't grow blubber because it wants to.

**Guided (Seedling):** A seal swims in icy water. What helps it stay warm?
Steps shown: 1. Look at the seal card. 2. Find what is under its skin. 3. Name it.
Answer: blubber, a thick layer of fat.

**Keeper's note (counter-current):** In an Arctic fox's legs, warm blood going down passes close to cold blood coming up and warms it. The paws stay cold, and the body keeps its heat.

### Keep-warm bench text

**Instruction:** Wrap each jar. Which will stay warm longest?
**Wrap buttons:** No layer · Thin fat layer · Thick fat layer
**Prediction:** Which jar will be warmest after 10 minutes?
**Button:** Wait 10 minutes
**Fair test card:** Same: start temperature, amount of water, ice bath, jar. Different: only the layer.
**Ice contrast:** Now wrap a jar of ice in a warm room. Which stays frozen longer?
**Ice result:** The wrapped ice stayed frozen longer. The fat didn't make cold. It slowed heat moving in.
**Model line:** A made-up model with made-up numbers. It shows that a thicker layer slows heat loss.

## Book 4. Who eats whom: polar food chains

**Legend (on every screen):** Arrow: food → eater. Food supplies energy to the eater.

**Teach:** Every polar food chain starts with tiny living things that make food from sunlight.

**Worked example (Antarctic):**
1. Phytoplankton, too small to see, make food from sunlight.
2. Krill eat phytoplankton. Penguins eat krill.
3. Leopard seals eat penguins. Phytoplankton → krill → penguin → leopard seal.

**Teach 2 (Arctic):** Under the sea ice, ice algae grow. Tiny animals eat them, fish eat those, seals eat fish, polar bears eat seals.

**Teach 3:** Krill are small, but almost two hundred kinds of animals eat them.

**Guided (Seedling):** Draw the first arrow in the Antarctic chain.
Steps shown: 1. Find the food that starts it. 2. Find what eats it. 3. Tap food, then eater.
Answer: phytoplankton → krill.

### Chain board text

**Instruction:** Tap the food, then the eater.
**Read it aloud (Antarctic):** Phytoplankton is food for krill. Krill is food for the Adélie penguin. The Adélie penguin is food for the leopard seal.
**Read-back for a reversed arrow:** This says the penguin is food for the krill. Is that right?
**What if button:** What if krill were scarce?
**What if prompt:** Follow the arrows up. Who may have less food?
**Model line:** A simplified picture: real animals eat more than one thing.

## Book 5. Two kinds of ice

**Teach:** Sea ice is frozen sea water. It forms on the ocean, from salty water.

**Teach 2:** A glacier is ice made from snow, pressed down over many years, on land.

**Worked example:**
1. Snow falls and stays, year after year.
2. The snow underneath is pressed into hard ice.
3. When there's enough, it slowly flows downhill: a glacier.

**Teach 3:** An ice sheet is a glacier so big it covers land. Antarctica and Greenland have them.

**Teach 4:** Icebergs break off glaciers. They float, with about nine-tenths of the ice under water.

**Teach 5 (Sapling, ice cores):** Snow falls in layers. Deeper ice is older ice. Its bubbles hold old air.

**Real core card (A true thing about the poles):** One ice core from Antarctica, drilled all the way to the rock, holds a record of 800,000 years.

**Pyrenees card:** The mountains around Willowbank's valley are real mountains, the Pyrenees. They still have a few small glaciers. The biggest, Aneto, is shrinking.

**U-valley card (Sapling):** In the Ice Age, a glacier carved the Ordesa valley, in the Spanish Pyrenees, into a U shape.

**Guided (Seedling):** This ice formed on the sea. Is it sea ice or glacier ice?
Steps shown: 1. Where did it form? 2. Sea ice forms on the sea. 3. So it is...
Answer: sea ice.

## Book 6. People of the North: the Inuit and the Sámi

**Teach (A true thing about people today):** About 180,000 Inuit live in the Arctic: Alaska, Canada, Greenland and a corner of Russia.

**Teach 2:** Inuit families know the sea ice, the weather and the animals in great detail. That knowledge is passed down and tested.

**Teach 3:** An inuksuk is a stone landmark Inuit build. It can show the way, or mark a good hunting place.

**Teach 4:** In Inuit languages, iglu means any house. Snow houses are mostly shelters for winter hunting trips.

**Teach 5:** The Sámi live in Sápmi: the north of Norway, Sweden and Finland, and part of Russia.

**Teach 6:** Some Sámi families herd reindeer, about one in ten. Other Sámi do every kind of job.

**Teach 7:** Joik is a Sámi way of singing. A joik can call to mind a person, an animal or a place.

**Teach 8:** Sámi National Day is 6 February. In 1917, Sámi from Norway and Sweden first met together that day.

**Worked example (then and now):**
1. A traditional tool: the qajaq, a kayak for hunting on water. English took the word "kayak" from it.
2. A tool today: a snowmobile, a phone, a weather forecast.
3. People use both, with knowledge their families have kept.

**Contrast card:** The Inuit and the Sámi are not people of the past. They live in the North today.

**Guided (Seedling):** What is an inuksuk for?
Steps shown: 1. Read the inuksuk card. 2. Find what it shows travelers. 3. Say it.
Answer: showing the way (or marking a place).

**What problem does it solve? (match):**
- qajaq (kayak) → hunting on water
- qamutik (dog sled) → traveling over snow and ice
- inuksuk → finding the way
- reindeer herding → food and a living that moves with the seasons
**Footer on the match:** These are Inuit and Sámi ways of knowing. They belong to those peoples.

**Keeper's note:** In the 1900s, many Sámi and Inuit children were sent to boarding schools where their own languages were not allowed. Today many communities are bringing those languages back.

## Book 7. To the South Pole

**Teach (A true thing from history):** In 1911, two teams raced to the South Pole. One was from Norway, one from Britain.

**Teach 2:** Roald Amundsen's team of five used dogs and skis. They reached the Pole on 14 December 1911.

**Teach 3:** From 1903 to 1906, Amundsen learned from Netsilik Inuit about fur clothing and dogs.

**Teach 4:** Robert Falcon Scott's team of five pulled their sledges by hand on the last stretch. They reached the Pole 34 days later.

**Worked example (how many days?):**
1. Amundsen: 14 December. Scott: 17 January.
2. 14 December to 31 December is 17 days.
3. Then 17 more days in January: 17 + 17 = 34.

**Shackleton card:** In 1915, Ernest Shackleton's ship was crushed by sea ice. He never reached the Pole.
**Shackleton card 2:** With five others, he crossed a stormy sea in a small boat to get help. All 28 men survived.
**Shackleton card 3:** In 2022, explorers found his ship, 3,008 meters down. They left it untouched.

**Contrast card:** Reaching a goal is one kind of success. Bringing everyone home is another.

**How we know card:** These were real journeys. Diaries, photographs and maps from them survive in museums.

**Guided (Seedling):** Who reached the South Pole first?
Steps shown: 1. Find the two dates. 2. Which is earlier? 3. Whose date is it?
Answer: Amundsen's team, on 14 December 1911.

**Keeper's note:** Sadly, Scott and the four men with him died on the long journey back. Historians think several things together were to blame: very cold weather, too little fuel and food, and the hard work of pulling sledges by hand. People still remember them, and the records they kept are cared for in museums.

## Book 8. Whose is Antarctica? The Treaty and the stations

**Teach (A true thing from history):** In 1959, twelve countries signed the Antarctic Treaty. It keeps Antarctica for peace and science.

**Teach 2:** Under the Treaty, no single country owns Antarctica. Countries' claims are set aside.

**Teach 3:** The Treaty bans military bases and weapons tests. A later agreement, in 1991, banned mining, except for science.

**Teach 4:** Scientists at research stations study the ice, the weather, the stars, penguins and krill. They come and go by season.

**Worked example (like a shared shelf):**
1. This library shelf belongs to nobody alone.
2. Everyone agrees on rules: read, share, don't take the books away.
3. The Treaty is rules like that, for a whole continent.

**Contrast card:** The Arctic is different. Its land belongs to the countries around it.

**Allowed / banned sort:** Studying penguins (allowed) · Drilling ice cores (allowed) · Building a military base (banned) · Mining for gold or coal (banned) · Watching the stars (allowed) · Sharing results with other countries (allowed)

**Guided (Sapling):** Is mining for coal allowed in Antarctica?
Steps shown: 1. Find the 1991 agreement. 2. What did it ban? 3. So is mining allowed?
Answer: no; mining is banned, except for science.

## Book 9. Watching the ice

**Teach (A true thing about the poles):** Since 1979, satellites have measured Arctic sea ice from space, every year.

**Teach 2:** The Arctic is getting warmer. Late-summer sea ice is shrinking, about 12 parts in every 100 each ten years.

**Teach 3:** One year can be higher than the last. A trend is the pattern over many years.

**Worked example (made-up chart):**
1. The made-up bars go up and down.
2. Draw a line through the middle of them.
3. The line slopes down. That is the trend.

**Teach 4:** Floating sea ice melting barely changes the sea's height. Ice melting from land raises it.

**Teach 5:** How much more the ice changes depends on choices people make. Scientists keep measuring.

**Guided (Sapling):** One year has more ice than the year before. Has the trend changed?
Steps shown: 1. Look at one year. 2. Now look at all the years. 3. Which way does the line slope?
Answer: no; one year is not a trend. The line still slopes down.

## Problem sets

Right answers in **bold**. Say-why answers are spoken to a grown-up, never saved. Every item has the I'm stuck ladder.

### Set A. Two poles, two lights

**A1 (Sprout, 3-choice).** Where do polar bears live in the wild?
- near the South Pole *(misconception: poles-same)*: The South Pole has penguins. Polar bears live far north.
- **near the North Pole**: Yes. Polar bears live in the Arctic, around the North Pole.
- at both of the poles *(misconception: poles-same)*: Warm oceans lie between the poles. Polar bears live only in the north.
Hint: Look at the polar bear card on the top globe.
Explanation: Polar bears live in the Arctic. Wild penguins live in the south.

**A2 (Sprout, 3-choice).** This cross-section is exactly at one geographic pole. Ice rests on rock. Which pole?
- **the Antarctic**: Right. Antarctica is land, so its ice sits on rock.
- the Arctic *(misconception: poles-same)*: Ice at the geographic North Pole floats on ocean, though Arctic land can also hold ice.
- you can't tell *(misconception: rock-not-a-clue)*: For this explicitly labeled geographic-pole section, which pole stands on a continent?
Hint: Which pole is land with ocean around it?
Explanation: Antarctica is a continent, so its ice rests on rock. The North Pole's ice floats.

**A3 (Seedling, 3-choice).** At the North Pole in June, what does the Sun do?
- it sets very early in the day *(misconception: whole-earth-winter)*: June is northern summer. The Sun stays up there.
- it is hot all day *(misconception: midnight-sun-hot)*: The Sun stays up, but low. It is still cold.
- **it stays up all night**: Yes. That's the midnight sun.
Hint: June is summer in the north.
Explanation: In northern summer the Arctic leans toward the Sun, so the Sun never sets.

**A4 (Seedling, 3-choice).** In polar night, where is the Sun?
- switched off for winter *(misconception: sun-switched-off)*: The Sun never switches off. It's shining somewhere else.
- **below the horizon**: Right. It's shining on the other half of Earth.
- hidden behind clouds *(misconception: just-cloudy)*: Clouds can hide it, but in polar night it doesn't rise at all.
Hint: Where does the Sun go after sunset in Willowbank?
Explanation: In polar night the Sun stays below the horizon all day. It is still shining.

**A5 (Seedling, guided number).** In Tromsø, the Sun stays up from 18 May to 24 July. How many days?
Steps shown: 1. 18 to 31 May: 14 days. 2. June: 30 days. 3. 1 to 24 July: 24 days. 4. 14 + 30 + 24 = ?
Answer: 68 days.
Hint: Count May's days from the 18th, including the 18th.
Explanation: 14 + 30 + 24 = 68. That's more than two months of Sun all night.

**A6 (Sapling, number).** How much longer is Tromsø's June day than Willowbank's?
Given: in June, Willowbank's day lasts 15 h 18 min; Tromsø's lasts 24 hours.
Answer: 8 hours 42 minutes.
Hint: 24 hours is 23 h 60 min. Take away 15 h 18 min.
Explanation: 23 h 60 min − 15 h 18 min = 8 h 42 min. Willowbank still has a night; Tromsø doesn't.

**A7 (Sapling, 3-choice).** It's polar night at the North Pole. What's happening at the South Pole?
- polar night too *(misconception: whole-earth-winter)*: The poles lean opposite ways. When one is dark, the other is light.
- a normal day and night *(misconception: just-longer-day)*: Near the South Pole, the Sun is up all day then.
- **midnight sun**: Yes. When the north leans away, the south leans toward the Sun.
Hint: Use the lean. If the north leans away, where does the south lean?
Explanation: The poles have opposite seasons: midnight sun at one, polar night at the other.

**A8 (Keeper's Desk, number).** About how many times the average is the thickest Antarctic ice?
Given: average about 1.9 km thick; thickest about 4.8 km.
Answer: about 2.5 times (4.8 ÷ 1.9 ≈ 2.5).
Hint: How many 1.9s fit in 4.8? Try 2, then a half more.
Explanation: 1.9 × 2 = 3.8; half of 1.9 more makes about 4.8. So about two and a half times.

### Set B. Keeping warm, who eats whom

**B1 (Seedling, 3-choice).** How does blubber keep a seal warm?
- the fat is hot *(misconception: fat-makes-heat)*: Fat isn't hot. A cold jar of fat stays cold.
- it soaks up the sunshine all day *(misconception: insulation-is-heater)*: Seals swim in dark, cold water. Blubber works there too.
- **it slows heat escaping**: Yes. The seal's body makes heat, and blubber slows its escape.
Hint: Remember the jars. Did the fat make heat, or keep it in?
Explanation: Blubber is insulation. It slows heat leaving the body; the body makes the heat.

**B2 (Seedling, guided number).** How much warmer is the thick-fat jar?
Given: after 10 minutes, the thick-fat jar reads 28 °C and the bare jar 10 °C.
Steps shown: 1. Warmer jar: 28. 2. Colder jar: 10. 3. 28 − 10 = ?
Answer: 18 °C.
Hint: Take the smaller number from the bigger one.
Explanation: 28 − 10 = 18. The thick layer kept 18 degrees more warmth in this made-up model.

**B3 (Sapling, 3-choice).** Bramble wraps an ice cube thickly in fat, in a warm room. What happens?
- **it stays frozen longer**: Right. The fat slows heat moving in, too.
- it melts faster than a bare cube *(misconception: fat-makes-heat)*: Fat doesn't make heat. Watch the bench's ice contrast.
- nothing changes *(misconception: insulation-one-way)*: A layer slows heat either way, in or out.
Hint: Try the ice contrast on the keep-warm bench.
Explanation: Insulation slows heat moving either way, so it keeps cold things cold too.

**B4 (Sapling, 3-choice).** Why are an Arctic fox's ears small?
- they hear better in snow *(misconception: wrong-function)*: Ear size helps hearing, but here the cold is the problem.
- **less heat escapes**: Yes. Smaller ears have less skin to lose heat from.
- it chose small ears *(misconception: choose-to-adapt)*: Animals can't choose. Small ears came over many generations.
Hint: Compare the Arctic fox with Pip. Who loses more heat?
Explanation: Small ears, a short muzzle and short legs mean less surface losing heat.

**B5 (Seedling, order).** Put the Antarctic chain in order, food first.
Cards: leopard seal · krill · phytoplankton · Adélie penguin.
Answer: phytoplankton → krill → Adélie penguin → leopard seal.
Hint: Start with the one that makes food from sunlight.
Explanation: The arrow goes from food to eater, so it starts with phytoplankton.

**B6 (Sapling, 3-choice).** What if krill grew scarce? What may happen to Adélie penguins?
- **they may have less food**: Yes. Penguins eat krill, so their food may shrink.
- nothing happens to them *(misconception: link-doesnt-matter)*: Follow the arrow from krill. Who eats krill?
- they may get much more food *(misconception: arrow-reversed)*: Check the arrow. Krill is food for penguins.
Hint: Follow the arrow that leaves krill.
Explanation: A lost link can spread up the chain. "May" matters: real animals eat other things too.

**B7 (Seedling, 3-choice).** What does every polar food chain start with?
- a big hunter at the top *(misconception: starts-with-animal)*: Big hunters come at the end. What feeds everything else?
- snow and ice *(misconception: nonliving-food)*: Ice holds algae, but the ice itself isn't food.
- **tiny sunlight-users**: Right. Phytoplankton or ice algae start the chain.
Hint: Which card makes food from sunlight?
Explanation: Tiny living things that make food from sunlight start every polar chain.

### Set C. Ice, people and the record

**C1 (Sprout, 3-choice).** What is sea ice made from?
- **frozen sea water**: Yes. Sea ice forms when the sea freezes.
- snow pressed for years *(misconception: glacier-is-sea-ice)*: That makes a glacier, on land.
- water from rivers *(misconception: all-ice-same)*: Rivers can freeze, but sea ice is the frozen sea.
Hint: Read the first card in book 5.
Explanation: Sea ice is frozen sea water, so it starts out salty. Glaciers grow from snow on land.

**C2 (Seedling, 3-choice).** Where is most of an iceberg?

- **under the water**: Right. About nine-tenths is hidden below.
- half above, half below *(misconception: tip-is-all)*: Ice floats low. Much more than half is underwater.
Hint: Look at the iceberg picture in book 5.
Explanation: Ice is only a little lighter than sea water, so most of it sits underwater.

**C3 (Seedling, 3-choice).** Which is true about the Inuit?
- they lived only long ago *(misconception: peoples-of-past)*: The Inuit live in the Arctic today, in towns.
- they all live in snow houses all year *(misconception: igloo-home)*: Snow houses are mostly for hunting trips. Families live in houses.
- **they live in the Arctic today**: Yes. About 180,000 Inuit live there now.
Hint: Read the first two cards in book 6.
Explanation: The Inuit are a living people with towns, schools, jobs and long knowledge of the North.

**C4 (Sapling, 3-choice).** About how many Sámi herd reindeer?
- nearly all of them *(misconception: all-herders)*: Herding is important, but most Sámi do other work.
- **about one in ten**: Right. About one in ten herd reindeer.
- none anymore *(misconception: peoples-of-past)*: Reindeer herding goes on today. In some areas it's common.

Explanation: About one in ten Sámi herd reindeer, more in some areas. Others do every kind of job.

**C5 (Sapling, number).** How many days after Amundsen did Scott's team arrive?

**C6 (Sapling, 3-choice).** Why did Amundsen's team travel fast?
- they were lucky with the weather *(misconception: amundsen-luck)*: Weather helps, but they planned carefully, with dogs and skis.
- they had motor sledges *(misconception: mix-up)*: Scott's team tried motor sledges. Amundsen used dogs.
- **dogs, skis and planning**: Yes. Some lessons came from Inuit in the Arctic.
Hint: Read Teach 2 and Teach 3 in book 7.
Explanation: Dogs pulled the loads, the team skied, and they planned their food stores well.

**C7 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice).** Under the Antarctic Treaty, who owns Antarctica?
- **no single country**: Right. Claims are set aside, and it's kept for peace and science.

## Key terms

- **Arctic:** the cold region around the North Pole, mostly ocean with land around it.
- **Antarctic:** the cold region around the South Pole; Antarctica is land with ocean around it.
- **Lean (tilt):** Earth's axis slants, so each half leans toward the Sun for part of the year.
- **Midnight sun:** when the Sun stays up all day and night.
- **Polar night:** when the Sun stays below the horizon all day.
- **Adaptation:** a feature or behavior that helps a living thing survive where it lives.
- **Insulation:** a layer that slows heat moving through it.
- **Blubber:** a thick layer of fat under the skin of seals, whales and others.
- **Food chain:** a line of who is food for whom, starting with a sunlight-user.
- **Phytoplankton:** tiny living things in water that make food from sunlight.
- **Krill:** small shrimp-like animals that many polar animals eat.
- **Sea ice:** frozen sea water.
- **Glacier:** a huge mass of ice made from snow, on land, slowly flowing.
- **Ice sheet:** a glacier big enough to cover a large area of land.
- **Iceberg:** a piece of a glacier floating in the sea.
- **Inuit:** "the people" in Inuktitut; one person is an Inuk. Inuit live in Alaska, Canada, Greenland (Kalaallit Nunaat) and Russia.
- **Inuksuk:** a stone landmark built by Inuit.
- **Sámi:** a people of the far north of Norway, Sweden, Finland and Russia. Their homeland is Sápmi.
