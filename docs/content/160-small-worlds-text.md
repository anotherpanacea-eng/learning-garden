## Story scenes

### Scene 1. Pip's bun (the brass microscope comes out)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** The librarian sets the brass microscope on the Science table. Pip wants to see his bun "really big."

**Short:**
> Pip puts a whole bun under the microscope. He sees only dark. Light has to shine through a thing. So the librarian shaves off one thin, thin slice.

**Longer:**
> "Make it big," says Pip, and puts his whole bun on the little stage.
>
> He squints into the eyepiece. "It's broken. It's all dark."
>
> "Look underneath," says the librarian. They tilt the small round mirror. Window light bounces up through a hole in the stage. "The light has to come up *through* what you're looking at. A bun is far too thick."
>
> They take a crumb, press it flat and lay it on a glass slide with a drop of water.
>
> "Start with the smallest lens. Turn the big knob slowly, and watch from the side so the lens never bumps the glass."
>
> Pip looks again. "Bubbles! Walls! It's like a sponge city."
>
> "A slice thin enough for light," says {guide}. "Those holes are baking bubbles, though. Cells come next."
>
> Pip eats the rest of the bun, for science.

### Scene 2. Bramble's giant cell

**Hook:** Bramble calls everyone over. She has found "the biggest, roundest cell in the whole valley."

**Short:**
> Bramble finds a perfect circle with a thick black edge. It's an air bubble, trapped when the cover glass dropped flat. She was right to look closely.

**Longer:**
> "Perfectly round," says Bramble. Her magpie peers into the eyepiece too. "Thick black edge. Nothing inside. Obviously important."
>
> The librarian looks. "That's an air bubble. The thin cover glass went down flat and trapped some air."
>
> Bramble folds her arms. "It *looks* like a cell."
>
> "It does," says the librarian. "Plenty of people have been fooled by bubbles. Real cells have edges that fit together, and things inside them."
>
> They show her how to lower the cover glass at a slant, one edge first, so the air slides out.
>
> The next slide has no bubbles. Just rows of small boxes, fitted together like bricks.
>
> "Those," says Bramble, "are obviously cells."
>
> "Obviously," says {guide}.

### Scene 3. The baker's bubbles (Ember's birthday kitchen)

**Hook:** The baker brings a spoon of yeast mixed with warm water. "This makes my bread rise. Is it alive?"

**Short:**
> Under the microscope, the baker's yeast is a crowd of tiny ovals. Some have small buds growing on them. Each oval is one whole living thing.

**Longer:**
> The baker sets down a spoonful of cloudy, foamy water. "Yeast," he says. "It makes my dough rise. My grandmother said it was alive. I never believed her."
>
> The librarian makes a slide. On the highest lens, the cloud turns into a crowd of tiny ovals.
>
> "Look," says Dot. "That one has a little lump on it."
>
> "A bud," says the librarian. "It will grow and split off as a new yeast cell. Each oval is a whole living thing, made of just one cell."
>
> "And the bubbles in my dough?"
>
> "Gas the yeast gives off as it feeds. Your grandmother was right."
>
> The baker looks into the eyepiece for a long time. "Hello," he says quietly, to all of them.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about living things):**
> Baker's yeast is a fungus that lives as single cells, each about 5 to 10 thousandths of a millimeter across. It makes new cells by budding. As it feeds on sugars in dough, it gives off carbon dioxide gas, which makes the dough rise.

### Scene 4. A drop from the wet corner (Act IV revisit)

**Hook:** Pip brings a jar of water from the reedy corner at the bottom of the orchard. "It looks clean," he says.

**Short:**
> One drop of the reedy-corner water is full of tiny swimmers. Each is one cell, moving, feeding and growing. Most are harmless. They are part of the wet meadow.

**Longer:**
> Pip holds up the jar. "Clear as glass. Nothing in it."
>
> The librarian puts one drop on a slide. On the middle lens, the drop is busy. A slipper shape glides past, covered in tiny beating hairs. A green speck spins and darts. A blob slowly pushes out an arm and flows after it.
>
> Pip steps back. "Are they dangerous?"
>
> "These ones are harmless," says the librarian. "Most tiny living things are. Each of these is a single cell that moves, feeds and grows. Some make food from light. Bigger animals in the wet corner eat them."
>
> "So the corner is full of life I can't see."
>
> "Full of it," says {guide}.
>
> Pip writes on the jar: CROWDED.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about living things):**
> The slipper shape is like a paramecium, which moves with thousands of hairs called cilia. A common kind is about 0.2 to 0.3 millimeters long. The green swimmer is like a euglena: it has chloroplasts and a whip-like flagellum. The blob is like an amoeba, which moves and feeds by pushing out parts of itself.

### Scene 5. Dot's clean hands (Harvest Supper)

**Hook (older depth):** Dot washes her hands before she spoons the jam. Pip holds up his paws. "Mine look clean. Why bother?"

**Short:**
> Pip's paws look clean. But microbes are far too small to see. Dot shows him: soap, a good scrub, a rinse under running water, then dry.

**Longer:**
> "They look clean," says Pip. "No mud. No jam. Yet."
>
> Dot rolls up her sleeves at the pump. "Remember the drop from the wet corner? It looked clean too."
>
> Pip thinks about the slipper shapes. "Oh."
>
> "Microbes are far too small to see," says the librarian. "So cooks wash before handling food, every time, even when hands look clean."
>
> Dot works soap into a lather, between her fingers, around her thumbs, under her nails. "The soap loosens dirt and microbes. The water carries them away."
>
> Pip copies her, very thoroughly, up to his elbows. Then they both dry their paws on a clean towel.
>
> "Now," he says, "the jam."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about living things):**
> Health agencies advise washing with soap and clean running water, scrubbing for about 20 seconds, then drying with a clean towel or the air. Lather and scrubbing lift dirt, grease and microbes off the skin, and rinsing washes them away.

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer; worked-example steps appear one tap at a time.

**Card 1. Light shines through (A true thing about living things)**
> A microscope shines light up through a very thin slice. Lenses make the picture look many times bigger.

Worked example: *Pip's crumb.*
1. A whole bun blocks the light: dark.
2. A thin, flat crumb lets light through.
3. Now the lenses can show its tiny bubbles and walls.

**Card 2. Start small, focus slowly**
> Start with the lowest-power lens. Turn the big knob while watching from the side. Then use the small knob to make it sharp.

Worked example: *Setting up the brass microscope.*
1. Click the 4-times lens into place.
2. Watching from the side, lower the lens close to the slide.
3. Look in. Turn the big knob slowly away until it's nearly clear.
4. Small knob: sharp.

**Card 3. Times bigger**
> "10 times" means the picture looks ten times longer. A microscope has two lenses. Multiply their numbers to find the total.

Worked example: *Eyepiece 10 times, lens 4 times.*
1. The eyepiece makes it 10 times bigger.
2. The lower lens makes it 4 times bigger first.
3. 10 × 4 = 40. It looks 40 times bigger.

**Card 4. Living things are made of cells**
> Every living thing is made of one or more tiny cells. Most are far too small to see without a microscope.

Worked example: *Three slides.*
1. Onion skin: rows of box-shaped cells.
2. Pondweed leaf: boxes with green dots inside.
3. A prepared slide of animal cells: rounder shapes, each with a dark dot.

**Card 5. Parts of our pictured plant and animal cells**
> A membrane holds the cell in and lets some things through. Cytoplasm fills it. The nucleus holds the instructions that run the cell.

Worked example: *One animal cell.*
1. The thin edge: the membrane.
2. The jelly inside: cytoplasm.
3. The dark dot: the nucleus.

**Card 6. Plant cells have extras**
> Plant cells also have a stiff wall outside the membrane, a big vacuole of watery sap, and often green chloroplasts that make food.

Worked example: *A pondweed leaf cell.*
1. A firm box shape: the wall.
2. A big clear space: the vacuole.
3. Green dots: chloroplasts, catching light to make food.

**Card 7. Cells, tissues, organs**
> Similar cells working together make a tissue. Tissues make an organ. Organs working together make a system. Systems make a whole living thing.

Worked example: *Zooming out from one leaf cell.*
1. One leaf cell with chloroplasts.
2. A layer of cells like it: leaf tissue.
3. Several tissues: a leaf, an organ.
4. Leaves and stems together: the shoot system.

**Card 8. One cell, one whole life**
> Some living things are just one cell. That one cell moves, feeds, grows, responds and makes more of itself.

Worked example: *A paramecium in pond water.*
1. Moves: tiny hairs beat like oars.
2. Feeds: sweeps food into a groove.
3. Makes more: splits into two.

**Card 9. Tiny helpers and germs (older depth)**
> Many bacteria and other microbes are harmless or helpful. A few cause illness. Washing with soap removes microbes we can't see.

Worked example: *Bacteria at work.*
1. In soil: breaking down dead leaves.
2. On bean roots: helping the plant get nitrogen.
3. In yogurt: turning milk thick and tangy.

## Two Cells Side by Side

**Prompt:** Sort each part. Both cells, or plant cells only?

**Cards:** membrane · cytoplasm · nucleus · cell wall · big vacuole · chloroplasts

| Card | Goes to | Feedback |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Membrane | Both | Yes. Every cell has one, plants too. |
| Cytoplasm | Both | Yes. The jelly that fills every cell. |
| Nucleus | Both | Yes. The instructions live here. |
| Cell wall | Plant only | Yes. A stiff layer outside the membrane. |
| Big vacuole | Plant only | Yes. Full of sap, it helps keep the plant firm. |
| Chloroplasts | Plant only | Yes. They catch light to make food. Not every plant cell has them. |

**Wrong-sort lines:** Wall in Both: Animal cells have only a soft membrane. Look at the edge. · Chloroplasts in Both: Animals eat their food. Which cells make food from light?

**After sorting:** Of these six parts, animal cells have none that plant cells lack. Plants have extras for standing up and making food.

**What if it were missing? (Sapling):** Tap a part to take it away.
- Wall: The plant cell goes floppy. Plants have no bones; walls help them stand.
- Chloroplasts: This cell can't make food from light.
- Nucleus: No instructions. The cell can't keep itself going.

## The Keeper's Desk: First Looks

**Header (A true thing from history):** Early looks at cells.

**Card: Robert Hooke, 1665.** Hooke was an English scientist. In his book *Micrographia* (1665) he drew a thin slice of cork. He saw tiny boxes "much like a Honey-comb" and called them cells, an old word for small rooms.

**Evidence and interpretation:**
- What he saw: walls around empty spaces.
- What we know now: cork cells are dead; only their walls are left.
- What he could not know: that living cells are full, with a nucleus.

**Card: Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, 1670s.** Leeuwenhoek sold cloth in Delft, in the Netherlands. He ground tiny single lenses. In 1675 he saw tiny swimmers in rainwater. His Dutch word meant "little animals"; English readers said "animalcules". His letter of 1676 reached London's Royal Society.

**Lens card:** One of his surviving microscopes, kept at Utrecht University's museum, magnifies about 270 times. It has one lens.

**Card: All living things (1838 and 1839).** Two German scientists, Schleiden and Schwann, argued that all plants and all animals are made of cells.

**Long Line cards** (added to `#archive/timeline`): 1665 Hooke names cells · 1676 Leeuwenhoek's animalcule letter · 1838-1839 all living things are made of cells

## Problem sets

Every item has the "I'm stuck" ladder. Choices appear in the order written; ✔ marks the right one. Tags in backticks name the misconception a wrong choice tests.

### Set A. Using the microscope (Sprout, Seedling)

**A1 (Sprout, guided).** Steps shown first: 1. Look at the three lenses. 2. Find the smallest number. 3. That one first.
- Prompt: Which lens do you start with?
- Choices: The 4-times lens ✔ · The 40-times lens (`start-high`) · Any lens at all (`order-irrelevant`)
- Feedback: 40 times: Up close it's hard to find anything. Start wide. · Any: Order matters. The low lens shows the most, to find your place.
- Hint: Which lens shows the biggest area?
- Explanation: The lowest power shows the widest view, so you find the thing first. Then you zoom in.

**A2 (Sprout, order the cards).**
- Prompt: Put the slide steps in order.
- Cards: thin slice · drop of water · cover glass at a slant · lowest lens · big knob · small knob
- Answer: in that order; slice and water may swap. Tag on cover glass before water: `order-irrelevant`.
- Hint: What has to go on the slide before you cover it?
- Explanation: Thin for light, water to hold it flat, a slanted cover glass to push air out, then focus from wide to sharp.

**A3 (Seedling).**
- Prompt: Bramble sees a perfect circle, black edge, empty inside. What is it?
- Choices: A giant cell (`bubble-is-cell`) · A nucleus, very large (`bubble-is-cell`) · An air bubble ✔
- Feedback: Giant cell: Cells fit together with parts inside. This is empty. · Nucleus: A nucleus sits inside a cell. This sits alone.
- Hint: Think about how the cover glass went down.
- Explanation: A cover glass dropped flat traps air. Bubbles are round, dark-edged and empty.

**A4 (Seedling, true / not true).**
- "The microscope makes the cells." Not true (`microscope-makes`): the cells were there; the lenses only show them.
- "A thin slice lets light through." True.
- Hint: Were the onion's cells there before anyone looked?
- Explanation: A microscope reveals. It doesn't create.

### Set B. Times bigger (Seedling, Sapling, Keeper's Desk)

**B1 (Seedling, guided number).** Steps shown first: 1. Real length: 2 mm. 2. Times bigger: 10. 3. Multiply.
- Prompt: A 10-times hand lens shows a 2 mm seed. How long does it look?
- Answer: 20 mm. Tag for 12 mm: `add-not-multiply`.
- Hint: 10 times bigger means 10 lots of 2 mm.
- Explanation: 2 × 10 = 20. It looks 20 mm long.

**B2 (Seedling).**
- Prompt: Eyepiece 10 times, lens 10 times. How much bigger altogether?
- Choices: 20 times (`add-not-multiply`) · 100 times ✔ · 10 times (`objective-only`)
- Feedback: 20: Lenses multiply. Each makes the last picture bigger. · 10: That's one lens. Both lenses work.
- Hint: The second lens enlarges what the first made.
- Explanation: 10 × 10 = 100. It looks 100 times bigger.

**B3 (Sapling, number).** A made-up model; real sizes vary.
- Prompt: A paramecium is about 0.2 mm long. How long does it look at 40 times?
- Answer: 8 mm. Tag for 0.8 mm: `decimal-slip`.
- Hint: 0.2 × 10 is 2. Now 2 × 4.
- Explanation: 0.2 × 40 = 8. It looks 8 mm long, easy to see.

**B4 (Sapling).**
- Prompt: A bacterium about 0.002 mm long, at 400 times. How long does it look?
- Choices: 0.8 mm ✔ · 8 mm (`decimal-slip`) · 80 mm (`decimal-slip`)
- Feedback: 8 mm: Check the zeros. 0.002 × 400 is less than 1. · 80 mm: Too big. Multiply carefully: 2 × 400 is 800 thousandths.
- Hint: Try 2 × 400 first. Then remember it was thousandths.
- Explanation: 0.002 × 400 = 0.8 mm. Just a speck, even on the strongest lens.

**B5 (Keeper's Desk, number).** A made-up model.
- Prompt: A cell is 0.3 mm long. Your drawing is 24 mm. How many times bigger?
- Answer: 80 times. Tag for 7.2: `multiply-not-divide`.

### Set C. Plant and animal cells (Seedling, Sapling)

**C1 (Seedling, guided).** Steps shown first: 1. Look at both cells. 2. Find a part in each. 3. Check the animal cell.
- Prompt: Which part do plant and animal cells both have?
- Choices: A cell wall (`animal-has-wall`) · Chloroplasts (`animal-has-chloroplasts`) · A nucleus ✔
- Feedback: Wall: Only plant cells have a stiff wall. · Chloroplasts: Animals eat their food. They don't make it from light.
- Hint: Find the dark dot in each cell.
- Explanation: Both cells have a nucleus, a membrane and cytoplasm.

**C2 (Seedling).**
- Prompt: Onion skin cells have no green chloroplasts. Why?
- Choices: Its inner layers grow hidden from light ✔ · They are animal cells, so they never had any (`green-means-plant`) · The water rinsed the green chloroplasts out (`artifact-cause`)
- Feedback: Animal cells: They have walls, so they're plant cells. Not every plant cell is green. · Water: Chloroplasts don't wash out. These cells never had them.
- Hint: Does light reach the inside of an onion?
- Explanation: Chloroplasts are in parts that get light, like leaves. An onion's inner layers stay dark.

**C3 (Sapling).**
- Prompt: What is true about a plant cell's edge?
- Choices: It has a wall instead of a membrane (`wall-instead-of-membrane`) · It has only a membrane (`plant-no-wall`) · It has a wall outside its membrane ✔
- Feedback: Instead: Plant cells have both. The wall is an extra layer. · Only membrane: Look again. Plant cells are boxy because of a wall.
- Hint: Card 6 said "outside the membrane."
- Explanation: Plant cells have a membrane and, outside it, a stiff wall.

**C4 (Sapling, match).** Match each part to its job.
- Wall: helps the cell keep its shape · Membrane: lets some things in and out · Nucleus: holds the instructions · Chloroplast: makes food using light · Vacuole: stores watery sap
- Tag on nucleus matched to "thinks": `nucleus-is-brain`.
- Hint: Which part is green? Start there.
- Explanation: Each part has a job, and the cell needs them all working together.

### Set D. Small lives (Seedling, Sapling, Keeper's Desk)

**D1 (Seedling).**
- Prompt: Is one yeast cell a whole living thing?
- Choices: Only while it is working inside dough (`alive-only-when-useful`) · Yes, it feeds, grows and makes more ✔ · No, a single cell is far too small (`one-cell-not-alive`)
- Feedback: Dough: Yeast lives in many places. Dough is just one. · Too small: Size doesn't decide. One cell can do every life job.
- Hint: What did the yeast cell with a bud do?
- Explanation: Each yeast cell feeds, grows and buds new cells. It is a whole living thing.

**D2 (Seedling).**

- Hint: Think of the bubbles in the yeast water.

**D3 (Sapling, true / not true).**
- "All microbes are harmful germs." Not true (`all-microbes-germs`): many are harmless or helpful.
- "Some bacteria help bean plants get nitrogen." True: they live in bumps on the roots.
- Hint: What did the bacteria in the yogurt and soil do?
- Explanation: Only a few microbes cause illness. Many keep soil, plants and foods working.

**D4 (Sapling, number).** A made-up model: at each step, every yeast cell buds into two.

**D5 (Keeper's Desk).**
- Prompt: Dot's hands look clean. Why wash before handling food?

- Hint: Think of the clear jar from the wet corner.

### Set E. Keeper's Desk: first looks and levels

**E1 (Keeper's Desk).**
- Prompt: What did Hooke see in his cork slice?
- Choices: Living cells, each with a nucleus (`present-back`) · Walls around empty spaces ✔ · Tiny animals swimming about (`hooke-leeuwenhoek-swap`)

- Hint: Is cork from a living part of the tree?

**E2 (Keeper's Desk, number).** From *Micrographia*.

**E3 (Keeper's Desk, true / not true from this source).** Source: the Leeuwenhoek cards.

- "He called them bacteria." Not true (`present-back`): his word meant little animals.
- "His microscope had one lens." True.
- Hint: Which word did he use? Look at the card.
- Explanation: Old sources use old words. We decide what he saw by reading his descriptions.

**E4 (Keeper's Desk, order).**
- Prompt: Order these from smallest to biggest.
- Cards: root hair cell · root tissue · a root · the root system · the whole plant
- Answer: in that order. Tag on root before tissue: `organ-not-cells`.
- Hint: Each step is made of many of the step before.
- Explanation: Cells make tissues, tissues make an organ, organs make a system, systems make the plant.

**E5 (Keeper's Desk).**
- Prompt: Which of these is a tissue?
- Choices: A single muscle cell, on its own (`cell-is-tissue`) · The whole heart, pumping blood (`tissue-is-organ`) · A layer of similar leaf cells ✔
- Feedback: One cell: A tissue is many similar cells together. · Heart: The heart is an organ, built from several tissues.
- Hint: A tissue is a group of similar cells doing one job.
- Explanation: A layer of similar cells working together is a tissue. Here "tissue" doesn't mean a paper tissue.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Cell:** the tiny building block of every living thing.
- **Microscope:** a tool with lenses that makes tiny things look bigger.
- **Magnification:** how many times bigger something looks.
- **Slide:** a strip of glass that holds what you look at. A **cover glass** is the thin square on top.
- **Nucleus:** the part that holds the cell's instructions.
- **Membrane:** the thin edge that holds a cell in and lets some things through.
- **Cytoplasm:** the jelly that fills a cell.
- **Cell wall:** a stiff layer outside a plant cell's membrane.
- **Vacuole:** a space filled with watery sap. Plant cells have a big one.
- **Chloroplast:** a green part that makes food using light.
- **Tissue:** a group of similar cells doing one job (not a paper tissue).
- **Organ:** a body part built from tissues, like a leaf or a heart.
- **Microbe:** a living thing too small to see without a microscope.
- **Bacteria:** very small single-celled microbes with no nucleus.
- **Yeast:** a single-celled fungus. Bakers use it.
- **Animalcules:** an old English word, meaning "little animals", for the tiny swimmers Leeuwenhoek saw.

## Off-screen card (adult-led, optional)

**An onion-skin slide.** Only with a grown-up, who cuts the onion and handles the slides.
1. A grown-up cuts an onion and peels the thin see-through skin from inside one layer.
2. Lay a small piece flat on a glass slide with a drop of water.
3. Lower the cover glass at a slant.
4. Start on the lowest lens.

**Safety lines (always shown):** A grown-up uses the knife. Slides and cover glasses are thin glass and can break. If a microscope has a mirror, never point it at the sun: reflected sunlight can hurt your eyes. Plain water works; a grown-up decides about any stain. Nothing is saved or recorded.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** how a light microscope is used (lowest power first, coarse then fine focus, a slanted cover glass), magnification as "times bigger" (eyepiece × objective; image ÷ real size), that living things are made of cells, the parts of plant and animal cells, cells to tissues to organs to systems, and that a single cell can be a whole organism. It adds two real histories: Hooke's cork cells (1665) and Leeuwenhoek's animalcules (1670s).

**What it does not claim:** the brass microscope and its slides are Willowbank fiction. Sizes are typical values given as "about." Mitochondria and ribosomes are left for later. The handwashing card gives only the standard advice (soap, scrub, rinse under running water) and no other health guidance. No question asks about a child's body, hands or home.

**Safety:** the onion-skin slide is optional, adult-led and off-screen.
