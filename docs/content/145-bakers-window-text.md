## Story scenes

### Scene 1. Will the pages melt?

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** The parcel's pages are drying on the baker's sunny sill. "They'll melt!" says Pip. The baker isn't sure. {guide} looks at you.

**Short:**
> Pip fears the sun will melt the pages. Paper doesn't melt. The water in the pages leaves as invisible vapor. That is drying.

**Longer:**
> Pip finds the pages on the sunny sill and clutches his tail. "Butter melts there in ten minutes," he says. "These will melt too!"
>
> "Paper likes slow, warm air," says the baker. "I know that much. I couldn't tell you why butter melts and paper doesn't."
>
> {guide} touches the nearest page. "Still damp. What is a page made of?"
>
> "Paper," says the baker. "And water, at the moment."
>
> "Butter is a solid that turns liquid when it warms," says {guide}. "Paper doesn't do that. It's the water in the paper that's changing."
>
> By supper the page is crisp and light, a little wrinkled. No puddle under it. No drips.
>
> "So where did the water go?" asks the baker.
>
> "Into the air," says {guide}. "It became water vapor. You can't see it."
>
> The baker looks at his butter, his dough and his tea. "Then I have questions," he says.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):**
> Drying is evaporation: liquid water becomes invisible water vapor and mixes into the air. Warmth usually speeds it up. A wet page weighs more than the same page dry; the difference is the water that left. Paper does not melt. Far too much heat would scorch or burn it, which is a different change that cannot be undone.

### Scene 2. Ember wants to help

**Hook:** It's nearly Ember's birthday. The baker is baking. Ember would like to help with the heating. Everyone would like Ember not to.

**Short:**
> The baker bakes for Ember's birthday. Ember offers to breathe on the oven. The baker says no, kindly. Pip offers to eat the experiments. Also no.

**Longer:**
> The bakery is busy. Bread for the supper, a cake for Ember, chocolate for the top.
>
> "I can help with the heating," says Ember, and takes a deep breath.
>
> "No," says the baker, Dot, {guide} and Pip, all at once.
>
> "Oven heat is steady," says the baker. "Dragon heat is exciting. Bread does not like exciting."
>
> Ember lets the breath out as a small, sad puff.
>
> "You can watch the chocolate instead," says the baker. "It's the most important job."
>
> Pip leans over the counter. "I can test things. By eating them."
>
> "Nobody eats the experiments," says {guide}. "Not even the chocolate."
>
> "Especially not the chocolate," says the baker, and moves it.
>
> The dough goes into the oven soft and pale. It comes out brown and crusty, and it smells wonderful.
>
> "Can you turn it back into dough?" asks Ember.
>
> The baker laughs. "Nobody can."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):**
> Warm bread dough rises because yeast in it makes carbon dioxide gas, and the gas makes bubbles. Baking makes new substances: a brown crust, a new smell, a firm spongy inside. That is why bread cannot become dough again. Melted chocolate is different: it sets when it cools, and it is still chocolate.

### Scene 3. Bramble's potion

**Hook:** Bramble stirs a spoonful of sugar into her potion. "Gone," she says. "Disappeared. That is magic." Her magpie looks doubtful.

**Short:**
> Bramble says her sugar disappeared. The scale says it's still there. It dissolved: it spread through the water in pieces too small to see.

**Longer:**
> Bramble arrives with a jar of very green potion and a spoonful of sugar.
>
> "Watch," she says. She tips in the sugar and stirs. The grains get smaller, then they're gone. "Disappeared. Magic."
>
> Her magpie tilts its head.
>
> "Did it melt?" asks Pip.
>
> "It wasn't warm," says {guide}. "Melting needs warmth. This is different. Can we weigh it?"
>
> The baker brings his scale. You weigh the potion and the sugar before, side by side. Then Bramble stirs the sugar in, lid on, and you weigh again.
>
> The number hasn't changed.
>
> Bramble frowns. "So it's still in there."
>
> "Dissolved," says {guide}. "Spread through the potion, in pieces too small to see."
>
> "Fine," says Bramble. "It's hiding. Magically." She screws the lid on tight. "And nobody is tasting it."
>
> "Nobody ever tastes the experiments," says the baker.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):**
> When sugar dissolves, it breaks into pieces far too small to see and spreads through the water. In a closed jar the total mass stays the same. You can get the sugar back by letting the water evaporate. In a real lab, nobody tastes anything to check: weighing and evaporating are the tests.

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer; worked-example steps appear one tap at a time.

**Card 1. Solid, liquid, gas (A true thing about science)**
> A solid keeps its shape. A liquid takes its container's shape. A gas spreads out to fill the space.

Worked example: *Water from a jug, poured into a tray.*
1. In the jug, the water is jug-shaped.
2. In the tray, it is tray-shaped.
3. It is the same amount of water.
4. It flows and takes the shape. It's a liquid.

**Card 2. Melting is not dissolving**
> Melting: one thing warms and turns liquid. In our bench, dissolving spreads a solid through water, in pieces too small to see.

Worked example: *An ice cube on the sill; sugar stirred into water.*
1. The ice cube warms. It becomes water. Nothing else was needed.
2. The sugar was stirred into water. Nothing was warmed.
3. The sugar is still there, spread out.
4. Ice melted. Sugar dissolved.

**Card 3. Can it be undone?**
> Some changes can be undone. Some make something new and can't. Ask: did something new get made?

Worked example: *Chocolate and bread dough.*
1. Warm chocolate melts. Cool it, and it sets again.
2. It's still chocolate. That can be undone.
3. Bake dough. It turns brown and smells new.
4. Nothing turns bread back into dough. That can't be undone.

**Card 4. Weigh the whole jar**
> In a closed jar, nothing gets in or out. So the total mass stays the same, even when you can't see the salt.

Worked example: *100 g of water and 20 g of salt.*
1. Before: 100 g + 20 g on the scale.
2. 100 + 20 = 120 g.
3. Stir the salt in. Lid on.
4. The scale still reads 120 g. The salt is still there.

**Card 5. Get it back**
> To separate a mixture, look for a difference. Different sizes: sieve. Won't dissolve: filter. Dissolved: evaporate the water.

Worked example: *Sand stirred into water.*
1. Sand doesn't dissolve. You can still see it.
2. Pour the mixture through a filter.
3. The sand stays on the paper.
4. Clear water drips through.

**Card 6. Particle pictures (Sapling and Keeper's Desk) (A simplified picture)**
> Scientists picture tiny particles. Solid: packed in rows. Liquid: close but jumbled. Gas: far apart and moving. It's a model.

Worked example: *Ice melting, in a particle picture.*
1. Before: dots in neat rows, wiggling in place.
2. Warm it. The dots move more.
3. After: dots still close, now jumbled, sliding past.
4. Same dots, same size. Only the pattern changed.

## The scale

**Title:** The scale

**Zeroing note (always shown):** The scale was set to zero with the empty jar and lid on it. It shows only what's inside.

**Model line (always shown):** A made-up model with exact numbers. A real scale can wobble by a gram or so.

**Case 1, Closed jar:**
1. Water in the jar: 100 g. Salt in the dish: 20 g.
2. Both on the scale: **120 g**.
3. Tip the salt in. Lid on. Stir.
4. The salt is out of sight. The scale reads **120 g**.

**After case 1:** The salt didn't disappear. It dissolved. Its mass is still there.

**Case 2, Open jar on the sill:**
1. A jar of salt water with no lid: 120 g.
2. It sits in the sun for many days.
3. The scale reads **100 g**.
4. Where did 20 g go?

**After case 2:** The water evaporated into the air. The salt didn't. It's all still in the jar.

**Case 3, Covered chocolate (Keeper's Desk):** 50 g of chocolate in a covered bowl, before and after melting: **50 g** both times.

## Get it back

**Prompt:** Choose a tool to get each part back.
**Tools:** Sieve · Filter · Evaporating dish on the sunny sill
**Safety line (always shown):** Simplified pictures. In real life, a grown-up helps with any heating.

| Mixture | Tool | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Flour and raisins | Sieve | The flour falls through. The raisins stay in the sieve. |
| Flour and raisins | Filter | The paper clogs with flour. A sieve is quicker. |
| Flour and raisins | Evaporate | Nothing to evaporate. They're both dry solids. |
| Sand and water | Filter | The sand stays on the paper. Clear water drips through. |
| Sand and water | Sieve | The sand grains are too small. They go through. |
| Sand and water | Evaporate | You get the sand back, but the water's gone. A filter keeps both. |
| Salt water | Evaporate | Days later the water has gone into the air. Salt crystals are left. |
| Salt water | Filter | It all drips through. The salt is dissolved, so it passes too. |
| Salt water | Sieve | It all goes through. Salt in water is too spread out. |

**How we know salt passed the filter:** Put a drop of what dripped through on a dish. When it dries, a white crust is left. That's salt. We never taste to check.

**Sand, salt and water (Keeper's Desk):** Choose two steps, in order.
- Filter, then evaporate: The sand stays on the filter. The salty water drips through. Evaporating it leaves the salt. All three parts back.
- Evaporate, then filter: The water goes into the air first. Sand and salt are left mixed together, dry. The filter can't help now.
- Sieve, then anything: The sieve lets everything through.

## Particle pictures (Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**Label (always shown):** A particle picture is a model. Real particles are far too small to see, and there are many more of them.

**Sapling prompt:** Match each box to solid, liquid or gas.
**Box captions after matching:**
- Solid: close together, in rows, wiggling in place. That's why it keeps its shape.
- Liquid: close together, jumbled, sliding past each other. That's why it flows.
- Gas: far apart, moving fast every way. That's why it fills the space.

**Keeper's Desk prompt:** Draw ice before and after melting. Use all twelve dots.
**Checks (gentle, never scored):**
- If dots are removed or added: Keep all twelve dots. Melting doesn't make or lose any.
- If "after" dots are spread far apart: Melted water's particles stay close. Far apart would be a gas.
**Annotation choices:** The dots moved more and lost their rows. · The dots got bigger. · The dots melted.
**Annotation feedback:** First: Yes. Same dots, more movement, no pattern. · Second: Particles stay the same size. Only spacing and movement change. · Third: Particles don't melt. The whole ice does.

**Keeper's Desk prompt 2:** Draw sugar dissolving in water. Use both colors of dots.
**Note after drawing:** The sugar dots spread out among the water dots. They're all still there.
**Gap note:** The space between particles is empty. It isn't filled with air.

## Problem sets

Each item lists prompt, choices (right answer marked ✔), misconception tags for wrong choices, hint, and explanation. Feedback for a wrong choice is the explanation's first sentence unless given.

### Set A. Solid, liquid or gas? (Sprout, Seedling)

**A1 (Sprout, guided).** Steps shown first: 1. Does it keep its shape? 2. Does it pour into the cup's shape?
- Prompt: Juice in a cup. Solid, liquid or gas?
- Choices: solid (`judges-by-container`) · liquid ✔ · gas (`gas-everyday`)
- Hint: Tip the cup in your mind. Does the juice keep its shape?
- Explanation: Juice flows and takes the cup's shape. That makes it a liquid.

**A2 (Sprout).**
- Prompt: An ice cube on a plate. Solid, liquid or gas?
- Choices: liquid (`made-of-water-so-liquid`) · gas (`random`) · solid ✔
- Hint: Does it keep its cube shape on the plate?
- Explanation: Ice keeps its own shape. It's a solid, made of water.

**A3 (Seedling).**
- Prompt: Flour pours like water. Is flour a liquid?
- Choices: No, each grain is solid ✔ · Yes, because it pours (`powder-is-liquid`) · Yes, because it's soft (`soft-not-solid`)
- Hint: Look at one grain with a magnifier. Does it keep its shape?
- Explanation: Each grain of flour keeps its shape. A pile pours because the grains slide.

**A4 (Seedling).**
- Prompt: You pour water from a tall jug into a wide tray. What changes?
- Choices: The amount of water (`shape-means-amount`) · Its shape ✔ · Nothing at all (`no-change`)
- Hint: Pour it back into the jug. Is it all still there?
- Explanation: A liquid takes the container's shape. The amount stays the same.

**A5 (Seedling).**
- Prompt: The white cloud above the oven door. What is it?
- Choices: Tiny drops of liquid water ✔ · Water vapor, the gas (`steam-cloud-is-gas`) · Smoke from the bread (`cloud-is-smoke`)
- Hint: Water vapor is invisible. Can you see the cloud?
- Explanation: You can see the cloud, so it's tiny drops. The vapor itself is invisible.

### Set B. Undo or not? (Seedling, Sapling)

**B1 (Seedling, guided).** Steps shown first: 1. What happened to it? 2. Can you make it go back? 3. Is it the same stuff?
- Prompt: Chocolate melts on the sill. Can that be undone?
- Choices: No, melting is forever (`melting-irreversible`) · Only if you add sugar (`needs-ingredient`) · Yes, cool it and it sets ✔
- Hint: Put the melted chocolate in the cold larder. What happens?
- Explanation: Cooled chocolate sets solid again. It's still chocolate, so melting can be undone.

**B2 (Seedling).**
- Prompt: Bread dough is baked into a loaf. Can that be undone?
- Choices: No, the loaf is something new ✔ · Yes, cool it down (`all-reversible`) · Yes, add water and squash it (`all-reversible`)
- Hint: Does the loaf have a new smell, color or crust?
- Explanation: Baking makes new substances. Nothing turns bread back into dough.

**B3 (Seedling).**
- Prompt: Ice warms into water. Sugar stirs into water. Which one melted?
- Choices: The sugar (`dissolve-is-melt`) · The ice ✔ · Both of them (`dissolve-is-melt`)
- Hint: Which one needed warmth? Which one needed water to go into?
- Explanation: Ice melted from warmth. The sugar dissolved: it spread through the water.

**B4 (Seedling).** Bramble's potion.
- Prompt: Bramble says her stirred-in sugar disappeared. What do you say?
- Choices: It's still there, dissolved ✔ · She's right, it's gone (`gone-when-invisible`) · It melted into the potion (`dissolve-is-melt`)
- Hint: What did the scale read before and after?
- Explanation: The scale read the same. The sugar is spread through the potion, too small to see.

**B5 (Sapling).**
- Prompt: Both used heat. Which change can be undone?
- Choices: Cooking an egg (`all-reversible`) · Toasting bread (`all-reversible`) · Melting butter ✔
- Hint: Which one makes something new? Which just changes state?
- Explanation: Butter melts and sets again. The egg and toast became something new.

**B6 (Sapling).** Sort the cards into "Can be undone" and "Can't be undone": water freezing · sugar dissolving · a log burning (picture only) · an egg cooking · a puddle drying · a cake baking.
- Answer: Can be undone: water freezing, sugar dissolving, a puddle drying. Can't be undone: a log burning, an egg cooking, a cake baking.
- Misconceptions tested: `melting-irreversible` (freezing placed as can't), `gone-when-invisible` (sugar or puddle placed as can't), `all-reversible` (cooking or baking placed as can).
- Hint: For each, ask: was something new made?
- Explanation: Freezing, dissolving and drying can go back. Burning, cooking and baking make new substances.

**B7 (Sapling, true / not true).** Read each and choose True or Not true.
- "If heat was used, it can't be undone." Not true (`heat-means-irreversible`): melting chocolate uses heat and can be undone.
- "Dissolving is a kind of melting." Not true (`dissolve-is-melt`): dissolving needs a liquid to spread into; melting needs warmth.
- "Melted ice is still water." True.
- Hint: Think of one example for each sentence.
- Explanation: The test is whether a new substance is made, not whether heat was used.

### Set C. The scale and the window (Seedling, Sapling, Keeper's Desk)

All C items use the scale model: zeroed with the empty jar, exact grams.

**C2 (Seedling).**

- Hint: The total is water plus sugar. Take the water away.
- Explanation: 180 g total minus 150 g water leaves 30 g of sugar, still in the jar.

**C4 (Seedling, the pages, number answer).** A simplified picture: made-up page masses.

- Hint: The paper stays. Only the water goes.

**C5 (Sapling, the open jar).**

**C7 (Seedling, thermometer reading).** Picture: a thermometer marked every 10 °C; the liquid stops halfway between 20 and 30.

- Answer: 25 °C. Worked steps: the marks go 20, 30; halfway is 25.

- Hint: Find the marks just below and just above the top of the liquid.

**C8 (Sapling, freezing and boiling).**

- Explanation: At sea level, pure water boils at 100 °C. That's 100 degrees above freezing.
- Keeper's note: Willowbank is in the mountains. Higher up, water boils a little below 100 °C.

### Set E. Keeper's Desk

**E1 (particle explanation).**
- Prompt: Why does a liquid flow, in the particle picture?
- Choices: Its particles are melting (`particles-change`) · Its particles slide past each other ✔ · There's air between them (`air-between-particles`)
- Hint: Look at the liquid box. Are the dots in rows?
- Explanation: Liquid particles are close but not fixed. They slide past each other.

**E2 (clues to a new substance).**
- Prompt: Which is a clue that baking made a new substance?
- Choices: The loaf got warm (`heat-means-irreversible`) · It's a different shape (`shape-means-new`) · It has a new smell and crust ✔
- Hint: Melting chocolate gets warm too, and changes shape. What's different here?
- Explanation: A new smell and a crust are clues. Warmth and shape change happen with melting too.

**E3 (clues, not proof).**
- Prompt: A drink fizzes when opened. Has a new substance formed?
- Choices: Yes, bubbles mean new stuff (`one-clue-proves`) · Not from this clue alone ✔ · No, gases don't count as stuff (`gas-not-matter`)
- Hint: Was the gas there before you opened it?
- Explanation: The gas was already dissolved in the drink. One clue is not proof.

**E4 (say why, read by a grown-up; never graded).**
- Prompt: Use a particle picture to explain how sugar can "disappear" yet still weigh something.
- Grown-up guide: A strong answer says the sugar particles are still there, spread among the water particles; the jar's mass is the same because no particles left; the particles stayed the same size. Note it's a model.

**Optional "say it" prompt (any depth, for a grown-up to hear):** Find a solid, a liquid and a gas in the bakery picture. Say how you know.

## "I'm stuck" ladder

**Step 1, hint:** the item's own hint.

**Step 2, an easier case and how to start:**
- States items: "Try this one first: a stone. Does it keep its shape? Yes, so it's a solid. Now ask the same question about yours."
- Undo items: "Try this one first: ice melting. Cool it and it freezes. Same water, so it can be undone. Now ask: can yours go back?"
- Melt/dissolve items: "Try this one first: a snowman in the sun. Only warmth, no water to go into. That's melting. Now look at yours."
- Mass items: "Try this one first: 10 g water and 2 g salt, lid on. 10 + 2 = 12 g. Now add your two masses."
- Separating items: "Try this one first: pebbles and water. Pebbles are big, so a sieve works. Now ask: how big are your pieces? Are they dissolved?"
- Particle items: "Try this one first: solid. Dots packed in rows. Now find what's different in yours."

**Step 3:** Skip for now. You can come back any time.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Solid:** keeps its own shape.
- **Liquid:** flows and takes the shape of its container.
- **Gas:** spreads out to fill the space. (In science, "gas" doesn't mean fuel.)
- **Melt:** a solid warms and becomes a liquid.
- **Freeze (set):** a liquid cools and becomes a solid.
- **Evaporate:** a liquid becomes an invisible gas, called vapor.
- **Condense:** a gas cools and becomes liquid drops, like mist on a cold window.
- **Water vapor:** water as a gas. You can't see it.
- **Boil:** a liquid gets hot enough to make bubbles of vapor all through it.
- **Dissolve:** a substance spreads through another to make a solution. Our bench uses solids in water; gases can dissolve too.
- **Solution:** a liquid with something dissolved in it.
- **Mixture:** two or more things mixed together that can be separated.
- **Reversible:** can be undone; you can get the first stuff back.
- **Irreversible:** can't be undone; something new was made.
- **Mass:** how much stuff there is. A scale measures it in grams.
- **Sieve / filter:** tools with holes. A sieve has bigger holes; filter paper has tiny ones.
- **Particle:** a piece of matter far too small to see. Scientists use particle pictures as a model.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** solids, liquids and gases by how they behave; melting, freezing, evaporating, condensing and dissolving; changes that can and can't be undone, judged by whether something new was made; mass staying the same in a closed jar; choosing a sieve, filter or evaporation to separate a mixture; and, for older explorers, particle pictures as a model.

**What it does not claim:** the bench pictures are simplified and authored, not simulations or measurements. Scale numbers are exact for clarity; real scales vary slightly. Real chocolate and butter melt over a range of temperatures, and melted butter can separate as it sets. The bench does not teach full chemistry, the water cycle, or gas-producing reactions. "Clues" to a new substance are clues, not proof.

**Safety:** nobody tastes, eats, heats or burns anything through this bench. Burning appears only as a picture. Any real kitchen follow-up, especially anything with an oven, hob, kettle or steam, is for a grown-up to lead. The off-screen card uses only sun, shade and cold.
