## Card 1. Salt and blue: what the ocean is

**Teach (A true thing about the ocean):** The ocean is a huge body of salt water. It covers most of Earth. There is more ocean than land.

**Worked example (tap to reveal):** Look at the globe.
1. Blue shows water. Green and brown show land.
2. Spin it. Blue is almost everywhere.
3. So there is more ocean than land.

**Teach 2:** Sea water is salty, so it's not safe to drink. Brook, lake and rain water are fresh.

**Teach 3:** The ocean is home to more kinds of living things than you could count. Tap below the waves.
**Dive-in scene labels:** whale · fish · crab · jellyfish · seaweed · seahorse · starfish

**Guided (Seedling):** 71 of every 100 parts of Earth are ocean. How many are land?
Steps shown: 1. All the parts: 100. 2. Ocean: 71. 3. 100 − 71 = ?
Answer: 29.

## Card 2. Sand, rock and mud: coasts

**Teach:** A coast is where land meets the sea. Some coasts are sandy, some rocky, some muddy.

**Worked example:**
1. Find where the land touches the water. That line is the coast.
2. What is this shore made of? Rock.
3. So this is a rocky coast.

**Teach 2:** Each kind of shore suits different animals. Crabs hide in rock cracks. Wading birds dig worms from mud.

**Contrast card:** A brook's edge is a bank, not a coast. A coast is beside the sea.

**Heron card (A true thing about birds):** Real gray herons hunt in shallow water on coasts and estuaries, as well as rivers. They stand still, then strike.

**Guided (Sprout):** Tap the line where the land meets the sea.
Steps shown: 1. Find the water. 2. Find the land. 3. Tap where they touch.

## Card 3. Twice a day: tides

**Teach:** The sea rises and falls twice a day at most coasts. These are tides. The Moon's pull mainly causes them.

**Worked example (on the Tide clock):**
1. High tide is at 06:10.
2. High tides come about 12 hours 25 minutes apart.
3. 06:10 + 12 hours = 18:10.
4. 18:10 + 25 minutes = 18:35. The next high tide.

**Teach 2:** Low tide comes about halfway between two high tides. That's about 6 hours 12 minutes after high tide.

**Teach 3 (Sapling):** Two tides take about 24 hours 50 minutes. So tomorrow's tides come about 50 minutes later.

**Teach 4, tide and wave:** Wind makes waves. The Moon mainly makes tides. On a still day, the tide still comes in.

**Teach 5, currents (Sapling):** Currents are huge steady flows in the ocean. They carry water and warmth around the world.

**Teach 6, spring and neap (Sapling):** At new and full Moon, the Sun's pull adds to the Moon's. Tides are bigger: spring tides.

**Spring tide note:** "Spring" here means the tide springs up. It happens in every season, about twice a month.

**Neap note:** About a week later, the Sun and Moon pull at right angles. Tides are smaller: neap tides.

**Keeper's note (optional, older):** There are two bulges of water on Earth, one facing the Moon and one on the far side. A coast turns through both each lunar day, which is why most places get two high tides. Real coasts and sea floors bend the timing.

### Tide clock text

**Bench title:** The Tide Clock
**Always shown:** A simplified picture. Real tides vary from place to place and day to day.
**Controls:** +1 hour · −1 hour · Ordinary tide · Spring tide · Neap tide · Move first high tide
**Markers:** high tide · low tide
**Pool line (when the water drops below the pool rim):** The rock pool is showing.
**Predict prompt (Seedling):** When is the next high tide? Set the clock, then check.
**After checking (right):** The water's at the top again. About 12 hours 25 minutes later.
**After checking (not yet):** Look at the high-tide marker on the clock. How far is it from 06:10?
**About the model (Details):** In this picture the water rises and falls in a smooth repeating curve. It moves slowest near high and low tide, and fastest halfway. Real beaches have weather, wind and coast shapes this picture leaves out. Never use it to plan a trip to the shore.

## Card 4. The pool left behind: rock pools

**Teach:** When the tide goes out, water stays in hollows in the rocks. A rock pool is a tiny sea home.

**Worked example:**
1. High tide: the sea covers the rocks.
2. The tide goes out.
3. Water stays in a hollow. Animals stay too. That's a rock pool.

**Teach 2:** At low tide the pool can get warm, and rocks around it dry out. Each animal has a way to cope.

**First card on the bench:** Look closely, but don't grab. On a real shore, put back anything you move.

## Card 5. Who blows at the top? Mammal or fish

**Teach:** Whales and dolphins are mammals. They breathe air at the surface and feed their babies milk.

**Worked example (dolphin):**
1. Does it breathe air? Yes, through a blowhole.
2. Does it feed its babies milk? Yes.
3. So a dolphin is a mammal, even though it lives in the sea.

**Contrast (shark):**
1. Does it breathe with gills? Yes.
2. Gills are the best fish clue.
3. So a shark is a fish.

**Bench title:** Mammal or fish?
**Clue flaps:** Breathes air, or uses gills? · Feeds babies milk?
**Animal cards:** dolphin · harbor porpoise · gray seal · humpback whale · shark · mackerel · seahorse
**Say-why prompt (a grown-up reads):** Pick one card. Tell your grown-up which clue you used.

## Card 6. Tiny green drifters: food chains and webs

**Teach:** Ocean food starts with phytoplankton: tiny drifting living things that make food from sunlight.

**Teach 2, the arrow:** In a food chain, an arrow means "is eaten by". It points to the eater.

**Worked example (build it):**
1. Phytoplankton → zooplankton. Read it: phytoplankton is eaten by zooplankton.
2. Zooplankton → small fish.
3. Small fish → shark.
4. Read the whole chain aloud, along the arrows.

**Teach 3, webs (Sapling):** Most sea animals eat more than one thing. Join chains together and you get a food web.

**Web cards:** phytoplankton · zooplankton · small fish · squid · seal · shark
**Read-back when an arrow points the other way:** This arrow says the {eater} is eaten by the {eaten}. Is that right?
**What if? button:** Take one away
**What if? prompt:** The {card} is gone. Follow every arrow. What may happen along each one?
**After a removal:** Some animals may have less to eat. Some may have fewer animals eating them. In a real sea, many things change at once.
**Chain note:** A chain shows one path. Real animals eat many things, so real seas make webs.

## Card 7. How deep? Depth zones

**Teach:** Light fades as you go down in the sea. Scientists name zones by how much light there is.

**Worked example (the strip, 1 cm for every 100 m):**
1. Sunlight zone: the top 200 m. That's 2 cm on the strip.
2. Twilight zone: 200 to 1,000 m. 8 cm.
3. Midnight zone: below 1,000 m. Dark all the way.
4. Look how thin the sunlit part is.

**Teach 2:** Phytoplankton need light, so they live near the top. Most sea animals live there too.

**Teach 3:** Some animals live deep in the dark, too. They find food in other ways.

**Strip labels:** Sunlight zone (0 to about 200 m) · Twilight zone (about 200 to 1,000 m) · Midnight zone (below about 1,000 m)
**Strip note:** The zones fade into each other. There's no wall between them.

## Card 8. Five names, one water

**Teach:** People named five oceans: Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Arctic and Southern. They're all joined as one water.

**Worked example (trace on the globe):**
1. Put your finger in the Atlantic.
2. Slide south, then east, without lifting.
3. You're in the Indian Ocean. No wall in the way.

**Teach 2:** The Southern Ocean goes all round Antarctica. Many maps have shown it as an ocean only recently.

**Teach 3:** The Arctic is a real ocean. Its ice floats on sea water.

**Teach 4:** A sea is usually a smaller part of an ocean. The Mediterranean Sea joins the Atlantic.

**Map strip (if no globe):** Spin the map. The Pacific on the left edge joins the Pacific on the right.

## Card 9. Two seas, two tides (Keeper's Desk)

**Teach (A true thing about the ocean):** The Mediterranean has small tides, often about 30 cm. On the Bay of Biscay, big tides reach about 3.5 to 6 meters.

**Teach 2:** The Pyrenees run between the Atlantic at one end and the Mediterranean at the other. Same mountains, very different tides.

**Teach 3:** The shape of each sea and coast changes its tides. So every place needs its own tide table.

### Set A. Tide times

**A1 (Sprout, order).** Put the beach pictures in order, starting at high tide.
Cards: water high · water going out · water low · water coming in.
Answer: high, going out, low, coming in.
Hint: After the water is high, which way does it go next?
Explanation: The sea goes out, stays low a little while, then comes back in. Then it does it all again.

**A2 (Sprout, 3-choice).** How many high tides does this beach have in about a day?
- one *(misconception: tide comes once a day)*: Count the high-water pictures on the clock again. There are more.
- **two**: Yes. Two high tides, about 12 hours 25 minutes apart.
- four *(misconception: counts highs and lows together)*: You counted the lows too. How many are high?
Hint: Look for the high-tide marks on the clock.
Explanation: Most coasts have two high tides and two low tides in a little over a day.

**A3 (Seedling, 3-choice).** What mainly makes the tide go in and out?
- the wind *(misconception: wind causes tides)*: Wind makes waves. On a still day, the tide still comes in.
- the brook flowing in *(misconception: the sea is a big brook)*: Rivers add fresh water, but they don't make tides.
- **the Moon's pull**: Right. The Moon's pull is the main cause. The Sun helps a little.
Hint: Think of something big in the sky.
Explanation: The Moon's gravity pulls on the ocean. The Sun's pull adds to it.

**A4 (Seedling, guided number).** High tide is at 06:10. When is the next high tide?
Steps shown: 1. High tides are 12 h 25 min apart. 2. 06:10 + 12 h = 18:10. 3. 18:10 + 25 min = ?
Answer: 18:35.
Hint: Add the hours first, then the minutes.
Explanation: 06:10 plus 12 hours 25 minutes is 18:35, or 6:35 in the evening.

**A5 (Sapling, number).** High tide is at 06:10. When is the low tide after it?
Answer: about 12:22 (accept 12:22 or 12:23).
Hint: Low tide is about halfway: about 6 hours 12 minutes later.
Explanation: Half of 12 h 25 min is 6 h 12½ min. 06:10 + 6 h 12½ min is about 12:22.

**A6 (Sapling, number, across midnight).** High tide is at 18:35. When is the next low tide?
Answer: about 00:47 the next day (accept 00:47 or 00:48).
Hint: Add 6 hours first. Then add 12 minutes. Watch for midnight.
Explanation: 18:35 + 6 h = 00:35 the next day. Add 12½ minutes: about 00:47.

**A7 (Sapling, 3-choice).** Today's morning high tide is at 06:10. When is tomorrow's?
- 06:10 *(misconception: tides come at the same time every day)*: Tides slip later each day, by about 50 minutes.
- 05:20 *(misconception: tides move earlier)*: Close, but the wrong way. Tides come later each day.
- **07:00**: Yes. Two tides take 24 h 50 min, so it's 50 minutes later.
Hint: Two tides take about 24 hours 50 minutes.
Explanation: 06:10 + 24 h 50 min = 07:00 the next morning.

**A8 (Keeper's Desk, number).** High tide is at 21:40. When is the next high tide?
Answer: 10:05 the next day.
Hint: 21:40 + 12 h is past midnight. Count on carefully.
Explanation: 21:40 + 12 h = 09:40 the next day. Add 25 minutes: 10:05.

**A9 (Keeper's Desk, number).** How many minutes are in 12 hours 25 minutes?
Answer: 745.
Hint: 12 × 60, then add 25.
Explanation: 12 × 60 = 720, and 720 + 25 = 745. Half of 745 is 372.5 minutes, the gap to low tide.

**C10 (Keeper's Desk, say-why, a grown-up reads).** Setup: Harbor A's tide table says high tide at 06:10.
Question: Why can't Harbor B use Harbor A's table?
Model answer: Each sea and coast has its own shape, so tides come at different times and heights. A table only works for its own place.
Hint: Think of the two seas by the Pyrenees.
Explanation: Tides depend on the Moon, but the timing and size depend on the place. That's why every harbor needs its own table.

## I'm stuck worked examples

- **Tide times (A4 to A8):** Easier one: high tide at 01:00. Add 12 h: 13:00. Add 25 min: 13:25. To start yours: add 12 hours to your time first.
- **Low tide (A5, A6):** Easier one: high tide at 00:00. Low is about 6 h 12 min later: about 06:12. To start yours: add 6 hours first.
- **Across midnight (A6, A8):** Easier one: 23:00 + 2 h. 24:00 is midnight, so it's 01:00 the next day. To start yours: count up to midnight first.
- **Percent (B3, B4):** Easier one: 60 of 100 are blue. Then 40 are not. 60 in 100 is 6 in 10. To start yours: write 71 out of 100.
- **Depth (B5, B7, B8):** Easier one: a zone from 100 m to 300 m is 300 − 100 = 200 m thick. To start yours: take the top from the bottom.
- **Arrows (C4 to C6):** Easier one: leaf → caterpillar. Say "the leaf is eaten by the caterpillar." To start yours: say your arrow the same way.
- **Mammal or fish (C7, C8):** Easier one: a cow breathes air and feeds milk. Mammal. To start yours: open the clue flaps.
- **Rock pool (C1 to C3):** Easier one: a snail on a dry path pulls into its shell. To start yours: think what keeps each animal wet.
- **Times bigger (C9):** Easier one: 6 is how many times 2? 6 ÷ 2 = 3. To start yours: divide the big range by the small one.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| ocean | The huge body of salt water that covers most of Earth. |
| sea | A smaller part of the ocean, often partly closed in by land. |
| coast | The place where land meets the sea. |
| tide | The sea slowly rising and falling, about twice a day. |
| high tide | When the sea has come in as far as it will. |
| low tide | When the sea has gone out as far as it will. |
| spring tide | A bigger tide, at new Moon and full Moon. |
| neap tide | A smaller tide, about a week after a spring tide. |
| wave | A bump of moving water, mostly made by wind. |
| current | A huge steady flow of water in the ocean. |
| rock pool | Sea water left in rocks when the tide goes out. |
| habitat | The place where a plant or animal lives and finds what it needs. |
| tentacle | A long bendy arm some sea animals use to catch food. |
| phytoplankton | Tiny drifting living things that make food from sunlight. |
| zooplankton | Tiny drifting animals. Many eat phytoplankton. |
| food chain | A line showing what is eaten by what. |
| food web | Food chains joined together, because animals eat many things. |
| mammal | An animal that breathes air and feeds its babies milk. |
| gills | Body parts fish use to breathe in water. |
| blowhole | The breathing hole on top of a whale or dolphin's head. |
| sunlight zone | The top of the sea, where light reaches. |
| twilight zone | Deeper water where light fades. |
| midnight zone | Deep water with no sunlight at all. |

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** that the ocean is salt water covering most of Earth (about 71%), one connected water with five names; coasts; tides about every 12 hours 25 minutes, mainly from the Moon, with spring and neap tides; rock pools and one coping move per animal; food-chain arrows meaning "is eaten by"; whales and dolphins as mammals; light and depth zones (about 200 m and 1,000 m).

**What it does not claim:** the Tide clock is a simplified picture, not a tide table, and gives no safety advice for any real shore. Real tides vary by place, weather and the Moon's phase. Food-web removals are "may", not predictions. The postcards, H., Willowbank and the coast town are made up; the ocean facts are real and sourced.

**Sources in brief:** NOAA National Ocean Service and NOAA Ocean Explorer (tides, five oceans, ocean zones), Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution (zones), National Geographic (Southern Ocean, 2021), Britannica (Bay of Biscay, Pyrenees), the Wildlife Trusts and MarLIN (rock-pool animals), the RSPB (waders), Cornell Lab of Ornithology (herons). Full list in the source audit.

**Off-screen idea (adult-led, optional):** if you visit a rocky shore, check the local tide table first, go on a falling tide with an adult, watch for slippery rocks and incoming water, and look without collecting. Turn any rock back the way it was. Never let a child taste sea water. No visit is needed for anything in the game.
