# Where the Mill's Energy Goes: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 147](../specs/147-mills-energy.md). Use as written, after independent spec review and a physics subject check. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion and is never gendered. The miller is she/her; Pip is he/him; Bramble is she/her and has a magpie. Real-world facts were checked against the sources in the [source audit](../source-audits/147-mills-energy.md); the physics still needs the subject check. Nothing here is scored or timed.

## Labels used on every card

Each card carries one label, always in the same place:

- **A story from Willowbank**: the town, the mill, the brook, the miller and every character are made up.
- **A true thing about science**: checked science, explained simply.
- **A true thing from history**: something people really did or wrote.
- **A made-up model**: a simple game rule, shown on the card, that is not real measurement.

## Arriving

**Place title:** The Energy Bench

**First visit** (A story from Willowbank):
> Beside the mill wheel there's a bench with a slate, a jug of water and a tray of little copper tokens. The miller has chalked a question on the slate: *Where does the go come from?*

**Later visits:** The tokens are back in their tray. The slate still asks its question.

**Four doors on the bench:** Follow the energy · The wheel bench · Perpetual-motion detective · Sort the sources

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. The jammed wheel (Act I, chapter 4: mend the village)

**Hook:** After the storm, branches jam the mill wheel. The brook rushes past, and nothing turns.

**Short:**
> The wheel is stuck. Pip watches the brook rush by. "All that go, going nowhere," he says. "Where does it come from?"

**Longer:**
> The storm has jammed branches between the paddles of the mill wheel. The brook still rushes down the millrace, loud and brown, but the wheel won't move. No turning wheel means no turning millstones, and no flour for the market.
>
> The miller sits on the step with her cat. "We'll have those branches out by noon," she says.
>
> Pip watches the water. "All that go, going nowhere. Where does the go come from? Is it in the water? Can it run out?"
>
> "Good questions," says the miller. "The water has energy because it's moving. Before that, it was high up the valley. And before that..." She points at the sky.
>
> {guide} hands you a copper token. "Let's follow it," {guide} says. "From the very start to the very end."
>
> The cat watches the token as if it might be a snack.

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about science):
> Scientists say energy is kept in **stores** and moves between them by **transfers**. Water high in the hills has energy in a gravitational store. As it runs downhill, energy moves into its kinetic store: the store of moving things. The brook pushes the wheel, and energy moves on again. Energy is never made or destroyed. It moves and spreads out.

### Scene 2. Bramble's wheel that never stops (Act II, chapter 1: the weekly market)

**Hook:** Bramble has drawn a wheel that fills its own buckets. "No brook needed," she says.

**Short:**
> Bramble's wheel pumps its own water back to the top. "It will turn forever," she says. Will it?

**Longer:**
> The wheel is mended, and the market wants flour. Bramble unrolls a drawing across the mill bench. Her magpie holds down one corner.
>
> "The trouble with your wheel," she tells the miller, "is that it needs a brook. Mine doesn't." In her drawing, water falls onto a wheel. The wheel turns a screw pump. The pump lifts the same water back up to the top tank, and round it goes. "It never stops. Free flour, forever."
>
> The miller looks at the drawing for a long time. "It's a beautiful drawing," she says. "Before we build it, let's ask the energy."
>
> "Ask the energy what?" says Pip.
>
> "Where it goes," says {guide}. "Every single turn."

**Keeper's note** (A true thing from history):
> People have drawn machines like this for hundreds of years. In 1618 the English physician Robert Fludd published a picture of a mill whose wheel pumps its own water back up. No machine like it has ever kept going. Two ideas explain why: no machine gives out more energy than it takes in, and every real machine spreads some energy out as heat and sound.

**After the detective** (Short):
> "So it slows down a little every turn," says Bramble. "Then stops," says Pip. Bramble keeps the drawing anyway.

**After the detective** (Longer):
> Bramble reads the token count twice. "So every turn, a bit of the energy warms the axle and the pump, and a bit goes off as noise."
>
> "And the pump can't lift back all the water that fell," says {guide}. "So there's less each time."
>
> "Then it stops," says Pip, who is pleased to get there first.
>
> Bramble rolls up the drawing. "I'm keeping it. It's still a beautiful drawing." The magpie agrees.
>
> "It is," says the miller. "And now we know why the brook matters."

### Scene 3. Too much go (Act VI, chapter 3: the long rain)

**Hook:** Days of rain upstream. The brook runs high and fast, carrying far more energy than the wheel needs.

**Short:**
> The brook roars. The miller lowers the sluice gate and sends extra water past the wheel. "Too much go," she says.

**Longer:**
> After days of rain upstream, Willow Brook is high and fast. Down the millrace, the water is racing.
>
> "If we sent all of that onto the wheel," says the miller, "it would spin far too fast. It could crack a paddle. More energy isn't always better. A wheel needs the right amount."
>
> She and her cat check the sluice gate together. She lowers it, and most of the water rushes down the spill channel, past the wheel and back into the brook. The wheel turns at its usual steady pace.
>
> Pip's eyes are wide. "So the water has more go than ever?"
>
> "Much more," says {guide}. "Faster water, and lots more of it."
>
> By the mill, the flood post shows the water rising. The mill holds. The wheel keeps turning, steady as ever.

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about science):
> The energy a stream can hand over each second grows with how much water flows and how far it falls. Faster water also carries more energy. Mills and hydroelectric stations have gates and spill channels so they can control how much water reaches the machinery and send extra water past it.

## Teach cards

Each card is one idea. Worked examples reveal one step per tap.

**T1. Energy makes things happen** (Sprout and up)
> Energy is what makes things happen: moving, warming, lighting up, making sound. Energy is never made from nothing. It moves from one thing to another.

Worked example: *Where does the wheel's energy come from?*
1. The brook is moving.
2. The brook pushes the paddles.
3. The wheel turns. Energy moved from the water to the wheel.

**T2. Waiting, moving, traveling** (Sprout and up)
> Energy can be **waiting** in something: water high up, grain, wood. A **moving** thing has energy too. Energy can **travel** between things as light, sound or heating.

Worked example: *Sort three things from the mill.*
1. Water in the high pond is waiting. Its energy is stored.
2. The turning wheel is moving. It has movement energy.
3. The mill's hum is sound. Energy is traveling away through the air.

**T3. Faster means more** (Seedling and up)
> The same water has more movement energy when it moves faster. You can tell: fast water pushes the paddles harder and turns the wheel faster.

Worked example: *A slow brook and a fast brook.*
1. Same wheel. Same amount of water.
2. The fast brook turns the wheel faster.
3. So the fast water carried more energy.

**T4. A push is a force** (Seedling and up)
> A force is a push or a pull. The water pushes each paddle: that's a force. Energy is what moves from the water into the wheel while it pushes.

**T5. Friction warms things** (Seedling and up)
> Friction is a force between surfaces that rub. It slows things down, and it warms them. When you rub your hands together fast, friction warms them.

Worked example: *Why does the miller grease the axle?*
1. The axle rubs in its bearing as it turns.
2. Friction there warms the axle and makes a squeak.
3. Grease means less rubbing, so more energy reaches the millstones.

**T6. Friction can help, too** (Seedling and up)
> Friction isn't always a waste. It lets your feet grip the path. Millstones have grooved faces. The grooves cut the grain and carry it to the edge.

**T7. Never used up, only spread out** (Sapling and up)
> Energy is never used up. It spreads out. Each turn, the stones and axle warm and the mill hums. That energy still exists, spread thin and hard to use.

Worked example: *The wheel stops when the gate shuts.*
1. Start: the wheel is moving. It has movement energy.
2. Friction slows it. The axle, stones and air warm slightly.
3. End: the wheel is still. Its energy is now spread out as warmth.

**T8. Two ways to say it** (Sapling and up)
> Everyday words: movement energy, heat energy, light energy. Scientists' words: energy **stores** (where it's kept) and **transfers** (how it moves). They describe the same energy.

Matching panel (shown under T8):

| Everyday words | Scientists' words |
| --- | --- |
| Energy of something high up | Gravitational store |
| Movement energy | Kinetic store |
| Heat energy in something warm | Thermal store |
| Energy in food, grain or wood | Chemical store |
| Light energy, sound energy | Ways energy travels (transfers), not stores |
| Electrical energy | A way energy travels along wires, not a store |

**T9. One step at a time** (Sapling and up)
> Look at one step at a time. Name where the energy starts and where it ends. The rest is the machine at work: paddles, axle, gears.

Worked example: *The brook turns the wheel.*
1. Start: the moving water's kinetic store.
2. How it moves: the water pushes the paddles.
3. End: the turning wheel's kinetic store. Some water splashes and warms a little.

**T10. No machine gives out more than it takes in** (Sapling and up)
> A machine can't give out more energy than goes in. And every real machine spreads some out as heat and sound. So no machine can keep itself going forever.

**T11. Efficiency** (Keeper's Desk)
> Efficiency compares the useful energy out with all the energy in. 100 units in and 60 useful out means 60 out of 100: 60% efficient.

Worked example: *A wheel takes in 50 units and does 40 units of useful grinding.*
1. Useful out is 40. Total in is 50.
2. 40 ÷ 50 = 0.8.
3. 0.8 is 80%. The other 10 units spread out as heat and sound.

**T12. A Sankey diagram** (Keeper's Desk)
> A Sankey diagram is an arrow that splits. Its width shows how much energy. The useful branch and the spread-out branch together are as wide as the arrow in.

Worked example: *Draw 100 units in, 60 useful.*
1. Scale: 1 square wide = 10 units. The arrow in is 10 squares wide.
2. The useful branch is 6 squares wide.
3. The spread-out branch is 4 squares wide. 6 + 4 = 10.

**T13. Renewable sources** (Seedling and up)
> A renewable source is refilled by nature quickly enough for people to keep using it: sunshine, wind, flowing water. Coal, oil and gas took millions of years to form.

**T14. Renewable isn't free** (Sapling and up)
> The brook's water costs nothing to buy. But wheels, stones and repairs cost a lot. And every source changes something: a dam changes a river for fish.

## Activity (a): Follow the energy

**Card:** Follow one token of energy from the Sun to the mill. Where does it end up?

**Stations** (each one a separate card; tap to move the token on):

| # | Station | Card line |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | The Sun | Sunshine warms the sea, lakes and wet ground. Some water evaporates and rises as invisible vapor. |
| 2 | The hills | High up, the vapor cools into clouds. Rain and snow fall on the hills. That water is high up: its energy is waiting. |
| 3 | The brook | The water runs downhill and speeds up. Now it's a rushing brook with movement energy. |
| 4 | The wheel | The brook pushes the paddles. The wheel and axle turn. |
| 5 | The millstones | The axle turns the top millstone. The stones grind grain into flour. |
| 6 | The end | Where are the tokens now? |

**Sprout version:** order the six station cards, then drag one token along them. No numbers.

**Seedling and up, with ten tokens:** from station 4 onward, three small pots wait beside the path: *splash and spray*, *warm axle and squeak*, *warm stones and hum*. At each station you choose how many tokens to drop in a pot (at least one at stations 4 and 5). The rest go on.

**Counter line, always visible:** 10 tokens at the start. {n} on the path, {m} spread out. Still 10 in all.

**At station 6:**
> Grinding warms the stones and flour. So every token ends up spread out as warmth and a little sound. Still 10. None used up.

**The wheat branch** (opens after station 6):
> The Sun did a second job: it grew the wheat. The energy in bread came from sunlight, stored in the grain. The wheel's energy ground the grain, then spread out.

**Older card** (Sapling and up, after the branch):
> We followed one long path to see the whole valley. Scientists usually look at one step at a time: where energy starts and where it ends. Try each station.

Per-station start and end (Sapling, revealed by tap):

| Station | Starts in | Ends in |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 to 2 | The Sun (energy travels as sunlight) | Water lifted as vapor; rain that falls high up has a gravitational store |
| 2 to 3 | Gravitational store of high water | Kinetic store of the moving brook |
| 3 to 4 | Kinetic store of the brook | Kinetic store of the wheel, plus some warming of the water |
| 4 to 5 | Kinetic store of the wheel | Grinding, then the thermal store of the stones, flour and air |

**Guided item (first try, steps shown):**
Prompt: Put the first three stations in order.
Steps shown first: 1. Where does the water get lifted? 2. Where does it fall? 3. Where does it move fast?
Answer: The Sun, the hills, the brook.

**Hint:** Start where the water gets lifted up.

**I'm stuck, worked example:** A ball on a shelf falls to the floor. First it's high up. Then it moves. Then it lands, and the floor warms a tiny bit. *Now start this one:* where is the water high up?

**Skip:** Skip for now. You can come back any time.

## Activity (b): The wheel bench

**Card:** Change the water, the fall and the grease. Watch the flour and the warmth.

**Made-up model line, always visible:**
> A made-up model. Energy in each hour = 10 × water × fall. Friction spreads out some of it: 20%, 40% or 60%. Flour scoops = useful energy ÷ 2. Real mills are more complicated.

**Controls:**
- **Water** (sluice gate notches): 1 · 2 · 3 · 4
- **Fall** (how far the water drops onto the wheel): low (1) · middle (2) · high (3)
- **Axle grease:** fresh grease (20% spread out) · old grease (40%) · dry axle (60%)

**Readout lines:**
- Energy in this hour: {in} units.
- Useful, to the millstones: {useful} units.
- Spread out as heat and sound: {spread} units.
- Flour this hour: {flour} scoops.
- Efficiency (Keeper's Desk only): {useful} out of {in}, so {pct}%.

**Sankey strip** (Sapling and up): an arrow {in} units wide splits into *useful* and *spread out*. Scale shown: 1 square wide = 10 units.

**Picture note:** the wheel turns faster in the picture as energy in goes up. This is a cartoon cue, not a measured speed.

**Dry axle line:** The axle is warm and squeaking. Some energy is warming it instead of grinding.

**Teaching step (first visit, one per tap):**
1. "Start small: water 1, fall low, fresh grease. 10 units in."
2. "Fresh grease spreads out 20%. That's 2 units. 8 units reach the stones."
3. "8 ÷ 2 = 4 scoops of flour this hour."
4. "Now double the water. What do you think happens to the flour?"

**Guided item:**
Prompt: Water 2, low fall, fresh grease. How much flour?
Steps shown: 1. Energy in: 10 × 2 × 1 = ? 2. Take away 20%. 3. Divide by 2.
Answer: 8 scoops (20 in, 4 spread out, 16 useful).

**Hint:** Find the energy in first. Then take away what spreads out.

**I'm stuck, worked example:** Water 1, low fall, fresh grease: 10 in, 2 spread out, 8 useful, 4 scoops. *Now start this one:* what is 10 × 2 × 1?

**Wondering prompts** (open, ungraded): What happens if you double the fall? Which grease grinds the most? Can you make 24 scoops?

## Activity (c): Perpetual-motion detective

**Card:** Three made-up machines claim they never stop. Find where the energy goes.

**Rule card** (A true thing about science):
> Two clues solve every case. One: no machine gives out more energy than goes in. Two: every real machine spreads some energy out as heat and sound.

### Case 1. Bramble's self-filling wheel (A story from Willowbank)

**Machine card:** Water turns the wheel. The wheel turns a pump. The pump lifts the same water back to the top.

**Token view:** 10 tokens of falling water. The wheel passes on 7; 3 spread out at the axle and splash. The pump lifts back water worth 3; 4 more spread out in the pump. *A made-up model.*

**Question:** Why must Bramble's wheel stop?
- A. The water gets tired after going round so many times. *(misconception: energy is a substance that wears out)*
- B. Each turn, some energy spreads out, so less water gets lifted back. *(right)*
- C. The pump runs out of energy because pumps use it up. *(misconception: energy gets used up)*

**Feedback:**
- A: Water doesn't tire. Follow the tokens: where do some go each turn?
- B: Yes. Only 3 of 10 come back. Next turn, even fewer. Then it stops.
- C: Energy isn't used up. It spreads out as warmth and noise. Check the pots.

### Case 2. Ember's fan-mill (A story from Willowbank)

**Machine card:** A little windmill turns a generator. Its electricity runs a fan. The fan blows on the windmill.

**Question:** Why must the fan-mill stop?
- A. The fan blows harder than the windmill turns, so it speeds up. *(misconception: more out than in)*
- B. The generator makes new energy, but too slowly to keep up. *(misconception: machines create energy)*
- C. The fan gets only part of the energy, so it blows weaker. *(right)*

**Feedback:**
- A: No machine gives out more than goes in. The fan only gets part of the windmill's energy.
- B: Generators don't make energy. They pass it on, and some spreads out.
- C: Right. The generator, wires and fan each warm a little. Each loop is weaker.

### Case 3. The tipping-weights wheel (A true thing from history: people drew wheels like this for over 800 years)

**Machine card:** Hinged weights swing out on one side of a wheel. The inventor says that side is always heavier, so it keeps turning.

**Question:** Why does the tipping-weights wheel stop?
- A. More weights on the other side balance the far-out ones. *(right)*
- B. It would keep turning if the hinges were oiled perfectly. *(misconception: friction is the only problem; underclaims the balance)*
- C. Gravity stops pulling on the weights after a few turns. *(misconception: forces run out)*

**Feedback:**
- A: Yes. The wheel settles where it balances, and friction takes the last of its movement.
- B: Even with no friction it wouldn't gain energy. It just settles where it balances.
- C: Gravity keeps pulling. The wheel stops because it balances, and friction warms the hinges.

**Say why** (grown-up reads, never graded): Pick one machine. Tell someone where its energy goes.

**Hint for all cases:** Follow the tokens. Where do some go on each turn?

**I'm stuck, worked example:** A swing slows down if nobody pushes. Each swing, the air and the ropes warm a tiny bit. *Now start this one:* where does the machine warm up?

## Activity (d): Sort the sources

**Card:** Sort each energy source. Will nature refill it soon enough to keep using?

**Bins:** Renewable · Non-renewable · Not a source (it carries energy from one)

| Card | Bin | Feedback when placed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Willow Brook's flowing water | Renewable | Yes. Rain keeps refilling the brook. |
| Wind on the hill | Renewable | Yes. The Sun keeps the air moving. |
| Sunshine | Renewable | Yes. The Sun shines again tomorrow. |
| Firewood from trees that are replanted | Renewable | Yes, if new trees grow. But burning wood makes smoke and carbon dioxide. |
| Coal | Non-renewable | Yes. Coal took millions of years to form. Once burned, it's gone. |
| Oil | Non-renewable | Yes. It formed over millions of years. We use it much faster. |
| Natural gas | Non-renewable | Yes. Like oil, it formed underground over millions of years. |
| Uranium for nuclear power | Non-renewable | Yes. It's mined and runs out. It makes very little carbon dioxide, though. |
| Electricity from the wire | Not a source | Right. Electricity carries energy from a source. Was that source renewable? |

**Wrong-bin feedback (general):** Ask: will nature refill this soon enough for people to keep using it?

**After sorting** (A true thing about science):
> Renewable means nature refills it. It doesn't mean free, and it doesn't mean harmless. Wind turbines and dams cost a lot to build, and dams change rivers.

**A modern comparison card** (A true thing about science):
> Some streams today turn a turbine that spins an electric generator. It takes some of the river's water. The river needs enough left, and fish need a way past.

**Say why** (grown-up reads): Which source would you pick for Willowbank's new lamps? What's one cost?

## Problem sets

Each item lists the prompt, the answer, what each wrong choice tests, a hint and an explanation. Right-answer positions vary. Numbers in sets 3 come from the made-up wheel-bench model or are labeled illustrative.

### Set 1. Follow the energy (Sprout and Seedling)

**1.1 (Sprout, order the cards)**
Prompt: Put these in order, from first to last.
Cards: rain on the hills · the Sun · grinding stones · the rushing brook · the turning wheel
Answer: the Sun → rain on the hills → the rushing brook → the turning wheel → grinding stones.
Misconception tested: starting with the wheel ("the wheel makes the go").
Hint: Where does the water get lifted up?
Explanation: The Sun lifts water, rain falls high up, the brook runs down, and the brook turns the wheel.

**1.2 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which one has energy waiting, stored?
- A. Water in a pond high on the hill *(right)*
- B. The hum of the mill *(misconception: sound is stored energy)*
- C. The turning wheel *(misconception: mixing moving with waiting)*
Hint: Which one isn't moving yet?
Explanation: High water is waiting to flow down. The wheel is already moving, and sound is energy traveling away.

**1.3 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: The wheel stops. Where did its movement energy go?
- A. It was used up and is gone. *(misconception: energy gets used up)*
- B. It warmed the axle and air, and made sound. *(right)*
- C. It flowed back into the brook. *(misconception: energy returns to where it came from)*
Hint: What feels warm after rubbing?
Explanation: Friction slowed the wheel. Its energy spread out as warmth and a little sound.

**1.4 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Same water, moving slowly or fast. Which has more movement energy?
- A. The slow water, because it stays longer. *(misconception: slower is stronger)*
- B. Both the same, because it's the same water. *(misconception: energy depends only on amount)*
- C. The fast water. *(right)*
Hint: Which turns the wheel faster?
Explanation: The same water has more movement energy when it moves faster.

**1.5 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: How can you tell the fast brook carries more energy?
- A. It looks browner and muddier than the slow one. *(misconception: judging by looks, not effects)*
- B. It turns the wheel faster and grinds more flour. *(right)*
- C. It is louder, so it must be heavier. *(misconception: confusing sound with mass)*
Hint: Look at what the water can do.
Explanation: Energy shows in what happens. Faster water makes the wheel do more.

**1.6 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: The miller greases a dry axle. What changes?
- A. Less energy warms the axle, so more reaches the stones. *(right)*
- B. The grease gives the wheel extra energy. *(misconception: lubricant is an energy source)*
- C. Only the squeak stops; the flour stays the same. *(underclaims: ignores the warmth evidence on the bench)*
Hint: Where was the warmth going before?
Explanation: Less friction at the axle means less energy spreads out there, so the stones get more.

**1.7 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Where did the energy stored in bread come from?
- A. The mill wheel, through the stones. *(misconception: grinding puts energy into flour)*
- B. The baker's oven, while it bakes. *(misconception: heating is the food's energy)*
- C. Sunlight, stored by the wheat plant. *(right)*
Hint: What did the Sun help grow?
Explanation: Wheat stores energy from sunlight in its grain. The wheel only ground the grain.

**1.8 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: The water pushes a paddle. Is that push the energy?
- A. Yes. A push and energy are the same thing. *(misconception: force equals energy)*
- B. No. The push is a force; energy moves to the wheel. *(right)*
- C. Yes. The push is stored inside the water. *(misconception: impetus, a stored push)*
Hint: A push is a force. What moves into the wheel?
Explanation: The push is a force acting now. Energy is what the water hands to the wheel.

### Set 2. Stores and transfers (Sapling)

**2.1 (3-choice)**
Prompt: Rainwater high in the hills has energy in which store?

- B. Kinetic store *(misconception: naming the store it will have later)*

**2.2 (3-choice)**

**2.3 (3-choice)**

- B. Sound *(right)*
- C. Thermal *(misconception: stores and transfers confused)*

Explanation: Sound, light and electric current carry energy from place to place. They aren't stores.

**2.4 (3-choice)**

- C. No. It spread out into warm stones, flour and air. *(right)*
Hint: Where did the tokens end at station 6?

**2.5 (3-choice, one step)**

- B. Starts: gravitational store. Ends: kinetic store of the wheel. *(misconception: names a different step)*
- C. Starts: kinetic store. Ends: nowhere; it's gone. *(misconception: energy destroyed)*

**2.6 (Say why, grown-up reads)**
Prompt: Pip says the brook will run out of energy. What would you tell him?
Answer notes: Good answers say the Sun keeps lifting water and rain keeps falling high up, so the brook keeps getting energy; energy isn't used up, it moves on and spreads out.
Misconception tested: "energy runs out" (energy as a substance that is consumed).
Hint: Where does the brook's water come from?
Explanation: The brook is refilled by rain, and the Sun keeps the water moving round.

### Set 3. The wheel bench in numbers (Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

Model rule for this set (A made-up model): energy in = 10 × water × fall; fresh grease spreads out 20%, old grease 40%, dry axle 60%; flour scoops = useful ÷ 2.

**3.1 (Sapling, number, guided)**
Prompt: Water 2, low fall, fresh grease. How many scoops of flour?
Steps: 10 × 2 × 1 = 20 in. 20% of 20 = 4 spread out. 20 − 4 = 16 useful. 16 ÷ 2 = 8.
Answer: 8 scoops.
Misconception tested: forgetting the spread-out part (answer 10).
Hint: Take away what spreads out before dividing.
Explanation: Only the useful energy grinds flour. 16 useful units make 8 scoops.

**3.2 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: Now open the gate to water 4. Same fall and grease. How much flour?
Steps: 10 × 4 × 1 = 40 in. 40 − 8 = 32 useful. 32 ÷ 2 = 16.
Answer: 16 scoops.
Misconception tested: adding 2 instead of doubling (answer 10).
Hint: The water doubled. What happens to the energy in?
Explanation: In this model, twice the water means twice the energy, so twice the flour.

**3.3 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Water 1 with high fall, or water 3 with low fall. Which grinds more?
- A. They grind the same. *(right)*
- B. The high fall, because height always wins. *(misconception: one factor matters more than the rule says)*
- C. Water 3, because more water always wins. *(misconception: one factor matters more than the rule says)*
Hint: Work out 10 × water × fall for each.
Explanation: 10 × 1 × 3 = 30 and 10 × 3 × 1 = 30. Same energy in, same flour.

**3.4 (Keeper's Desk, number with Sankey)**
Prompt: 100 units in, 60 useful. How many spread out? What's the efficiency?
Steps: 100 − 60 = 40 spread out. 60 ÷ 100 = 0.6 = 60%.
Answer: 40 units; 60%.
Misconception tested: treating spread-out energy as destroyed (no branch drawn).
Hint: The two branches must add up to the arrow in.
Explanation: Total in equals useful plus spread out. 60 of 100 is 60%. (Illustrative numbers.)

**3.5 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: Water 3, middle fall, old grease. Find useful energy, flour and efficiency.
Steps: 10 × 3 × 2 = 60 in. 40% of 60 = 24 spread out. 60 − 24 = 36 useful. 36 ÷ 2 = 18 scoops. 36 ÷ 60 = 60%.
Answer: 36 useful units; 18 scoops; 60%.
Misconception tested: using 40% as the efficiency.
Hint: 40% spreads out. What percent is useful?
Explanation: If 40% spreads out, 60% is useful. 60% of 60 is 36.

**3.6 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: A wheel takes in 80 units and is 75% efficient. How many useful?
Steps: 75% of 80 = 0.75 × 80 = 60. Spread out: 80 − 60 = 20.
Answer: 60 useful units (20 spread out).
Misconception tested: subtracting 75 from 80 (answer 5).
Hint: 75% is three quarters.
Explanation: Three quarters of 80 is 60. The other 20 warms things and makes sound. (Illustrative numbers.)

**3.7 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Bramble's chart says: 100 units in, 110 units useful out. What's wrong?
- A. Nothing. A really good wheel can do that. *(misconception: efficiency over 100%)*
- B. More useful energy out than went in. That's impossible. *(right)*
- C. Nothing, as long as the stones are new. *(misconception: better parts make energy)*
Hint: Can a branch be wider than the arrow in?
Explanation: No machine gives out more energy than goes in. Efficiency can never pass 100%.

**3.8 (Keeper's Desk, number, illustrative)**
Prompt: Same water and fall: 100 units in. Overshot gives 60 useful, undershot 30. Difference?
Steps: 60 − 30 = 30. Overshot efficiency 60%; undershot 30%. 60 is twice 30.
Answer: 30 more useful units from the overshot wheel, twice as many.
Misconception tested: thinking wheel design can't change the useful share.
Hint: Subtract the smaller useful number from the larger.
Explanation: Both get the same energy in. The overshot wheel turns more of it into grinding. (Illustrative numbers. In 1759, John Smeaton's model tests found overshot wheels about twice as efficient as undershot ones.)

### Set 4. Sources and the Keeper's Desk (Seedling to Keeper's Desk)

**4.1 (Seedling, sort)**
Prompt: Sort these: flowing water, wind, sunshine, coal, oil, gas.
Answer: Renewable: flowing water, wind, sunshine. Non-renewable: coal, oil, gas.
Misconception tested: sorting by "clean or dirty" or "natural or not" (coal is natural too).
Hint: Will nature refill it soon enough to keep using?
Explanation: Rain, wind and sunshine keep coming. Coal, oil and gas took millions of years to form.

**4.2 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Firewood from trees that are replanted. Which is true?
- A. Renewable, but burning it makes smoke and carbon dioxide. *(right)*
- B. Non-renewable, because anything burned is gone. *(misconception: burning means non-renewable)*
- C. Renewable, and it does no harm at all. *(misconception: renewable means harmless)*
Hint: Will new trees grow? Does burning still make smoke?
Explanation: New trees refill the supply. But renewable doesn't mean harmless.

**4.3 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Is uranium for nuclear power renewable?
- A. Yes, because it makes almost no smoke. *(misconception: low-carbon means renewable)*
- B. Yes, because a power station runs for years. *(misconception: long-lasting means renewable)*
- C. No. It's mined, and the supply runs out. *(right)*
Hint: Does nature make more uranium soon?
Explanation: Uranium is non-renewable. It makes little carbon dioxide, which is a separate question.

**4.4 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: The brook's water costs nothing. So is the mill's flour free?
- A. Yes. Renewable energy is always free to use. *(misconception: renewable means free)*
- B. No. Building and mending the wheel and stones costs a lot. *(right)*
- C. Yes, because energy is never used up. *(misconception: conservation means no cost)*
Hint: What did the miller pay for after the storm?
Explanation: The water is free, but machines, repairs and work are not.

**4.5 (Keeper's Desk, say why, grown-up reads)**
Prompt: Why can't any wheel lift its own water back up and keep going?
Answer notes: Strong answers name both reasons. Lifting the water back needs at least as much energy as it gave falling. Every turn, friction and splashing spread some energy out as heat and sound, so less is left to lift water. Each turn is weaker until it stops.
Misconception tested: "it only fails because of friction; perfect parts would work" (ignores that no machine gives out more than goes in, and a frictionless wheel still gains nothing).
Hint: Use both clues from the detective's rule card.
Explanation: No machine gives out more than goes in, and real machines always spread some out.

**4.6 (Keeper's Desk, say why, grown-up reads)**
Prompt: You want to compare two wheel designs. What makes it a fair test?
Answer notes: Change only the wheel design. Keep the same water, the same fall, the same grain, the same grease and the same length of time. Measure flour per hour for each. Repeat each test more than once and compare the results.
Misconception tested: changing two things at once, or trusting a single run.
Hint: What must stay the same? What will you measure?
Explanation: If only the wheel changes, a difference in flour points to the wheel. Repeats show how much results vary.

**4.7 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Is electricity from the wire renewable?
- A. Always. Electricity never runs out. *(misconception: electricity is a source)*
- B. Never. Wires are made in factories. *(misconception: judging the wire, not the source)*
- C. It depends on what made the electricity. *(right)*
Hint: Electricity carries energy. Where did that energy start?
Explanation: Electricity from a water turbine is from a renewable source. Electricity from burning coal isn't.

## Key terms (glossary)

| Term | Child-friendly meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Energy | What makes things happen: moving, warming, lighting, making sound. |
| Stored energy | Energy waiting in something, like water high up or grain. |
| Movement energy | The energy a moving thing has. Faster means more. |
| Store | Scientists' word for where energy is kept. |
| Transfer | When energy moves from one thing to another. |
| Gravitational store | Energy something has because it is high up. |
| Kinetic store | Energy something has because it is moving. |
| Thermal store | Energy something has because it is warm. |
| Chemical store | Energy kept in food, grain, wood or fuel. |
| Force | A push or a pull. |
| Friction | A force between rubbing surfaces. It slows things and warms them. |
| Spread out | When energy goes into warming lots of things a little. Scientists say "dissipated". |
| Efficiency | How much of the energy in does the useful job. |
| Sankey diagram | An arrow that splits. Its widths show where energy goes. |
| Renewable | Refilled by nature soon enough for people to keep using it. |
| Non-renewable | Used much faster than nature can make more. |
| Evaporate | When liquid water turns into invisible vapor in the air. |
| Sluice gate | A gate that controls how much water flows. |
| Millrace | The channel that carries water to the mill wheel. |
| Axle | The rod the wheel turns on. |
| Millstones | Two heavy round stones that grind grain into flour. |
| Perpetual motion | A machine that keeps going forever by itself. It can't exist. |

## Off-screen invitations

Off-screen invitations record nothing.

- Rub your hands together fast, then slowly. Which way warms them more? (Safe for anyone.)
- Blow on a paper pinwheel softly, then harder. What changes? (Safe for anyone.)
- **Adult-led, at a sink:** with a grown-up, make a small wheel from a paper plate and small paper cups taped on as buckets, on a drinking-straw or pencil axle. Hold it over the sink and pour water from a jug, first from low, then from higher. Count the turns. Keep the water cool, keep electrical things away, and mop up spills so nobody slips.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** energy makes things happen and moves from one thing to another; it is never made or destroyed, but it spreads out (dissipates) and becomes less useful; friction warms things; faster water carries more energy; efficiency compares useful output with total input; renewable sources are refilled by nature, which doesn't make them free or harmless.

**Two ways of talking about energy:** younger cards use everyday words (movement energy, heat energy, stored energy) and sort them into waiting, moving and traveling. Older cards use the stores-and-transfers words now used in many UK and US classrooms. Card T8 shows how the two line up. Light, sound and electricity are treated as ways energy travels, not as stores, so nothing has to be unlearned later.

**What it doesn't claim:** the wheel bench is a made-up model with a stated rule. Its numbers are not measurements of any real mill, and the cartoon wheel speed is not a real speed. The long "follow the energy" path is a map of the valley; older cards look at one step at a time, as physics teachers recommend. Real waterwheel efficiencies vary a lot with design. In 1759 John Smeaton's model tests found overshot wheels about twice as efficient as undershot ones; modern studies report well-designed wheels of both kinds reaching much higher figures. Item 3.8's 60 and 30 are illustrative. The small hydro card describes stations in general and makes no claim about any real project.
