## The story scene (Act II, chapter 1, the weekly market)

*A story from Willowbank.*

**Short:**
> Market day. Bramble says the baker's loaves have shrunk. The baker says they haven't. Nobody knows who should decide. Dot fetches the post office scale.

**Longer:**
> The market is back, and so is the smell of bread. Bramble holds up a loaf. "Smaller than last week."
>
> "It is not," says the baker. His sign says *Loaf: 500 g*.
>
> Pip offers to eat the loaf and report back. Nobody accepts.
>
> "Who decides?" asks {guide}. Nobody knows. Willowbank has never had a market rule.
>
> Dot fetches the scale from the post office. "Letters get weighed. So can loaves." The loaf weighs exactly 500 g.
>
> Bramble frowns, then nods. "Fine. I was wrong about the loaf. We still need the scale."
>
> She is right about that. By evening a board hangs by the bridge:
> 1. The scale stays at the market.
> 2. Anyone accused tells their side first.
> 3. Neighbors who were not in the quarrel listen and decide.
>
> "Rules have a long history," says the librarian. "Come and see who made the old ones."

**Keeper's note:**
> In medieval England, a law set how much a loaf must weigh. Officials checked, and a baker caught again and again could be put in the pillory. Medieval jurors were often neighbors who knew the people involved; Willowbank chose the opposite. The board is made up. The bread law was real.

## Teach cards

**Teach 1. Then and now** *(A true thing from history)*
> To compare then and now, ask two questions. What was the same? What was different? Both answers can be true at once.

Worked example, one step per tap:
1. A Roman child learning letters scratched them on a wax tablet with a pointed stylus.
2. Different: today, many children write with pencils or on screens.
3. Same: children then and now practiced letters over and over.

**Teach 2. Who makes the rules?** *(A true thing from history)*
> For nearly 500 years, Rome was a republic. Citizens elected its leaders. Two consuls led together for one year, so neither could rule alone.

Worked example:
1. Who makes the rules? Citizen men voted in assemblies. The Senate gave advice.
2. Who carries them out? Two consuls, chosen for one year.
3. What stops one man taking over? Each consul could block the other.

**Teach 3. Who tells the story?** *(A true thing from history)*
> Before you trust a story from the past, ask three things. Who wrote it? When? Which side were they on?

Worked example:
1. Who wrote about Boudicca? Two Roman writers, Tacitus and Cassius Dio.
2. When? Tacitus, about 55 years later. Dio, about 150 years later.
3. Which side? Rome's. The Iceni left no writing.

**Teach 4. A fair hearing** *(A true thing from history)*
> A rule needs someone to make it, someone to keep it, and a fair way to decide who broke it.

Worked example:
1. Make: an English law set how much a loaf must weigh.
2. Keep: officials weighed bakers' loaves.
3. Decide: a baker caught again and again could be fined, then shamed.

## Station 1. Then and Now

**Card 1.1 School** *(A true thing from history)*
> Some Roman children went to a small school, if their family could pay. Many did not. Many worked, and some children were enslaved.

**Card 1.2 Writing** *(A true thing from history)*
> Children wrote on a wooden tablet filled with wax. A pointed stylus scratched the letters. Its flat end smoothed the wax to start again.

**Card 1.3 Games** *(A true thing from history)*
> Roman children played with dolls, balls and hoops. They played knucklebones with small animal ankle bones, thrown like dice.

**Card 1.4 Food and clothes** *(A true thing from history)*
> Many meals were bread, porridge, olives and grapes. Children wore a simple tunic. Free-born boys often wore a charm on a necklace, called a bulla.

**Card 1.5 Greece** *(A true thing from history)*
> In Athens, some boys went to teachers for reading, music and sport. In Sparta, boys were trained to be soldiers.

**Card 1.6 How long ago?** *(A true thing from history)*
> These children lived about 2,000 years ago. That is many, many lifetimes before ours.

## Station 2. Who Rules Rome?

**Card 2.1 The Republic** *(A true thing from history)*
> Rome threw out its kings, by tradition in 509 BCE. Citizen men elected leaders each year. Women and enslaved people could not vote.

**Card 2.2 Patricians and plebeians** *(A true thing from history)*
> Old rich families were patricians. Most citizens were plebeians. After a long struggle, plebeians won their own officials, tribunes, who could block a law.

**Card 2.3 Athens and Rome** *(A true thing from history)*
> Both Athens and Rome had citizen assemblies voting on laws. Many Athenian offices were chosen by lot; Roman consuls were elected.

**The Rule Board** *(A made-up model)*
> Four safeguards kept one man from ruling Rome: a one-year term, two consuls, armies kept out of Rome, and tribunes who could block.

Events slide on one at a time. Predict which safeguard each breaks, then flip.

| Event card | Safeguard it breaks | Flip line |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 88 BCE: Sulla marches his army into Rome. | Armies kept out | A general led his army into Rome itself. Others copied him. |
| 49 BCE: Caesar crosses the Rubicon with his army. | Armies kept out | The little river was the edge of his province. Crossing it meant civil war. |
| 44 BCE: Caesar is made dictator for life. | One-year term | A dictator was meant for a short emergency. "For life" sounded like a king. |
| 27 BCE: Octavian is named Augustus. | All four, quietly | He kept the Senate and old titles, and the real power for life. |

**Model line:** *A made-up model. Many people and choices are left out.*

**Card 2.4 The end of Caesar** *(A true thing from history; Sapling and up)*
> On 15 March 44 BCE, senators killed Caesar, saying they were saving the Republic. War followed instead.

**Card 2.5 Augustus** *(A true thing from history)*
> Caesar's adopted son Octavian won the wars. In 27 BCE the Senate named him Augustus. Historians call him Rome's first emperor.

**Card 2.6 Two ways to tell it** *(A true thing from history)*
> Augustus said he gave the state back to the Senate and people. Later writers said one man ruled. Who was right?

**Keeper's note** *(A true thing from history)*
> Trouble began long before Caesar. In 133 and 121 BCE, two brothers, Tiberius and Gaius Gracchus, pushed to give land to poor citizens. Both were killed by opponents in Rome. Fighting between Roman leaders grew from then on. The Lighthouse and the Library drawer tells how Octavian's last war ended, in Egypt.

## Station 3. Two Romans Tell Boudicca

**Card 3.1 Rome comes to Britain** *(A true thing from history)*
> Julius Caesar raided Britain in 55 BCE and left. In 43 CE the emperor Claudius sent an army that stayed. Rome wanted land, metals, taxes and glory.

**Card 3.2 Legions** *(A true thing from history)*
> Rome's army was organized in legions: large units of trained, paid soldiers. They built roads and forts too.

**Card 3.3 The Iceni** *(A true thing from history)*
> The Iceni lived in eastern Britain. Their king, Prasutagus, was Rome's ally. Boudicca was his wife, and they had two daughters.

**Card 3.4 The will** *(A true thing from history)*
> Prasutagus left his kingdom to his two daughters and the emperor Nero together. When he died, Roman officials took it all.

**Card 3.5 The revolt** *(A true thing from history)*
> In 60 or 61 CE, Boudicca led the Iceni and their neighbors against Rome.

**Card 3.6 Three towns** *(A true thing from history)*
> Her army burned three Roman towns: Camulodunum (Colchester), Londinium (London) and Verulamium (St Albans). Then a Roman army defeated hers. Nobody knows where.

**Card 3.7 The red layer** *(A true thing from history)*
> Under Colchester and London, diggers find a layer of burnt red earth from that time. It is the ground's own record of the fire.

**Tacitus card** *(A true thing from history)*
> Tacitus, a Roman senator, wrote about the revolt about 98 and 116 CE. His father-in-law, Agricola, served in Britain then. He blames Roman mistreatment of the Iceni.

**Dio card** *(A true thing from history)*
> Cassius Dio, a Roman senator, wrote in Greek in the early 200s. He describes Boudicca as tall, with tawny hair and a golden necklace. He blames money Romans demanded back.

**The statue** *(A picture made much later)*
> In 1902, a bronze statue of Boudicca in a chariot was set up by Westminster Bridge in London. Its sculptor began it in 1856.

**Keeper's note** *(A true thing from history)*
> Tacitus says the Romans treated Boudicca and her daughters cruelly. The two writers tell her end differently, and both give her a speech that no one wrote down at the time. Victorians called her Boadicea and made her a symbol of Britain. Later, women campaigning for the vote rallied at her statue.

**Who says so? (sort)** Drag each claim to a tray: *Tacitus*, *Dio*, *Both*, *The ground*, *A picture made much later*.

| Claim | Tray | Feedback |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Roman towns were destroyed. | Both | Yes, and the ground agrees. |
| Romans mistreated the Iceni first. | Tacitus | Yes. Tacitus gives this reason. |
| She had tawny hair. | Dio | Yes. Only Dio describes her looks. |
| A burnt red layer under London. | The ground | Yes. Diggers found it. |
| She rode a chariot with blades on its wheels. | A picture made much later | Yes. That's the 1902 statue. |

## Station 4. A Fair Hearing

**Card 4.1 Who makes the rules?** *(A true thing from history)*
> In medieval England, the king made laws. Lords held courts on their manors. Churches and towns had their own courts and rules.

**Card 4.2 Who keeps the rules?** *(A true thing from history)*
> There were no police. Groups of about ten men answered for each other. Anyone who saw a crime raised a shout, the hue and cry, and neighbors gave chase.

**Card 4.3 The sheriff** *(A true thing from history)*
> The sheriff was the king's officer in each shire, or county. The word comes from "shire reeve". He kept order and brought people to court.

**Card 4.4 Deciding, the old way** *(A true thing from history)*
> Long ago, some cases were decided by an ordeal: a hard test, such as one with cold water. People believed God would show the truth.

**Card 4.5 Trial by battle** *(A true thing from history)*
> After 1066, the Normans brought trial by battle to England. The two sides fought, and people believed God helped the right one.

**Card 4.6 1215** *(A true thing from history)*
> In 1215, a great Church council told priests to stop taking part in ordeals. Without priests, ordeals faded. English courts needed a new way.

**Card 4.7 Juries** *(A true thing from history)*
> From about 1220, English courts used juries of local men to decide guilt. Jurors were often neighbors expected to know the people and the facts.

**Card 4.8 Magna Carta** *(A true thing from history)*
> In June 1215, King John agreed to Magna Carta. One promise: no free man would be jailed without the lawful judgment of his equals, or the law of the land.

**Card 4.9 Who it left out** *(A true thing from history)*
> "Free man" left out many people. Lots of peasants were not free. The Pope canceled the charter after about ten weeks. It was issued again later.

**Card 4.10 Punishments** *(A true thing from history)*
> For small wrongs, most punishments were fines. Some people were put in the stocks or the pillory: wooden frames in a public place, so everyone saw what they had done.

**Card 4.11 Today** *(A true thing from history)*
> Today in England and Wales, jurors decide from evidence admitted in court. They report prior knowledge or connections for the judge to assess.

**Keeper's note** *(A true thing from history)*
> Four copies of the 1215 charter survive. Later, many read its promise as a right to trial by jury, though that was not its first meaning.

**The loaf bench** *(A made-up model)*
> The baker's sign gives a loaf's weight. Put a loaf on the scale. Is it the right weight, or short?

Model line: *A made-up model. Real bread laws changed the weight with the price of wheat.*
