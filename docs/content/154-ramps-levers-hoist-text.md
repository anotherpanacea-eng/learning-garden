# Ramps, Levers and the Mill Hoist: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 154](../specs/154-ramps-levers-hoist.md). Use as written, after independent spec review and a physics subject check. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion and is never gendered. The miller is she/her; Pip is he/him; Bramble is she/her and has a magpie; the librarian is they/them. Real-world facts were checked against the sources in the [source audit](../source-audits/154-ramps-levers-hoist.md) (no open checks remain). Nothing here is scored or timed.

## Labels used on every card

- **A story from Willowbank**: the town, the mill, the yard and every character are made up.
- **A true thing about science**: checked science, explained simply.
- **A true thing from history**: something people really did or wrote.
- **A made-up model**: a simple game rule, shown on the card, that is not real measurement.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. The beam in the lane (Act I, chapter 4: mend the village)

**Hook:** A storm-fallen beam blocks the mill lane. Nobody can lift it. The miller fetches a pole and a log.

**Short:**
> The beam is too heavy to lift. The miller slides a pole under it, over a log, and leans. The beam rises. "Trade," she says.

**Longer:**
> The storm has dropped a roof beam across the mill lane, and the mud is full of nails from a smashed crate. Pip tries to lift the beam. It doesn't move. Bramble tries. It doesn't move either.
>
> The miller rolls a log close to the beam and slides a long pole over it, one end under the beam. She leans on the far end. Slowly, the beam rises off the ground.
>
> "Magic," says Bramble.
>
> "Trade," says the miller. "I push a little, but my end moves a long way. The beam moves a short way, but it's heavy."
>
> Then she sweeps the mud with the big magnet. Nails leap up and cling to it. A copper pipe stays where it is.
>
> "Why not the pipe?" asks Pip.
>
> {guide} picks it up. "Let's find out what a magnet will pull."

### Scene 2. Bramble's feather (Act II, chapter 1: the weekly market)

**Hook:** Grain sacks go up to the top floor. At the loft door, Bramble and Pip argue about falling.

**Short:**
> "Heavy things fall faster," says Pip. "Galileo said everything falls together," says Bramble. They test it from the loft door. Both are half right.

**Longer:**
> The market wants flour, so the grain sacks go up through the loft door. Pip watches from below. "If a sack fell, it would beat a little bag of flour down," he says. "Heavy things fall faster."
>
> "Wrong," says Bramble, from the loft. "Galileo proved everything falls together. My magpie's feather would fall as fast as my iron key."
>
> "Let's try," says {guide}. Pip steps well back. Nobody drops a sack. They drop two apples, one big and one small. They land together. Pip frowns.
>
> Then Bramble drops her key and a feather. The key lands at once. The feather drifts, and drifts, and lands on the cat.
>
> "So who was right?" says Pip.
>
> The librarian has come out with a card from the Archive. "You both were, a little. It depends on the air."

**Keeper's note** (A true thing from history):
> In 1971, on the Moon, astronaut David Scott dropped a hammer and a falcon feather together. With no air to slow the feather, they landed at the same moment.

### Scene 3. Up before the water (Act VI, chapter 3: the long rain)

**Hook:** Rain upstream for days. The flour must go up to the top floor before the water comes.

**Short:**
> The wheel turns the hoist. Sack after sack rises through the trap doors. Bang, bang. Pip counts every bang.

**Longer:**
> The brook is rising. The miller wants every sack of flour on the top floor, high and dry, before night.
>
> The waterwheel is still turning, so she pulls a cord, and the sack hoist wakes up. A chain winds onto a drum. A sack lifts and pushes up through a pair of trap doors. The doors fall shut behind it. Bang. Then through the next floor. Bang.
>
> "That's how I know it's arrived," says the miller. "I count."
>
> Pip counts out loud for every sack. Bramble ties a ribbon on each one that reaches the top. {guide} and you work the hand pulley in the yard for the small bags.
>
> By dark, the flour is up. The water reaches the mill's flood post. The mill holds.

**Keeper's note** (A true thing from history):
> Real watermills had sack hoists like this, driven by the waterwheel.

## Teach cards

Each card is one idea. Worked examples reveal one step per tap.

**T1. A push or a pull** (Sprout and up, A true thing about science)
> A force is a push or a pull. One thing gives it to another. Forces can start, stop, speed up, slow down or turn things.

Worked example: *Pip's cart.*
1. Pip pushes the cart. It starts to move.
2. Pip pushes from the side. It turns.
3. The ground rubs the wheels. That's friction. The cart slows and stops.

**T2. Touching, or across a gap** (Sprout and up)
> Most forces need touching: a hand, a rope, the wind. Some act across a gap: gravity and magnets. That's strange, and it's true.

Worked example: *A magnet and a paper clip.*
1. Hold the magnet near the clip. Don't touch.
2. The clip jumps to the magnet.
3. They weren't touching. The magnet pulled across the gap.

**T3. Arrows show forces** (Seedling and up)
> An arrow shows one force. Its tail touches the object. It points the way the force acts. A longer arrow means a bigger force.

Worked example: *A sack hanging still on the rope.*
1. The rope pulls the sack up. Draw an arrow up, tail on the sack.
2. Gravity pulls the sack down. Draw an arrow down.
3. Label each one: "rope" and "gravity".

**T4. Balanced or unbalanced** (Seedling and up)
> Equal arrows pointing opposite ways are balanced. Nothing changes: still things stay still, and moving things keep going the same.

Worked example: *Tug-of-war over a log.*
1. Pip pulls 3 squares. Bramble pulls 3 squares the other way.
2. Balanced. The log stays put.
3. Pip pulls 4. Now it's unbalanced, and the log slides toward Pip.

**T5. Everything falls** (Seedling and up)
> Gravity pulls everything toward the Earth. When the air hardly matters, heavy and light things fall together.

Worked example: *Two apples from the loft door.*
1. One big apple, one small apple. Same shape.
2. Drop them together.
3. They land together. Air barely slows either one.

**T6. Air pushes back** (Seedling and up)
> Air resistance pushes against anything moving through air. It grows when the thing is wider and when it goes faster.

Worked example: *Same paper, two shapes.*
1. A flat sheet and a crumpled sheet weigh the same.
2. The flat sheet is wide. Lots of air pushes back.
3. The crumpled ball lands first.

**T7. Galileo's slow fall** (Sapling and up, A true thing from history)
> Falling was too fast for the clocks of his day. So Galileo rolled balls down a long, gentle ramp and timed them with water.

Worked example: *Equal steps of time.*
1. In the first step, the ball rolls 1 length.
2. Next steps: 3 lengths, then 5, then 7.
3. It keeps speeding up. Totals: 1, 4, 9, 16.

**T8. Levers** (Seedling and up)
> A lever turns on a pivot. Move the pivot closer to the load, and you push less. But your end moves farther.

Worked example (Sapling numbers): *A balanced beam.*
1. A 2 kg sack hangs 3 pegs from the pivot. 2 × 3 = 6.
2. A 3 kg sack hangs 2 pegs away on the other side. 3 × 2 = 6.
3. Both sides make 6. The beam balances.

**T9. Ramps** (Seedling and up)
> A ramp lets you push a load up instead of lifting it. A longer ramp needs a smaller push, along a longer path.

Worked example: *A barrel 1 m up onto the cart.*
1. Lifting it straight up: the full weight.
2. A 2 m ramp: half the push, twice the path.
3. A 4 m ramp: a quarter of the push, four times the path.

**T10. Pulleys and gears** (Sapling and up)
> One fixed pulley turns your pull around. Add a moving pulley, and you pull half as hard, but pull twice the rope.

Worked example: *A 12 kg sack up 3 m.*
1. One fixed pulley: pull as hard as 12 kg. Pull 3 m of rope.
2. Two rope sections hold it: pull 6. Pull 6 m.
3. Gears trade too: a small gear turns fast and easily; a big one turns slowly and strongly.

**T11. Only some metals** (Sprout and up)
> Magnets pull iron, steel, nickel and cobalt. They don't pull aluminum, copper, brass, gold or silver. Shiny doesn't tell you. Testing does.

Worked example: *Two things from the mud.*
1. A steel nail: the magnet pulls it.
2. A copper pipe: nothing happens.
3. Both are metal. Only one is magnetic.

**T12. Pulled, or a magnet?** (Seedling and up)
> A magnet pulls a nail. Two magnets can also push apart. Flip your magnet: if the thing is pushed away, it's a magnet too.

Worked example: *The flip test.*
1. A nail and a small magnet both stick.
2. Flip the big magnet over.
3. The nail still sticks. The small magnet is pushed away. Only it is a magnet.

**Gravity or magnet?** (side card after T12, Seedling and up)
> Gravity always pulls, and pulls everything. Magnets pull or push, and only some things. Neither needs air.

## Activity (a): Touch or gap?

**Instruction:** Put each card in a bin. Do they have to touch?

**Bins:** They touch · Across a gap

**Guided first card:** *A rope hauls a sack.* Shown: "Is the rope touching? Yes: *They touch*."

| Card | Bin | Feedback |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pip pushes the cart | They touch | Hands on the handle. That's a touching push. |
| A rope hauls a sack | They touch | The rope is tied on. It pulls by touching. |
| A magnet lifts nails from the mud | Across a gap | The nails jump before they touch. A gap force. |
| An apple drops from the tree | Across a gap | Nothing touches it, yet it falls. Gravity pulls across the gap. |
| Wind fills a hanging sheet | They touch | You can't see air, but air touches the sheet. |
| The brake rubs Trundle's wheel | They touch | Rubbing surfaces touch. That's friction. |
| A rubbed balloon lifts Ember's hair | Across a gap | Static pulls across a gap too. It isn't magnetism. |
| The floor holds up a sack | They touch | The floor pushes up on the sack, touching it. |
| A magnet pulls a pin through paper | Across a gap | Paper between them, and the pull still works. |
| The brook pushes a log | They touch | Water touches the log and pushes it along. |

**After the sort:** show the *Gravity or magnet?* card.

## Activity (b): The Arrow Board

**Instruction:** Pick one thing. Add every push and pull on it.

**Tray labels:** rope · gravity · floor · Pip · Bramble · push · friction · air resistance

**Snap rule message** (if a tail lands off the object): Arrows start on the thing they push or pull.

**Prediction question after each board:** What will it do?
- Stay still, or keep going the same
- Speed up or turn
- Slow down

**Guided first board: the hanging sack.**
1. Tap the sack. The rope pulls it up: add a rope arrow, 3 squares.
2. Gravity pulls it down: add a gravity arrow.
3. It hangs still. How long is the gravity arrow? *(3 squares)*

Feedback: "Equal and opposite. That's why it hangs still."

**Boards:**

| Board | Arrows (squares) | Prediction | Feedback |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tug-of-war, even | Pip 3, Bramble 3 | Stay still | Equal pulls, opposite ways. The log stays put. |
| Tug-of-war, uneven | Pip 4, Bramble 3 | Speed up (toward Pip) | Pip's arrow is longer. The log slides his way. |
| Trundle rolling steadily | Push 2 forward, friction 2 back | Keep going the same | Balanced, and still moving. Balanced means no change. |
| Falling apple | Gravity 3 down, air resistance 1 up | Speed up | Gravity wins, so it speeds up. |
| Sack on the floor | Gravity 3 down, floor 3 up | Stay still | The floor pushes up. Still things have forces too. |

**Model line:** A simplified picture: arrow lengths are relative, not measured.

## Activity (c): The Drop Door

**Instruction:** Pick a pair. Predict. Then drop them.

**Prediction choices:** This one first · That one first · Together

**Guided first drop: two same-size balls, one heavy, one light.**
1. Same size and shape. One is much heavier.
2. Predict, then watch.
3. They land together. Air barely slows either one.

**Results:** in the yard, the balls land together (a hair apart), the crumpled paper first, and the hammer far ahead of the feather. On the Moon, every pair lands together.

**Feedback lines:**
- Yard, balls: "Together, or a hair apart. Air barely slows either ball."
- Yard, paper: "Same paper, same weight. Only the shape changed, so air made the difference."
- Yard, feather: "Air pushes hard on a light, wide feather. It drifts."
- Moon, any pair: "No air on the Moon. Everything falls together."

**Moon switch label:** Take the air away (the Moon)

**Readout** (Keeper's Desk only): Falling 4.9 m, with no air: about 1.0 s on Earth, about 2.5 s on the Moon.

**Model line:** A made-up model. Fall times use Earth's and the Moon's gravity, with air left out. Real air slows light, wide things much more.

**Galileo's Ramp panel** (Sapling and up, A true thing from history):
> Galileo rolled a bronze ball down a long, gentle groove. Water ran into a cup while it rolled; he weighed the water to measure time. Watch each equal step.

**Pivot slider (Sprout and Seedling):** Slide the log. Watch the push bar and the dotted path.

2. Slide the log one peg closer to the sack.
3. Your push bar got shorter. Your dotted path got longer.

Feedback: "Less push, longer path. That's the trade."

**Ramp panel label:** Lift the barrel 1 m. Choose a ramp: 1 m · 2 m · 4 m

**Pulley panel label:** How many rope sections hold the sack? 1 · 2 · 4

**Gears panel label:** Turn the handle. The small gear turns the big drum gear.

**Unit line:** "kg-weight" means "as hard as holding up that many kg".

**Real hoist toggle:** Real pulleys rub a little. Turn this on: you need a bit more than the model says.

## Activity (e): The Magnet Sweep

**Instruction:** Will the magnet pull it? Predict, then sweep.

**Guided first item:** *A steel nail and a copper pipe.*
1. Predict for each one.
2. Sweep. The nail jumps up. The pipe stays.

| Object | Result | Feedback |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Steel nail | Pulled | Steel is mostly iron. Magnets pull it. |
| Iron hinge pin | Pulled | Iron is magnetic. |
| Steel paper clip | Pulled | A steel clip, so it jumps. |
| Aluminum can | Not pulled | Metal, but aluminum isn't magnetic. |
| Copper pipe | Not pulled | Copper is a metal magnets don't pull. |
| Brass key | Not pulled | Brass is shiny and golden. Still not magnetic. |
| Silver spoon | Not pulled | Real silver is a metal magnets don't pull. |
| Wooden peg | Not pulled | Wood isn't magnetic. |

| Pewter cup (mostly tin) | Not pulled | Silvery and metal. Still not magnetic. Looks don't tell you. |

**Euro coin card** (A true thing about science, from Europe):
> Euro 1, 2 and 5 cent coins look like copper. Inside they are steel, so a magnet lifts them. Gold-colored 10, 20 and 50 cent coins stay put.

## Problem sets

Each item lists the prompt, the answer, the misconception each wrong choice tests ("tests:"), a hint and an explanation.

### Set 1. Touch, gap and magnets (Sprout and Seedling)

**1.1 (Sprout, sort)**
Prompt: Do they touch? Sort the cards.
Cards: Pip pushes the cart · a rope pulls a sack · a magnet lifts a nail · an apple falls · wind fills a sheet · the brook pushes a log
Answer: *They touch:* cart, rope, wind, brook. *Across a gap:* magnet, apple.
Tests: wind is invisible, so it must be a gap force; nothing pulls a falling apple.
Hint: Is anything touching the apple?
Explanation: Air and water touch what they push. Gravity and magnets pull across a gap.

**1.2 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: A sack sits still on the floor. Is any force acting on it?
- A. Yes. Gravity pulls down; the floor pushes up. *(right)*
- B. No. It isn't moving, so nothing pushes or pulls. *(tests: a force only if there's movement)*
- C. Just gravity. A floor can't push on anything. *(tests: only living things push)*
Hint: Why doesn't the sack sink into the floor?
Explanation: Two forces, equal and opposite. That's why it stays still.

**1.3 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Pip's cart rolls, then stops. Why does it stop?
- A. Its push ran out, like a battery does. *(tests: impetus, a stored push)*
- B. It stopped because carts that heavy just stop. *(tests: heavy things just stop by themselves)*
- C. Friction from the ground slowed the wheels. *(right)*
Hint: What does a rough path do to wheels?
Explanation: Friction pushed back on the wheels. Something always pushes back when a thing slows down.

**1.4 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which one pulls without touching?
- A. A rope tied to a sack *(tests: every pull works across a gap)*
- B. The Earth pulling an apple down *(right)*
- C. Wind pushing on a hanging sheet *(tests: invisible means not touching)*
Hint: Which one has empty space in between?
Explanation: Gravity pulls across the gap. Rope and air both touch.

**1.5 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which one will the magnet pick up?
- A. A steel nail *(right)*
- B. An aluminum can *(tests: all metals are magnetic)*
- C. A shiny brass key *(tests: shiny things are magnetic)*
Hint: Which one has iron in it?
Explanation: Steel is mostly iron. Aluminum and brass are metals that magnets don't pull.

**1.6 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: A nail sticks to the magnet. Does that show the nail is a magnet?
- A. Yes. Anything that sticks to a magnet is a magnet. *(tests: attracted means it is a magnet)*
- B. Yes, because every steel thing is a magnet. *(tests: all steel things are magnets)*
- C. No. Plain steel sticks too. The flip test can tell. *(right)*
Hint: What would a real magnet do if you flipped yours?
Explanation: A magnet would be pushed away when flipped. A plain nail just keeps sticking.

**1.7 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Is gravity the same thing as magnetism?
- A. Yes. The Earth is one giant magnet pulling us. *(tests: gravity is magnetism)*
- B. No. Gravity pulls everything; magnets, only some. *(right)*
- C. Yes. Both of them stop working without air. *(tests: gap forces need air)*
Hint: Does a magnet pull a wooden peg? Does gravity?
Explanation: Gravity pulls wood, apples and feathers. A magnet pulls only some metals. Neither needs air.

### Set 2. Arrows and falling (Seedling and Sapling)

**2.1 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Pip pulls the log 3 squares left. Bramble pulls 3 right. What happens?
- A. It moves left, because Pip pulled first. *(tests: the first push wins)*
- B. It stays put. The pulls are equal. *(right)*
- C. It stays put, because there are no forces now. *(tests: balanced means no forces)*
Hint: Compare the two arrows.
Explanation: Two equal forces are still acting. They balance, so nothing changes.

**2.2 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Trundle rolls steadily. Push forward 2, friction back 2. What next?
- A. It slows down and stops soon. *(tests: balanced means stopped)*
- B. It speeds up, because it's moving forward. *(tests: motion needs a bigger forward force)*
- C. It keeps rolling at the same speed. *(right)*
Hint: Balanced means no change. What is it doing now?
Explanation: Balanced forces keep a moving thing moving the same way at the same speed.

**2.3 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which drawing fits a sack hanging still on the rope?
- A. Rope arrow up, gravity arrow down, same length *(right)*
- B. One gravity arrow, floating beside the sack *(tests: arrows can float off the object)*
- C. Rope arrow up, longer than the gravity arrow *(tests: unbalanced forces can keep things still)*
Hint: It's still. Are the forces equal?
Explanation: Still means balanced. Both arrows start on the sack and match in length.

**2.4 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: Pip pulls 4 squares. Bramble pulls 3 the other way. What's left over?
Answer: 1 square, toward Pip.
Worked steps: 1. The pulls point opposite ways. 2. Take the smaller from the bigger: 4 − 3 = 1. 3. The leftover points Pip's way, so the log slides toward him.
Tests: adding opposite forces (7 squares).
Hint: Opposite arrows cancel. How much doesn't cancel?
Explanation: The leftover arrow shows which way the motion changes.

**2.5 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Same paper: one flat, one crumpled. Drop both. Which lands first?
- A. Together, since both sheets weigh the same. *(over-correction: everything always falls together)*
- B. The flat sheet, because it's the bigger one. *(tests: bigger things fall faster)*
- C. The crumpled ball. Less air pushes on it. *(right)*
Hint: Which shape catches more air?
Explanation: Same weight, different shape. The flat sheet meets more air resistance.

**2.6 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which way does air resistance push on a falling apple?
- A. Up, against its fall *(right)*
- B. Down, the way it falls *(tests: air resistance acts downward)*
- C. All around it, equally *(tests: air resistance has no direction)*
Hint: Air resistance pushes against the motion.
Explanation: The apple moves down, so air pushes up.

**2.7 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: On the Moon, why did the hammer and feather land together?
- A. The Moon has no gravity, so both floated down. *(tests: no gravity on the Moon)*
- B. The Moon has no air to slow the feather. *(right)*
- C. The Moon's gravity pulls feathers extra hard. *(tests: gravity picks favorites)*
Hint: What slows a feather on Earth?
Explanation: The Moon has gravity but no air. With no air resistance, both fall together.

### Set 3. Levers, ramps, pulleys and gears in numbers (Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

All of Set 3 uses the made-up bench model: no friction, weightless beams and ropes.

**3.1 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: A 4 kg sack is on peg 2. Which peg balances a 2 kg sack?
Answer: Peg 4, on the other side.
Worked steps: 1. Left: 4 × 2 = 8. 2. Right: 2 × ? = 8. 3. ? = 4.
Tests: putting the lighter sack closer (peg 1).
Hint: A lighter sack needs to hang farther out.
Explanation: Kg × pegs must match. Half the weight needs twice the distance.

**3.2 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: A 6 kg sack is on peg 1. You push at peg 3. How hard?
Answer: As hard as holding up 2 kg (2 kg-weight). Your hand moves 3 times as far as the sack.
Worked steps: 1. Sack side: 6 × 1 = 6. 2. Your side: ? × 3 = 6. 3. ? = 2.
Tests: the machine gives something for nothing (forgetting the longer path).
Hint: Make both sides match.
Explanation: A third of the push, three times the distance.

**3.3 (Sapling, number)**
Card: left side, a 2 kg sack on peg 3 and a 3 kg sack on peg 2.
Prompt: Two sacks on the left. How many kg balance them at peg 4?
Answer: 3 kg.
Worked steps: 1. 2 × 3 = 6. 2. 3 × 2 = 6. 3. Left total 12. 4. ? × 4 = 12, so ? = 3.
Tests: adding the kg and ignoring distance (5 kg).
Hint: Work out each sack's kg × pegs, then add.
Explanation: Each sack counts by its weight and its distance.

**3.4 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: Beam: 60 kg, 0.5 m from the log. Push 2 m away. How hard?
Answer: 15 kg-weight. If the beam rises 10 cm, your end moves 40 cm.
Worked steps: 1. 60 × 0.5 = 30. 2. ? × 2 = 30, so ? = 15. 3. Your arm is 4 times longer, so your end moves 4 × 10 = 40 cm. 4. Check: 60 × 10 = 600 and 15 × 40 = 600.
Tests: a lever makes force from nothing.
Hint: How many times longer is your side than the beam's side?
Explanation: Push × distance comes out the same on both sides. That's the trade.

**3.5 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: A 4 m ramp needs a quarter of the push. What's the catch?
- A. The ramp makes the barrel weigh less. *(tests: a machine changes the load)*
- B. There's no catch. You save effort for free. *(tests: machines give force for nothing)*
- C. You push 4 m to lift it 1 m. *(right)*
Hint: Compare the path along the ramp with the lift.
Explanation: A quarter of the push over four times the distance. A real ramp adds friction too.

**3.6 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: Four rope sections hold a 20 kg sack. Lift it 2 m. Pull and rope?
Answer: Pull 5 kg-weight; pull 8 m of rope.
Worked steps: 1. The load shares across 4 sections: 20 ÷ 4 = 5. 2. Each section must shorten by 2 m: 2 × 4 = 8. 3. Check: 20 × 2 = 40 and 5 × 8 = 40.
Tests: more pulleys means less rope too.
Hint: Each of the 4 sections has to get 2 m shorter.
Explanation: In the Real hoist view the pull reads a bit more than 5, because real pulleys rub.

**3.7 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: Handle gear: 12 teeth. Drum gear: 36 teeth. Lift a sack 3 m. Handle turns?
Answer: 18 handle turns (6 drum turns).
Worked steps: 1. The drum winds 0.5 m per turn: 3 ÷ 0.5 = 6 drum turns. 2. 36 ÷ 12 = 3 handle turns per drum turn. 3. 6 × 3 = 18.
Tests: the big gear turns faster.
Hint: How many small-gear turns make one big-gear turn?
Explanation: The small gear turns more times with less force. The big gear turns fewer times, more strongly.

**3.8 (Keeper's Desk, number, Galileo's Ramp)**
Prompt: A ball rolls 5 cm in the first time step. How far after 4 steps?
Answer: 80 cm.
Worked steps: 1. Galileo's pattern: 1, 3, 5, 7 times the first step. 2. 5, 15, 25, 35 cm. 3. 5 + 15 + 25 + 35 = 80. 4. Shortcut: 4 × 4 = 16, and 16 × 5 = 80.
Tests: falling at a steady speed (4 × 5 = 20 cm).
Hint: Each step covers more than the one before.
Explanation: A rolling or falling ball keeps speeding up, so later steps are longer.

### Set 4. How do we know? (Keeper's Desk)

**Source card for 4.1** (A true thing from history):
> The Leaning Tower story comes from Viviani, Galileo's student, about 1654. Galileo wrote about heavy and light balls falling, but never said he used that tower. Many historians doubt it.

**4.1 (true or not true from this source)**
Prompt: Is each one true from this source?
- "Viviani wrote that Galileo dropped weights from the tower." **True from this source.**
- "Galileo wrote that he dropped balls from the Leaning Tower." **Not true from this source.** *(tests: a famous story must come from the person)*
- "We know for certain the tower drop never happened." **Not true from this source.** *(overclaims: the card leaves room for doubt either way)*
Hint: Read what the card says, word for word.
Explanation: The card gives one witness and a doubt. Doubt is not proof it never happened.

**4.2 (3-choice)**
Prompt: Stevin's two lead balls landed nearly together. Why only nearly?
- A. Air slowed each a little, by different amounts. *(right)*
- B. The heavy ball fell ten times faster. *(tests: Aristotle's rule)*
- C. Gravity pulled the lighter ball a little harder. *(tests: gravity favors light things)*
Hint: What was in the way of both balls?
Explanation: In air, things fall almost together when air resistance is small. Not exactly.

**4.3 (3-choice)**
Prompt: Why did David Scott drop a feather beside the hammer?
- A. Feathers were the only light thing they had. *(tests: a convenience choice, missing the test)*
- B. Air slows a feather a lot: a hard test. *(right)*
- C. A feather has no weight at all on the Moon. *(tests: no gravity on the Moon)*
Hint: On Earth, which falls slower: a hammer or a feather?
Explanation: A feather drifts on Earth. If it keeps up on the Moon, air was what slowed it.

## "I'm stuck" worked examples

- **Touch or gap?** Easier case: you push a door. Your hand touches it. *Start this one:* ask "is anything touching it?"
- **Arrows:** Easier case: a ball resting on a table. Gravity down, table up, same length. *Start this one:* draw the pull you're sure of first.
- **Falling:** Easier case: two same-size marbles, one glass and one steel. They land together. *Start this one:* ask "does air matter much here?"
- **Levers:** Easier case: a seesaw with two equal friends at equal distances balances. *Start this one:* work out kg × pegs on the side you know.
- **Pulleys:** Easier case: one fixed pulley. Same pull, same rope as the lift. *Start this one:* count the rope sections holding the load.
- **Magnets:** Easier case: a paper clip jumps; a coin made of copper doesn't. *Start this one:* ask "is there iron or steel in it?"

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Force:** a push or a pull that one thing gives another.
- **Contact force:** a force that works only when things touch.
- **Non-contact force:** a force that works across a gap, like gravity or magnetism.
- **Gravity:** the pull between things with mass. Near the Earth, it pulls everything down.
- **Friction:** a force between surfaces that rub. It pushes against sliding.
- **Air resistance:** air pushing back on something moving through it.
- **Balanced forces:** equal forces pointing opposite ways. The motion doesn't change.
- **Unbalanced forces:** forces that don't cancel. The motion changes.
- **Leftover arrow (resultant):** what all the forces add up to.
- **Terminal speed:** the steady speed of a falling thing once air resistance matches its weight.
- **Lever:** a bar that turns on a pivot to lift or move a load.
- **Pivot (fulcrum):** the point a lever turns on.
- **Load:** the thing you lift. **Effort:** the push or pull you give.
- **Ramp (inclined plane):** a slope to push a load up.
- **Pulley:** a wheel with a rope over it.
- **Gear:** a toothed wheel that turns another toothed wheel.
- **Magnetic material:** something a magnet pulls, like iron, steel, nickel or cobalt.
- **Poles:** the two ends of a magnet's pull. Like poles push apart; opposite poles pull together.
- **kg-weight:** this game's word for a push as hard as holding up that many kg.

## Off-screen invitations (optional, grown-up-led)

- **Paper pair.** Drop a flat sheet and the same sheet crumpled, from hand height while standing on the floor. Which lands first? Then fold the flat sheet in half and try again.
- **Ruler lever.** Lay a ruler over a pencil. Put a few coins near one end. Press the other end. Move the pencil and feel the push change.
- **Magnet hunt.** With a grown-up, test kitchen things with a fridge magnet. Find a metal it doesn't pull.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** pushes and pulls; contact and gap forces; force arrows; falling with and without air; how levers, ramps, pulleys and gears trade force for distance; which materials are magnetic. Numbers only at older depths.

**What it does not claim:** the benches are made-up models (no friction, weightless beams and ropes). Forces are relative or in "kg-weight", never measured newtons. Energy, work, magnetic fields and formal moments are left to other packs. Nothing here measures a real object.

**Real history, briefly:** Stevin and de Groot's 1586 tower drop in Delft; Galileo's ramp in *Two New Sciences* (1638) and the doubtful Pisa story; the Apollo 15 hammer and feather (2 August 1971); real watermill sack hoists. Sources are in the source audit.

**Safety for the off-screen activities:** a grown-up leads each one. Drop only paper, from hand height, standing on the floor; never drop things from heights or onto people. Keep small, strong magnets away from young children, because swallowed magnets are dangerous. Keep magnets away from cards, phones and medical devices. Nothing from these activities is recorded in the game.
