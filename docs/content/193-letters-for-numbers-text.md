# Letters for Numbers: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 193](../specs/193-letters-for-numbers.md). Use as written, after independent spec review and a math check. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion and is never gendered; `{courier}` is the explorer's courier. The librarian is they/them, Pip he/him, Dot she/her, Bramble she/her (with her magpie), Nuria she/her, the Vadoalto baker she/her, the Willowbank baker he/him. Real history is in the [source audit](../source-audits/193-letters-for-numbers.md); items still marked `[check]` stay off screen until cleared. Nothing here is scored or timed. Money is in US dollars.

## Labels used on every card

- **A story from Willowbank**: the town, its library, its account books and every character are made up.
- **A true thing about math**: checked math, explained simply.
- **A true thing from history**: something people really did or wrote.
- **A made-up model**: a simple game rule, shown on the card, that is not a real price or measurement.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. The price rule (Act III, chapter 1: the floating market)

**Hook:** The Vadoalto baker calls her price in words. The librarian writes it in letters.

**Short:**
> "Three dollars a loaf, and two for the boat!" calls the Vadoalto baker. The librarian writes C = 3L + 2. Pip asks where the words went.

**Longer:**
> The boats from Vadoalto tie up at the landing. The Vadoalto baker stands on a crate. "Three dollars a loaf," she calls, "and two dollars for the boat, however many you take!"
>
> Pip wants four loaves. The miller wants ten. Dot wants one, for the post office. Everyone does the sums out loud, all at once.
>
> The librarian opens an old account book from the library. Its prices are written in long sentences. "The old Keeper wrote every price this way," they say. "It takes a whole page."
>
> They write on a scrap: **C = 3L + 2.**
>
> "C is the cost in dollars. L is how many loaves."
>
> Pip squints. "Where did the words go?"
>
> "Into the letters," says {guide}. "Put in your number of loaves, and the rule does the rest."
>
> Pip puts in 4. "Fourteen dollars," he says. "Huh. That was quick."

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> C = 3L + 2 gives the cost for any number of loaves. Four loaves: 3 × 4 + 2 = 14. No loaves: just the boat, 2 dollars. The baker's price is made up.

### Scene 2. Twelve bundles (Act III, chapter 2: the seed exchange)

**Hook:** Nuria's boat can carry twelve bundles. Some are reed, some are apple seedlings. How many of each?

**Short:**
> "Twelve bundles fit in my boat," says Nuria. "Reed and seedlings." Pip says six and six. Bramble says seven and five. Both fit.

**Longer:**
> Nuria's boat is small. "Twelve bundles fit," she says, "no more. Some reed for you, some apple seedlings for us."
>
> "Six and six," says Pip at once.
>
> "Seven reed and five seedlings," says Bramble.
>
> "Eleven and one," says the magpie, or seems to.
>
> "You're all right," says {guide}. "Every pair that adds to twelve fits in the boat."
>
> The librarian draws a table: reed down one side, seedlings down the other. They start at one reed and go up, one at a time.
>
> "That's a lot of answers," says Pip.
>
> Nuria taps the table. "Here's another rule. Vadoalto needs four more bundles of reed than seedlings. Their roofs blew off."
>
> The librarian runs a finger down the table. Only one row works for both rules.
>
> "Eight reed, four seedlings," says Bramble. "I was close."

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> One rule with two unknowns has many pairs. A second rule can leave only one pair that fits both. Later you'll meet ways to find that pair without a table.

### Scene 3. A correction (Act IV, chapter 3: the newspaper's two explanations)

**Hook:** The Gazette prints a price rule with a mistake in it. The librarian, who edits it, needs a correction.

**Short:**
> The Gazette says four baskets of (L + 3) cost 4L + 3. "That's too cheap," says the baker. The librarian reaches for very small print.

**Longer:**
> Hidden on page three of the *Willowbank Gazette*, under the two big headlines, is a small notice. **Basket rule: 4 baskets, each with L loaves and 3 rolls. Total: 4L + 3.**
>
> The Willowbank baker reads it over his tea. "Four baskets," he says, "and only three rolls? I put three rolls in every basket."
>
> The librarian goes pink. "I multiplied the loaves by four and forgot the rolls."
>
> {guide} draws a long rectangle on the back of a flour bag. Four rows. Each row: L loaves, then 3 rolls. "Count the rolls," says {guide}.
>
> "Twelve," says Pip. "So it's 4L + 12."
>
> "Correction," says the librarian, writing very small. Then, larger: "Every basket gets its rolls."

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> A number in front of a bracket multiplies everything inside. 4(L + 3) = 4L + 12. Check with L = 1: 4 × 4 = 16, and 4 + 12 = 16.

### Scene 4. The formula column (Act V, path 2: a Willowbank newspaper)

**Hook:** The librarian starts a new column: an old rule from Hollis's account books, written once in words and once in letters.

**Short:**
> Hollis's account books are full of rules in words. The Gazette's new column turns one into letters each week. This week, you choose the rule.

**Longer:**
> The Gazette has a new column. It is called **Letters for Numbers**, and it is the librarian's favorite page.
>
> Each week they open one of Hollis's old account books. Hollis wrote every rule in words: "for the ferry, two dollars for each crossing and one more for a cart." Each week the column writes it again in letters: F = 2n + 1.
>
> "Why both?" asks Dot, who delivers it.
>
> "The words say what it means," says the librarian. "The letters say it fast. Hollis liked the words. I like both."
>
> Bramble wants the column called *Bramble's Letters*. The librarian says no.
>
> This week the column is empty. "Your turn," says the librarian. "Find a rule in the old books. Write it both ways. Then show us how to use it."

**Keeper's note** (A story from Willowbank):
> A column is a small, regular piece in a newspaper. Hollis's account books and their prices are made up. Writing in words, then in letters, is very old. See the history cards.

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer. Worked steps appear one tap at a time.

**T1. A rule in words** (A true thing about math)
> A formula is a rule for working something out. The baker's rule: 3 dollars for each loaf, plus 2 dollars for the boat.
Worked example: 1. Four loaves: 3 × 4 = 12. 2. Add the boat: 12 + 2 = 14. 3. Cost: $14.

**T2. The same rule in letters** (A true thing about math)
> C stands for the cost in dollars. L stands for the number of loaves. The rule becomes C = 3L + 2.
Worked example: 1. 3L means 3 × L. 2. L = 5: 3 × 5 = 15. 3. C = 15 + 2 = 17.

**T3. A letter stands for a number** (A true thing about math)
> L is the number of loaves. 3L means three lots of that number. Write the times sign before you put the number in.
Worked example: 1. 3L with L = 4. 2. Write 3 × 4. 3. 3L = 12, never 34.

**T4. Put it in, then follow the order** (A true thing about math)
> Put each number where its letter was. Then work out brackets first, then times and divide, then add and take away.
Worked example: P = 2(l + w), l = 5, w = 3. 1. P = 2 × (5 + 3). 2. Bracket first: 8. 3. 2 × 8 = 16 cm.

**T5. Squared or doubled?** (A true thing about math)
> a² means a × a. 2a means a + a. With a = 5, a² is 25 and 2a is 10.
Worked example: 1. a² with a = 3. 2. 3 × 3 = 9. 3. 2a with a = 3 is 6. Different.

**T6. Like terms join up** (A true thing about math)
> Like terms have the same letter and the same power. 3a + 2a = 5a: three a-bars and two more make five.
Worked example: 1. 4L + 3L. 2. Both are L-terms. 3. 4 + 3 = 7, so 7L.

**T7. Unlike terms stay apart** (A true thing about math)
> 3a + 2b cannot get shorter. a-bars and b-bars are different lengths. 3a + 2b is a finished answer.
Worked example: 1. 4L + 2R + 3L. 2. L-terms: 4L + 3L = 7L. 3. Answer: 7L + 2R. Two terms, finished.

**T8. The sign stays with its term** (A true thing about math)
> In 5a − 3a, the minus belongs to the 3a. Five a-bars, take away three: 2a.
Worked example: 1. 6b + 2 − 4b. 2. b-terms: 6b − 4b = 2b. 3. Answer: 2b + 2.

**T9. A bracket is a rectangle** (A true thing about math)
> 3(x + 4) is a rectangle 3 tall and x + 4 long. Split it into two parts: 3 × x and 3 × 4.
Worked example: 1. Left part: 3 by x, so 3x. 2. Right part: 3 by 4, so 12. 3. 3(x + 4) = 3x + 12. 4. Check, x = 1: 3 × 5 = 15; 3 + 12 = 15.

**T10. Open the bracket, then tidy** (A true thing about math)
> In 3 + 2(x + 1), open the bracket first. Then collect like terms.
Worked example: 1. 2(x + 1) = 2x + 2. 2. 3 + 2x + 2. 3. Collect the numbers: 2x + 5.

**T11. Find the common factor** (A true thing about math)
> Factoring runs a bracket backwards. In 6x + 9, what divides both terms? 3. So 6x + 9 = 3(2x + 3).
Worked example: 1. 6x ÷ 3 = 2x. 2. 9 ÷ 3 = 3. 3. 3(2x + 3). 4. Check: 3 × 2x + 3 × 3 = 6x + 9.

**T12. Take out the biggest one** (A true thing about math)
> In 12x + 8, 2 divides both, and so does 4. Take out the biggest: 4(3x + 2). Nothing inside shares a factor now.
Worked example: 1. Factors of 12 and 8 in common: 1, 2, 4. 2. Biggest: 4. 3. 12x ÷ 4 = 3x; 8 ÷ 4 = 2. 4. 4(3x + 2).

**T13. One rule, many pairs** (A true thing about math)
> a + 7 = 12 has one answer: 5. a + b = 12 has many answers. Each answer is a pair.
Worked example: 1. a = 5, b = 7 works. 2. So does a = 9, b = 3. 3. Keep looking.

**T14. Step one letter in order** (A true thing about math)
> Try a = 1, then 2, then 3. Work out b each time. A table in order shows when you have them all.
Worked example: 2x + y = 20. 1. x = 1: y = 18. 2. x = 2: y = 16. 3. Each step, y goes down by 2.

**T15. A second rule** (A true thing about math)
> One rule gives many pairs. Add a second rule, and often only one pair fits both.
Worked example: 1. Pairs with a + b = 12. 2. Which also have a − b = 4? 3. Only a = 8, b = 4.

**T16. Turn the rule around** (A true thing about math)
> C = 3L + 2 finds cost from loaves. To find loaves from cost, undo the steps in reverse: take away 2, then divide by 3.
Worked example: 1. Numbers first: 3L + 2 = 20, so 3L = 18, so L = 6. 2. Letters: C − 2 = 3L. 3. L = (C − 2) ÷ 3.

**T17. A quick estimate** (A true thing about math)
> Round the numbers. Work out a rough answer. If your exact answer is far from it, look again.
Worked example: 1. C = 3L + 2 with L = 19. 2. 19 is about 20: 3 × 20 + 2 = 62. 3. Exact: 59. Close, so sensible.

## History cards

**H1. A book of restoring and balancing** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> In Baghdad, around the year 820, al-Khwarizmi wrote a book about solving problems. He used words, never letters. The unknown was called "the thing".
Keeper's note:
> The book's title has the word *al-jabr*, about restoring and balancing. Our word "algebra" comes from it. He wrote it to help with trade, sharing out what people left in wills, and measuring land. Every step was a sentence.

**H2. Letters for amounts** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> In 1591, in France, François Viète printed a book using letters for amounts. Vowels stood for numbers he was looking for. Other letters stood for numbers he knew.
Keeper's note:
> Viète trained as a lawyer and worked for French kings. His vowel idea did not last. Later, in 1637, René Descartes used letters near the end of the alphabet, like x, for unknowns. That habit stuck.

## Bench (a): The Recipe Ledger

> Prices and recipes here are made up. The rules work the way real formulas do.

## Bench (b): The Tile Tray

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> A long green bar stands for a. A shorter blue bar stands for b. A small square stands for 1. A big square stands for a².

**Opening:** Tip the tiles out. Put each kind in its own pile.
**Buttons:** Tip out · Sort · Try to join · Write it · Take-away tiles
**When two different tiles are pushed together:** They won't join. Different lengths stay different.
**When piles are finished:** These piles are your answer. Read them out.
**Take-away tiles card:** A red tile takes away one tile of its kind. A green bar and a red bar make nothing.

**Sprout mode opening:** Put the tiles that match into piles.

**Guided item:** Sort 3a + 2b + 2a.
- Step 1: Find the green a-bars. *(3 and 2 more.)*
- Step 2: Pile them: 5a.
- Step 3: The blue bars stay apart: 5a + 2b.

## Bench (c): The Area Board

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> Each rectangle's area shows a multiplication. A letter side can be any length; the picture is not to scale.

**Expand mode opening:** Split the rectangle at the plus sign. Find each part's area.
**Factor mode opening:** Build one rectangle from all the tiles. What is the side they share?
**Buttons:** Split · Label the parts · Add them up · Check with a number · Build a rectangle · Try a wider side

**Guided item (expand):** Open the bracket 3(x + 4).
- Step 1: Split the long side into x and 4.
- Step 2: Parts: 3 × x = 3x and 3 × 4 = 12.
- Step 3: 3(x + 4) = 3x + 12.

**Guided item (factor):** Factor 6x + 9.
- Step 1: What divides 6 and 9? *(3.)*
- Step 2: 6x ÷ 3 = 2x, and 9 ÷ 3 = 3.
- Step 3: 3(2x + 3). Open it to check.

**Keeper's Desk worked example (negative outside):**
> −2(x − 4): multiply each part by −2. −2 × x = −2x. −2 × −4 = +8. So −2x + 8.
Check: x = 1 gives −2 × −3 = 6, and −2 + 8 = 6.

**After a check:** Both ways gave the same number. That's a good sign.
**After a check that differs:** The two ways gave different numbers. Look at each part again.

## Bench (d): The Pair Finder

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> Bundles, loaves and baskets come in whole numbers here. Nobody cuts a loaf in half.

**Opening:** Pick a number for the first letter. The finder works out the second.
**Buttons:** Next number · Fill the table · Add a second rule · Show the pairs that fit both
**Table headers:** First letter · Second letter · Does it work?
**When the table is complete:** Every number has been tried, in order. These are all the pairs.

**Sprout mode opening:** Red books and blue books fill the row. Try different mixes.

**Guided item:** Find pairs with a + b = 12.
- Step 1: a = 1. What is b? *(11.)*
- Step 2: a = 2. What is b? *(10.)*
- Step 3: Keep going in order until a = 11.

## Bench (e): The Backwards Page

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> The machine runs a rule forward, then backward. Each step backward undoes one step forward.

**Opening:** Here is the rule forward. Run it backward to find the start.
**Buttons:** Forward · Backward · Numbers first · Now with letters · Check by putting it back

**Guided item:** The baker's bill was $20. How many loaves?
- Step 1: Forward was: times 3, then add 2.
- Step 2: Backward: take away 2. 20 − 2 = 18.
- Step 3: Then divide by 3. 18 ÷ 3 = 6 loaves.
- Step 4: Check: 3 × 6 + 2 = 20.

## Problem sets

Twenty three-choice items put the right answer at A seven times, B seven times and C six times.

### Set A. Rules in words and pictures (Sprout and Seedling)

**A1 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Every jar gets one ribbon. More jars come. What about ribbons?
- A. You need more ribbons. *(right)*
- B. You need the same ribbons. *(tests: ignoring the rule)*
- C. You need fewer ribbons. *(tests: reversing the rule)*
Hint: One ribbon for every jar. Count the jars.
Explanation: Each new jar needs its own ribbon. More jars, more ribbons.
Feedback A: Yes. The rule follows the jars.
Feedback B: The new jars need ribbons too.
Feedback C: The jars went up. What does the rule say?

**A2 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which tiles can go in one pile?
- A. A long green bar and a small square. *(tests: mixing kinds)*
- B. Two long green bars. *(right)*
- C. A green bar and a shorter blue bar. *(tests: bars look alike, so they join)*
Hint: A pile is one kind of tile only.
Explanation: Matching tiles pile up. Different tiles stay apart.
Feedback A: A bar and a square are different kinds.
Feedback B: Yes. Same tile, same pile.
Feedback C: Look at the lengths. They're different kinds.

**A3 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Red and blue books fill the row. More red. What about blue?
- A. More blue books fit. *(tests: both go up together)*
- B. The same blue books fit. *(tests: ignoring the full row)*
- C. Fewer blue books fit. *(right)*
Hint: The row is full. Red books take up room.
Explanation: More red books leave less room, so fewer blue books fit.
Feedback A: The row is full. Where would they go?
Feedback B: The red books took some of the room.
Feedback C: Yes. More of one, fewer of the other.

**A4 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: 3 dollars a loaf, plus 2 for the boat. Cost of 4 loaves?
- A. $14 *(right)*
- B. $18 *(tests: adding the boat before multiplying)*
- C. $12 *(tests: leaving out the boat)*
Hint: Do the loaves first. Then add the boat.
Explanation: 3 × 4 = 12 for loaves. Add 2 for the boat: $14.
Feedback A: Yes. Loaves, then the boat.
Feedback B: The boat costs 2 once, for the whole order.
Feedback C: Close. Don't forget the boat.

**A5 (Seedling, match)**
Prompt: Match each rule in words to its rule in letters.
- "3 dollars a loaf, plus 2 for the boat" → C = 3L + 2
- "2 dollars a loaf, plus 3 for the boat" → C = 2L + 3
- "Add the length and the width, then double" → P = 2(l + w)
- "Half the number of people, plus 1" → E = p ÷ 2 + 1
Tests: swapping the two cost rules; reading "then double" as doubling only the length.
Hint: Which number goes with each loaf? Which is added once?
Explanation: The number in front of L is the price of each loaf. The added number is paid once.

**A6 (Seedling, number)**
Prompt: Eggs: half the number of people, plus 1. Six people?
Answer: 4 eggs.
Worked steps: 1. Half of 6 is 3. 2. 3 + 1 = 4 eggs.
Tests: 3 (leaving out the plus 1); 2 (adding first: 6 ÷ 3).
Hint: Halve first. Then add 1.
Explanation: E = p ÷ 2 + 1. 6 ÷ 2 = 3, and 3 + 1 = 4.

### Set B. Putting numbers into letters (Seedling and Sapling)

**B2 (Seedling, 3-choice)**

- C. 2 *(right)*

Explanation: 3 × 0 = 0, and the boat still costs 2.
Feedback A: The loaves cost 0. The boat still costs 2.
Feedback B: 3L with L = 0 is 3 × 0, which is 0.
Feedback C: Yes. Just the boat.

**B3 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: P = 2(l + w). l = 5 cm, w = 3 cm. Find P.
Answer: 16 cm.

Tests: 13 (2 × 5 + 3, the bracket ignored); 15 (5 × 3, the area instead).

Explanation: Add length and width, then double. That walks all the way around.

**B4 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: a = 5. What is a²?

- B. 10 *(tests: squaring read as doubling)*
- C. 7 *(tests: adding the 2)*
Hint: a² means a × a.
Explanation: 5 × 5 = 25. Doubling gives 10, which is 2a.
Feedback A: Yes. 5 × 5.
Feedback B: That's 2a. a² is a × a.
Feedback C: The small 2 means times itself.

**B5 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: Find 2 + 3a when a = 4.
Answer: 14.
Worked steps: 1. 2 + 3 × (4). 2. Times first: 3 × 4 = 12. 3. 2 + 12 = 14.
Tests: 20 (left to right: 2 + 3 = 5, then 5 × 4); 36 (reading 3a as 34).
Hint: Which comes first, add or times?
Explanation: Times before add, even when the add is written first.

**B6 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: C = 3L + 2. Which cost makes sense for 19 loaves?
- A. $21 *(tests: forgetting to multiply by 3)*
- B. $590 *(tests: a place-value slip)*
- C. $59 *(right)*
Hint: 19 is about 20. Try 3 × 20 + 2.
Explanation: The estimate is 62. Exactly: 3 × 19 + 2 = 59. Close to the estimate.
Feedback A: That's 19 + 2. Each loaf costs 3.
Feedback B: The estimate is about 60. 590 is ten times too big.
Feedback C: Yes. It's near the estimate of 62.

### Set C. Like terms (Seedling and Sapling)

**C1 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Simplify 3a + 2a.
- A. 5a *(right)*
- B. 5 *(tests: dropping the letter)*
- C. 6a *(tests: multiplying 3 and 2)*
Hint: Three a-bars and two a-bars. How many a-bars?
Explanation: Same letter, so they join: 5a.
Feedback A: Yes. Five a-bars.
Feedback B: Five what? Keep the a.
Feedback C: Three bars and two more is five bars.

**C2 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Simplify 3a + 2b.
- A. 5ab. Join the letters too. *(tests: forcing the terms together)*
- B. 3a + 2b. It's finished. *(right)*
- C. 5. Add the 3 and the 2. *(tests: wanting one number)*
Hint: Are a-bars and b-bars the same length?
Explanation: Different letters can't join. Two terms is a finished answer.
Feedback A: a and b are different numbers. They stay apart.
Feedback B: Yes. Nothing joins, so it's done.
Feedback C: An answer can have two terms.

**C3 (Seedling, tiles)**
Prompt: Sort the tiles for 4L + 2R + 3L. Write the answer.
Answer: 7L + 2R.
Worked steps: 1. L-bars: 4 and 3 more, so 7L. 2. R-bars: 2R. 3. 7L + 2R.
Tests: 9L (joining all the bars); 7L (losing the R-bars).
Hint: Make one pile for each kind of bar.
Explanation: L stands for the number of loaves, R for rolls. Only L with L.

**C4 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Simplify 5a − 3a.
- A. 8a *(tests: losing the minus sign)*
- B. 2 *(tests: dropping the letter)*
- C. 2a *(right)*
Hint: Five a-bars. Take three away.
Explanation: The minus belongs to the 3a. 5 − 3 = 2, so 2a.
Feedback A: The minus means take away.
Feedback B: Two what? Keep the a.
Feedback C: Yes. Two a-bars left.

**C5 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Simplify a + a².
- A. 2a². Both have an a. *(tests: ignoring the power)*
- B. a + a². It's finished. *(right)*
- C. a³. Add the powers. *(tests: adding the powers)*
Hint: Is a bar the same as a big square?
Explanation: a is a bar; a² is a square. Different kinds stay apart.
Feedback A: a and a² are different kinds. Try a = 3: 3 + 9 = 12.
Feedback B: Yes. Same letter, different power, so they stay apart.
Feedback C: a³ is a × a × a. Try a = 2: 2 + 4 = 6, but a³ = 8.

**C6 (Sapling, number with check)**
Prompt: Simplify 2a + 3 + a + 4. Check with a = 2.
Answer: 3a + 7. Check: 2 × 2 + 3 + 2 + 4 = 13, and 3 × 2 + 7 = 13.
Worked steps: 1. a-terms: 2a + a = 3a. 2. Numbers: 3 + 4 = 7. 3. 3a + 7.
Tests: 10a (joining numbers with letters); 2a + 7 (counting a as 0a).
Hint: A lone a means 1a.
Explanation: Letters with letters, numbers with numbers. The check gives 13 both ways.

### Set D. Brackets and common factors (Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**D1 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Open the bracket: 3(x + 4).
- A. 3x + 4 *(tests: multiplying only the first term)*
- B. 3x + 12 *(right)*
- C. x + 7 *(tests: adding the 3)*
Hint: Split the rectangle. Find both areas.
Explanation: 3 × x = 3x and 3 × 4 = 12. Both parts.
Feedback A: The 4 is in the bracket too. Multiply it.
Feedback B: Yes. Both parts multiplied.
Feedback C: A number touching a bracket means times.

**D2 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: Open the bracket: 5(2x + 1).
Answer: 10x + 5.
Worked steps: 1. 5 × 2x = 10x. 2. 5 × 1 = 5. 3. 10x + 5.
Tests: 10x + 1 (only the first term); 7x + 6 (adding 5 to each part).
Hint: Multiply each part by 5.
Explanation: Check with x = 1: 5 × 3 = 15, and 10 + 5 = 15.

**D3 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Factor 6x + 9.
- A. 3(2x + 9) *(tests: dividing only the first term)*
- B. 3(2x) *(tests: losing a term)*
- C. 3(2x + 3) *(right)*
Hint: Divide both 6x and 9 by 3.
Explanation: 6x ÷ 3 = 2x and 9 ÷ 3 = 3. Opening 3(2x + 3) gives 6x + 9.
Feedback A: Open it: 3 × 9 = 27. Divide the 9 too.
Feedback B: Where did the 9 go? Every term stays.
Feedback C: Yes. Open it up to check.

**D4 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Factor 12x + 8 all the way.
- A. 4(3x + 2) *(right)*
- B. 2(6x + 4) *(tests: a common factor that isn't the biggest)*
- C. 4(3x + 8) *(tests: dividing only the first term)*
Hint: What is the biggest number that divides 12 and 8?
Explanation: 4 is the biggest. Inside, 3x and 2 share nothing more.
Feedback A: Yes. Nothing left to share inside.
Feedback B: True, but 6x and 4 still share a 2.
Feedback C: Open it: 4 × 8 = 32. Divide the 8 too.

**D5 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: Simplify 3 + 2(x + 1).
Answer: 2x + 5.
Worked steps: 1. Open the bracket: 2x + 2. 2. 3 + 2x + 2. 3. Collect numbers: 2x + 5.
Tests: 5x + 5 (adding 3 + 2 before the bracket); 2x + 4 (only the first term multiplied).
Hint: Open the bracket before anything else.
Explanation: Check with x = 1: 3 + 2 × 2 = 7, and 2 + 5 = 7.

**D6 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Factor x² + 3x.
- A. x(x + 3) *(right)*
- B. 3(x² + x) *(tests: looking only for number factors)*
- C. It can't be factored. No number divides both. *(tests: missing a letter factor)*
Hint: Look at the letters. What does every term have?
Explanation: Both terms contain x. x × x = x², and x × 3 = 3x.
Feedback A: Yes. A letter can be the common factor.
Feedback B: Open it: 3x² + 3x. That's different.
Feedback C: No number, true. But both terms share an x.

### Set E. Which pairs work? (Seedling, Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**E1 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: a + b = 12. How many answers are there?
- A. One: a = 6 and b = 6. *(tests: one answer only)*
- B. Many pairs. *(right)*
- C. None. Two letters are too many to solve. *(tests: withholding more than the rule requires)*
Hint: Try a = 5. What is b? Now try a = 9.
Explanation: Many pairs add to 12. Each pair is one answer.
Feedback A: 6 and 6 works. So does 5 and 7.
Feedback B: Yes. Lots of pairs.
Feedback C: Try a = 5. Then b = 7 works.

**E2 (Seedling, number)**
Prompt: a + b = 12, whole numbers above 0. How many pairs?
Answer: 11 pairs: (1, 11), (2, 10), and so on up to (11, 1).
Worked steps: 1. a = 1, b = 11. 2. Step a up by 1 each time. 3. a can go from 1 to 11: 11 pairs.
Tests: 6 (counting (5, 7) and (7, 5) as one); 13 (letting 0 in).
Hint: Start at a = 1. Go in order.
Explanation: The table in order shows every pair. a = 12 would need b = 0.

**E3 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: 2x + y = 20. Which pair works?
- A. x = 3, y = 17 *(tests: reading it as x + y)*
- B. x = 3, y = 14 *(right)*
- C. x = 14, y = 3 *(tests: swapping the order)*
Hint: Work out 2 × x first.
Explanation: 2 × 3 = 6, and 6 + 14 = 20.
Feedback A: 2 × 3 = 6. 6 + 17 is 23.
Feedback B: Yes. 6 + 14 = 20.
Feedback C: x comes first. 2 × 14 + 3 = 31.

**E4 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: 2x + y = 20, whole numbers above 0. How many pairs?
Answer: 9 pairs: x goes from 1 to 9.
Worked steps: 1. x = 1: y = 18. 2. Each step, y goes down by 2. 3. x = 9: y = 2. 4. x = 10 would need y = 0.
Tests: 19 (counting pairs for x + y = 20); 10 (letting y be 0).
Hint: Step x from 1. Stop when y would reach 0.
Explanation: y = 20 − 2x. y stays above 0 for x from 1 to 9.

**E5 (Keeper's Desk, number pair)**
Prompt: Reed plus seedlings: 12. Reed minus seedlings: 4. Find each.
Answer: r = 8, s = 4.
Worked steps: 1. Call reed r and seedlings s. List pairs with r + s = 12. 2. Check each for r − s = 4. 3. Only (8, 4) fits.
Tests: (6, 6) (using only the first rule); (4, 8) (swapping reed and seedlings).
Hint: Make the table. Then check each row against the second rule.
Explanation: Two rules leave one pair: 8 + 4 = 12 and 8 − 4 = 4.

**E6 (Keeper's Desk, number pair)**
Prompt: $3 loaves, $5 baskets. You spent $31 on 9 things. How many each?
Answer: 7 loaves and 2 baskets.
Worked steps: 1. Pairs with 3L + 5B = 31: (2, 5) and (7, 2). 2. Which has 9 things? 3. 7 + 2 = 9.
Tests: 2 loaves and 5 baskets (only the money rule: 7 things); 5 loaves and 4 baskets (only the count rule: $35).
Hint: Step L from 0. When is 31 − 3L a whole number of fives?
Explanation: Only two pairs cost $31. Only one of them makes 9 things.

### Set F. Turning rules around, and the old books (Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**F1 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: 3L + 2 = 20. How many loaves is L?
Answer: 6.
Worked steps: 1. Take away 2: 3L = 18. 2. Divide by 3: L = 6. 3. Check: 3 × 6 + 2 = 20.
Tests: 18 (stopping at 3L); about 7.3 (adding 2 instead of taking it away: 22 ÷ 3).
Hint: Undo the add first, then the times.
Explanation: Undo in reverse order. The check puts 6 back into the rule.

**F2 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: A square fence: P = 4s. Which rule finds s?
- A. s = P − 4 *(tests: undoing times with take away)*
- B. s = 4P *(tests: multiplying again)*
- C. s = P ÷ 4 *(right)*
Hint: What undoes times 4?
Explanation: Divide undoes times. Check with s = 7: P = 28, and 28 ÷ 4 = 7.
Feedback A: Try P = 28: 28 − 4 = 24. Too long.
Feedback B: That makes it bigger again. Undo the times.
Feedback C: Yes. Divide undoes times.

**F3 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Turn C = 3L + 2 around to find L.
- A. L = (C − 2) ÷ 3 *(right)*
- B. L = C − 2 ÷ 3 *(tests: dividing only part of the side)*
- C. L = C ÷ 3 − 2 *(tests: undoing in the wrong order)*
Hint: Forward was times 3, then add 2. Undo in reverse.
Explanation: Take away 2 from all of C, then divide all of it by 3. Check: C = 20 gives 6.
Feedback A: Yes. Try C = 20: 18 ÷ 3 = 6.
Feedback B: Try C = 20. This gives about 19.3, not 6.
Feedback C: Try C = 20. This gives about 4.7. Undo the add first.

**F4 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: Bramble's rule: n = 2d + 10. Find n when d = −3.
Answer: 4.
Worked steps: 1. n = 2 × (−3) + 10. 2. 2 × (−3) = −6. 3. −6 + 10 = 4.
Tests: 16 (dropping the minus: 2 × 3 + 10); −16 (taking the minus onto the 10 as well).
Hint: Put −3 in brackets. Then times first.
Explanation: Brackets keep the minus with the 3.

**F5 (Keeper's Desk, true / not true from this source)**
Source: card H1, a book of restoring and balancing.
Prompt: Tap each sentence: true from this card, or not from this card.
- "al-Khwarizmi wrote his steps in words." *(true from this card)*
- "Our word 'algebra' comes from his book's title." *(true from this card)*
- "al-Khwarizmi wrote x for the unknown." *(not from this card: he used words, and called it "the thing")*
- "His book was only about puzzles, never real life." *(not from this card: trade, wills and land)*

**F6 (Keeper's Desk, say why; spoken to a grown-up, not stored)**
Prompt: Words or letters for the Gazette's rule? Which, and why?
Model answer for the grown-up: Any reasoned choice. Words tell a new reader what each part means. Letters are short and quick to use. Many writers give both, with each letter explained.
Tests: thinking letters are only for experts; thinking words are not real math.

## "I'm stuck" worked examples

Step 2 of the ladder: an easier case, then how to start this one.

- **Picture rules:** "Every cup gets a spoon. Three cups, three spoons. Start: what does each new thing need?"
- **Sorting tiles:** "Two red buttons and a blue one: the red ones go together. Start: find the ones that match exactly."
- **Full rows:** "A plate holds 4 cakes. 3 chocolate means 1 lemon. Start: the row is full, so one goes up and one goes down."
- **Word rules:** "Pip's rule: 2 dollars a cup, plus 1. Two cups: 2 × 2 + 1 = 5. Start: do the 'each' part first."
- **Substituting:** "2b with b = 3: write 2 × 3 = 6. Start: put your number in brackets and write the times sign."
- **Order:** "1 + 2 × 5: times first, 10, then 1 + 10 = 11. Start: find the times part."
- **Estimates:** "3 × 9 is about 3 × 10 = 30. Start: round your number to a friendly one."
- **Like terms:** "2 bars and 1 bar make 3 bars: 2c + c = 3c. Start: circle each kind in its own color."
- **Brackets:** "2(a + 1): a rectangle 2 tall. Parts 2a and 2. So 2a + 2. Start: split at the plus."
- **Common factors:** "4x + 6: 2 divides both. 2(2x + 3). Start: list what divides each number."
- **Pairs:** "a + b = 5: (1, 4), (2, 3), (3, 2), (4, 1). Start: begin at 1 and go in order."
- **Two rules:** "a + b = 6 and a = b: only (3, 3). Start: make the table, then test each row."
- **Turning around:** "Forward: times 2. Backward: divide by 2. Start: write the forward steps, then reverse them."

## Key terms (glossary)

- **formula**: a rule for working out an amount, in words or in letters.
- **letter (unknown)**: a letter that stands for a number.
- **substitute**: put a number where its letter was.
- **term**: one part of an expression, like 3a or 7.
- **expression**: terms joined by + or −, with no equals sign.
- **like terms**: terms with the same letter and the same power.
- **simplify**: write an expression shorter, without changing its value.
- **squared**: a number times itself. a² means a × a.
- **open a bracket (expand)**: multiply each term inside by the number outside.
- **common factor**: a number or letter that divides every term.
- **factor (an expression)**: write it as a common factor times a bracket.
- **pair**: two numbers that work in a rule together.
- **subject**: the letter a formula finds, on its own side of the equals sign.
- **estimate**: a rough answer from rounded numbers, used to check.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** formulas in words and in letters; substituting numbers, including zero, fractions and (at Keeper's Desk) negatives; collecting like terms with tiles; opening single brackets as areas; factoring by the biggest common factor; listing every pair that fits a rule with two unknowns, and seeing that a second rule can leave one pair; turning a formula around; estimating to check.

**What it does not claim:** it does not teach solving equations from scratch (the Balance Stall and the Keeper's Desk do that), graphs of rules, or methods for two rules at once. Negative numbers appear only at Keeper's Desk. Prices and recipes are made up.

**Sources in brief:** al-Khwarizmi's book (around 820 to 830, Baghdad) and the word "algebra" via the St Andrews MacTutor archive and encyclopedia summaries; François Viète's 1591 letters and Descartes' 1637 x via MacTutor and encyclopedia summaries. Full list in the source audit.

**Story note:** Willowbank, Vadoalto, the old Keeper's account books, the ferry rule and every price are made up.
