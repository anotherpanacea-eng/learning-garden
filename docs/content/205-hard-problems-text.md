# The Problem Shelf: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 205](../specs/205-hard-problems.md). Use as written, after independent spec review and a math check. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion and is never gendered; `{courier}` is the explorer's courier. Wren Halloway is she/her, Pip he/him, Dot she/her, Bramble she/her (with her magpie), Nuria she/her, the baker he/him, the orchard keeper she/her, the librarian they/them. Hollis (they/them) is named only in Act IV and Act VI text; Act III text says "the old Keeper". Real-world facts are in the [source audit](../source-audits/205-hard-problems.md); items still marked `[check]` stay off screen until cleared. Nothing here is scored or timed.

## Labels used on every card

- **A story from Willowbank**: the town, its traders, prices, problems and every character are made up.
- **A true thing about math**: checked math, explained simply.
- **A true thing from history**: something people really did or wrote.
- **A made-up model**: a simple rule or set of numbers, shown on the card, that is not real data.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. Slips from the exchange (Act III, chapter 2: the seed exchange)

**Hook:** The seed exchange leaves a pile of problems behind. Wren hands you the first stack.

**Short:**
> Nuria trades reed for apple seedlings. Everyone writes sums on scraps. Wren sweeps the scraps into a stack. "These need more than one step," she says.

**Longer:**
> The seed exchange ends with honey on every table and scraps of paper on every chair. Nuria has traded reed bundles for apple seedlings all morning. The orchard keeper has a list. The baker has a list. Pip has a list, mostly of honey.
>
> Wren gathers the scraps into a neat stack and carries it to the Keeper's Desk. "The old Keeper kept a shelf just like this," she says. "Problems that need more than one step."
>
> She reads the top slip aloud. "The baker baked nine trays of twelve honey buns. He sold twenty at the exchange. How many are left? Also, his cart has a squeaky wheel."
>
> "What does the wheel have to do with it?" asks Pip.
>
> "Nothing," says Wren. "That's the first thing to notice."

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> Two steps. Nine trays of twelve is 108 buns. Take away the twenty he sold: 88 buns are left. The squeaky wheel is extra information.

### Scene 2. Sixty thousand what? (Act IV, chapter 4: the proposal for the low fields)

**Hook:** A number on the meeting board has no unit. Bramble wants a reason, not a promise.

**Short:**
> The board says the marsh "holds 60,000". Bramble stands up. "Sixty thousand what? And why should we believe it?"

**Longer:**
> The meeting board is crowded with notes in the librarian's best pen. Under *The Sponge* someone has written: *holds 60,000*.
>
> Bramble stands up. "Sixty thousand what? Spoons? Ducks?"
>
> The room laughs. The librarian does not. "Liters," they say, a little pink. "Sixty thousand liters, about. We measured a corner and scaled it up."
>
> "Then write that," says Bramble. "And write *about*. And show me how you scaled it, because 'trust me' isn't a reason."
>
> Wren nods slowly. "Hollis always wrote the unit," she says. "Every page."
>
> The librarian crosses it out and writes it again: *holds about 60,000 liters (measured one corner, then scaled up).*
>
> Bramble sits down. "Better," she says. "Now I can argue with it properly."

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> A number without a unit can't be checked. "About" tells a reader it's an estimate. Showing how you got it lets a skeptic test your reasoning, not just your answer.

### Scene 3. A plan that meets the rain (Act VI, chapter 2: compare routes and keep a plan; chapter 4: the first journey)

**Hook:** Dot's exchange plan needs numbers. After the first journey, the numbers need changing.

**Short:**
> Dot plans the weekly parcels: 120 kilograms, 30 a trip, so four trips. Then the first week happens.

**Longer:**
> Dot spreads the plan on the café table. "Vadoalto wants 120 kilograms of apples each week. One courier trip carries 30 kilograms. So four trips." She writes it down with a flourish.
>
> "That's a model," says {guide}. "It assumes every trip carries a full 30."
>
> A week later, {courier} brings back the trip notes: 26, 24, 27 and 23 kilograms. Rain, a slippery bank, a basket that wouldn't close.
>
> Dot stares at them. "The plan didn't work."
>
> "The plan was a first try," says Wren. "Now you have data. Test the model against it, and change it."
>
> Dot adds the trips up. Then she picks up her pen again.

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> The trips averaged 25 kilograms. At 25 a trip, four trips carry only 100. 120 ÷ 25 = 4.8, and you can't make 0.8 of a trip, so plan five.

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer. Worked steps appear one tap at a time.

**T1. Read it twice** (A true thing about math)
> Read the problem once for the story. Read it again for the math. What do you know? What is the question asking for?
Worked example: 1. "320 apples. She packs 4 crates of 50. How many are left?" 2. Known: 320 apples, 4 crates, 50 each. 3. Asked: apples left.

**T2. Plan the steps** (A true thing about math)
> Draw a bar for the problem. Name each step before you work it out. Words like "left" don't tell you the step. The story does.
Worked example: 1. Bar: 320, split into "packed" and "left". 2. Step 1: packed = 4 × 50 = 200. 3. Step 2: left = 320 − 200 = 120.

**T3. Estimate first, check after** (A true thing about math)
> Before you work it out, guess the size. After, check another way: undo it, or try a different method. If they disagree, look again.
Worked example: 1. 480 − 195: about 500 − 200 = 300. 2. Work it out: 285. 3. Undo: 285 + 195 = 480. It checks.

**T4. Extra and missing** (A true thing about math)
> Some problems give facts you don't need. Some leave out a fact you do need. Sort them before you start.
Worked example: 1. "Loaves cost $3. The oven is old." 2. Oven age: extra. 3. "How many trips?" with no load size: missing.

**T5. Found and still to find** (A true thing about math)
> In a long problem, keep two lists: what you've found, and what's still to find. Set up the unknown with a bar or a letter first.
Worked example: 1. Pip and Bramble have 30 ribbons. Bramble has 6 more. 2. Let p be Pip's ribbons. Bramble's are p + 6. 3. Take off the 6: p + p = 24, so p = 12.

**T6. Three people to convince** (A true thing about math)
> Convince yourself: try it. Convince a friend: show it. Convince a skeptic: give a reason that works every time. Link reasons with "since" and "so".
Worked example: 1. Since 6 × 10 = 60 2. and 6 × 2 = 12, 3. so 6 × 8 = 60 − 12 = 48.

**T7. Find the broken step** (A true thing about math)
> To check someone's working, find the first step that stops making sense. Say why it breaks. A different answer alone doesn't show where.
Worked example: 1. 71 − 26. Step: "6 − 1 = 5." 2. That swaps the numbers. 3. Exchange a ten: 11 − 6 = 5, then 6 − 2 = 4. 45.

**T8. One example, one counterexample** (A true thing about math)
> One example that works doesn't prove "always". One example that fails is enough to show "not always". You may have met this at the desk with Bramble.
Worked example: 1. "Every shape with 4 sides is a square." 2. Try a rectangle 2 cm by 5 cm. 3. It has 4 sides but is not a square, so not always.

**T9. Units carry meaning** (A true thing about math)
> A length is in cm. An area counts squares: cm². A volume fills cubes: cm³. A number with no unit can't be checked.
Worked example: 1. A rug 9 cm by 7 cm. 2. 9 × 7 = 63 squares, each 1 cm on a side. 3. Area: 63 cm².

**T10. Exact or about?** (A true thing about math)
> Say "about" or write ≈ for an estimate. Round to fit the job. Too many digits can pretend to be more exact than you are.
Worked example: 1. A path around a circle 7 m across. 2. Calculator: 21.99114858 m. 3. On a sign: about 22 m.

**T11. Words with one meaning** (A true thing about math)
> Math words each mean one thing. A factor fits into a number; a multiple is in its times table. 5² means 5 × 5.
Worked example: 1. 6 fits into 12 two times. 2. So 6 is a factor of 12. 3. And 12 is a multiple of 6.

**T12. Choose a tool** (A true thing about math)
> Read the numbers first. Friendly numbers: in your head. Hard ones: write it out. Shapes: draw. Many cases: a table. A calculator checks, but you judge.
Worked example: 1. 25 × 4: friendly, so in your head: 100. 2. 167 × 3: write it out: 501. 3. Calculator: 501. It checks.

**T13. Look for a shortcut in the structure** (A true thing about math)
> Before using a long method, ask: what do I already know that this is like? 99 is 100 take away 1.
Worked example: 1. 47 × 99 = 47 × 100 − 47 × 1. 2. 4,700 − 47. 3. 4,653.

**T14. Same thing, different forms** (A true thing about math)
> A square is a special rectangle, so it keeps every rectangle fact. 1/8 = 0.125 = 12.5%. Pick the form that makes the job easy.
Worked example: 1. Which is more, 1/8 or 12%? 2. 1/8 = 12.5%. 3. 12.5% is more than 12%.

**T15. The model cycle** (A true thing about math)
> To use math on a real problem: say what you assume, build a model, work it out, test it against real data, then change it if needed.
Worked example: 1. Assume: each trip carries 30 kg. 2. Model: 120 ÷ 30 = 4 trips. 3. Data: trips carried about 25 kg. 4. Revise: 5 trips.

**T16. Round to fit the story** (A true thing about math)
> The story decides how to round. Boats for people: round up. Full crates: round down. Sometimes it divides exactly: no rounding.
Worked example: 1. 48 people, boats hold 8. 2. 48 ÷ 8 = 6. 3. Nobody left over: exactly 6 boats.

**T17. Fermi estimates** (A true thing about math)
> Some questions have no data to look up. Break them into pieces you can guess. Multiply the guesses. Aim for the right size, not the exact number.
Worked example (A made-up model): 1. How many eggs does Willowbank eat each week? 2. Guess 50 families, 3 eggs a day each. 3. 50 × 3 × 7 = 1,050: about 1,000.

**T18. One slip, many forms** (A true thing about math; Plan Card)
> A bar, an equation, a table and a grid can all show one slip. Each part keeps its meaning. = means both sides are the same amount.
Worked example: 1. 2 bags of b marbles and 3 loose make 11. 2. Equation: 2b + 3 = 11. 3. 11 − 3 = 8, so b = 4. 4. Grid point (2, 8): 2 bags hold 8.

## Real-world and history cards

**H1. Four steps, looked at again** (A true thing from history; Seedling and up)
> You may have met George Pólya's four steps at Pip's basket. His book *How to Solve It* (1945) says more about the last step: look back.
Keeper's note:
> Pólya taught at Stanford University. His "look back" step asks three things: check the result, try to get it a different way, and see if the method works on other problems. The Plan Card's last box asks the same.

**H2. Problem, answer, method** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> In Han dynasty China, about 2,000 years ago, a book called *The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art* was finished. Each problem gives the answer, then the method.
Keeper's note:
> Its problems are about fields, grain, taxes, canals and fair shares. Much later a mathematician named Liu Hui wrote notes explaining why the methods work. That is the "convince a skeptic" step, written down.

**H3. Questions that seem impossible** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk only)
> Enrico Fermi was a scientist who taught at the University of Chicago. He asked students questions that seemed impossible, then showed them how to break them into pieces.
Keeper's note:
> Questions like these are now called Fermi problems. School science teams in the United States still answer them in contests. They give only the right size, as a power of ten. Nobody expects the exact number.

## Tray (a): The Plan Card

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> Town problems with made-up numbers. Four boxes: Understand, Plan, Do, Look back.

**Opening:** Fill the four boxes. Start in any box.
**Box labels:** Understand: what do you know, and what is asked? · Plan: what are the steps? · Do: work each step · Look back: does it make sense? Check another way.
**Side lists:** Found · Still to find · Extra · Missing
**Buttons:** Add a step · Draw a bar · My estimate · Check my working · Try another way
**After Check my working:** Your answer matches the model. Now look back: does it fit your estimate?
**Try another way prompt:** Stuck? Swap the bar for a table, or work backward.

**Guided item:** Pip has $20. He buys 3 pies at $4 each. What's his change?
- Step 1: What's asked? *(His change.)*
- Step 2: Plan: pies first, then take from $20.
- Step 3: Estimate: less than $20.
- Step 4: 3 × 4 = 12; 20 − 12 = 8. Check: 8 + 12 = 20.

## Tray (d): The Tool Rack

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> Six tools on hooks: in your head, written, calculator, diagram, table, grid. Each card says what it's good for and its limit.

**Tool cards:**
| Tool | Good for | Its limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| In your head | Friendly numbers | Easy to slip on hard ones |
| Written | Big or awkward numbers | Slow for easy ones |
| Calculator | Checking your answer | Only right if you typed it right; you judge the size |
| Diagram | Shapes, fractions, parts of a whole | Rough if drawn roughly |
| Table | Many cases, ratios, patterns | Only shows the rows you made |
| Grid | Moving shapes, plotting points | Only as good as your plotting |

**Opening:** Read the numbers. Pick a tool. Then say why.

**Guided item:** Which tool for 398 + 247?
- Step 1: Read the numbers: 398 is close to 400.
- Step 2: In your head works: 400 + 247 = 647.
- Step 3: Take back the 2 extra: 645.

## Tray (e): The Shortcut Lens

**Model card** (A true thing about math):
> Structure means how a problem is built. Spot it, and a long sum can become a short one.

**Opening:** Look through the lens. What is this like?
**Buttons:** Split it · Round and fix · Change the form · Shape family · Show the area picture
**Shape family chart:** quadrilateral → parallelogram → rectangle → square (each one keeps every fact of the one before)

**Guided item:** Work out 23 × 101 with a shortcut.
- Step 1: 101 is 100 + 1.
- Step 2: 23 × 100 = 2,300, and 23 × 1 = 23.
- Step 3: 2,300 + 23 = 2,323.

## Tray (f): The Model Workshop

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> Town plans with made-up data. Assumptions sit on sliders you can change.

**Opening:** Say what you assume. Build it. Test it. Change it.
**Cycle labels:** Assume · Build · Work out · Test against data · Revise

## Problem sets

Twenty-two three-choice items put the right answer at A seven times, B eight times and C seven times.

### Set A. Plan, do, look back (Seedling and Sapling)

**A1 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: 320 apples. She packs 4 crates of 50. How many are left?
- A. 270, because "left" means take away 50. *(tests: keyword strategy, one step)*
- B. 120 *(right)*
- C. 520, adding the crates on. *(tests: wrong operation from the story)*
Hint: How many apples did she pack in all?
Explanation: Packed: 4 × 50 = 200. Left: 320 − 200 = 120.
Feedback A: "Left" doesn't say which step. Four crates went, not one.
Feedback B: Yes. Pack first, then take away.
Feedback C: Packing takes apples away from the pile.

**A2 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: 3 jars of honey at $4.85 each. Best estimate first?
- A. About $15 *(right)*
- B. About $12 *(tests: rounding $4.85 down to $4)*
- C. About $8 *(tests: adding 3 and $4.85)*
Hint: $4.85 is close to which whole dollar?
Explanation: $4.85 is nearly $5. 3 × $5 = $15. The exact cost is $14.55.
Feedback A: Yes. Nearly $5 each, three times.
Feedback B: $4.85 is much nearer $5 than $4.
Feedback C: Three jars means three lots of $4.85.

**A3 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Pip says 480 − 195 = 385. Which check helps most?
- A. It looks about right to me, so move on to the next slip. *(tests: no check)*
- B. Add the two first numbers: 480 + 195 = 675. That's far bigger. *(tests: undoing the wrong way)*
- C. 385 + 195 = 580. That's not 480, so look again. *(right)*
Hint: Undo the take-away. What should you get back?
Explanation: Adding back should give 480. It gives 580, so 385 is 100 too big. It's 285.
Feedback A: About 500 − 200 = 300. 385 is far off.
Feedback B: To undo taking 195, add 195 to the answer.
Feedback C: Yes. The check catches it: the answer is 285.

**A4 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: 156 letters, bags of 12. 4 bags go upstream. How many letters stay?
Answer: 108 letters.
Worked steps: 1. Bags: 156 ÷ 12 = 13. 2. Bags that stay: 13 − 4 = 9. 3. Letters: 9 × 12 = 108.
Tests: 9 (bags, not letters); 152 (taking 4 letters, not 4 bags).
Hint: Is the question about bags or letters?
Explanation: Another way: 4 bags hold 48 letters. 156 − 48 = 108.

**A5 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: 3/5 of 40 seed packets are reed. Half the reed goes upstream. How many?
Answer: 12 packets.
Worked steps: 1. Estimate: 3/5 is more than half, so more than 20 reed. 2. 1/5 of 40 = 8, so 3/5 = 24. 3. Half of 24 = 12.
Tests: 20 (half of all 40); 8 (only 1/5); 24 (stopping after step 1).
Hint: Find the reed packets first. Then halve.
Explanation: Two steps: the reed share, then half of it.

### Set B. Extra, missing and longer problems (Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**B1 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: How much money does the baker have left? Which fact on the slip is extra?
Facts on the slip: 36 loaves sold · $3 each · his oven is 12 years old · $40 on flour.
- A. He spends $40 on flour *(tests: dropping a needed fact)*
- B. He sells 36 loaves at $3 *(tests: dropping a needed fact)*
- C. The oven is 12 years old *(right)*
Hint: Which fact changes his money?
Explanation: The oven's age changes nothing. He has 36 × $3 − $40 = $68.
Feedback A: The flour costs money, so you need it.
Feedback B: You need the loaves to know what he made.
Feedback C: Yes. Use the rest: $108 − $40 = $68.

**B2 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Bramble moves 50 sacks by wagon. How many trips? Which fact is missing?
- A. How many sacks fit in one load. *(right)*
- B. How far away each trip has to go. *(tests: asking for a fact that doesn't change the answer)*
- C. Nothing is missing, so it's 50 trips. *(tests: grabbing the only number)*
Hint: What would you divide 50 by?
Explanation: Trips depend on the wagon's load. Distance changes the time, not the number of trips.
Feedback A: Yes. That fact is missing.
Feedback B: Distance changes how long, not how many trips.
Feedback C: 50 trips only if the wagon holds one sack.

**B3 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: Seedlings cost $2.40. Vadoalto wants 3 dozen, with 25% off. Total?
Answer: $64.80.
Worked steps: 1. Estimate: 36 × $2.50 = $90; three quarters of that is about $67. 2. 36 × $2.40 = $86.40. 3. 25% off leaves 75%: $86.40 × 0.75 = $64.80.
Tests: $21.60 (the discount, not the price paid); $86.40 (forgot the discount); $5.40 (3 seedlings, not 3 dozen).
Hint: How many is 3 dozen? Then take off a quarter.
Explanation: Answer the question asked: the price paid, in dollars and cents.

**B4 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: 60 of 80 trees survived. Pip writes "133% survived". Your reply?
- A. 133% is fine, because a percent can be any number at all. *(tests: no size sense for a part of a whole)*
- B. Part of the trees can't top 100%. It's 75%. *(right)*
- C. It should say 60%, since 60 of the trees survived. *(tests: reading a count as a percent)*
Hint: Can more than all the trees survive?
Explanation: 60 out of 80 is 3/4, or 75%. Pip divided 80 by 60, the other way round.
Feedback A: A part can't be more than the whole group. A jug filled 2.5 times is 250%, though.
Feedback B: Yes. 60 ÷ 80 = 0.75 = 75%.
Feedback C: 60 is the count. Out of 80, that's 75%.

**B5 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: 2 jars of honey go with every 3 cups of oats. 14 jars: oats?
Answer: 21 cups of oats.
Worked steps (ratio table): 1. Jars 2, cups 3. 2. 14 jars is 7 lots of 2. 3. 7 × 3 = 21 cups.
Tests: 15 (adding 1, as if 2 → 3 means "one more"); 9.33 (multiplying by 2/3, the ratio upside down).
Hint: How many lots of 2 jars are in 14?
Explanation: A ratio grows by multiplying both sides. Check: 14 : 21 is 2 : 3.

**B6 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: Pip and Bramble have 30 ribbons. Bramble has 6 more. How many has Pip?
Answer: 12 ribbons.
Worked steps: 1. Let Pip's be p. Bramble's: p + 6. 2. p + p + 6 = 30, so 2p = 24. 3. p = 12. Check: 12 + 18 = 30.
Tests: 15 (halving 30 and forgetting the 6); 18 (Bramble's ribbons, not Pip's).
Hint: Draw two bars. One is 6 longer.
Explanation: Take away the extra 6 first. Then the rest splits equally.

**B7 (Keeper's Desk, match)**
Slip: Hob packs 3 sacks with the same number of apples. 5 apples are loose. 41 in all.
Equation on the slip: 3s + 5 = 41. Grid: sacks across, apples up.
Prompt: Match each part to what it means in the slip.
- 3s → the apples in the 3 sacks
- 5 → the loose apples
- = 41 → the same amount as all the apples
- the point (3, 36) → 3 sacks hold 36 apples
Then: How many apples in one sack? *Answer: 12 (41 − 5 = 36; 36 ÷ 3 = 12).*
Tests: = read as "work it out" (41 matched to the sacks); 5 matched to one sack; (3, 36) read as 36 sacks.
Hint: Draw the bar first: 3 equal parts and 5 loose.
Explanation: Bar, equation and grid show one slip. 41 − 5 = 36 in sacks, so 12 each.

### Set C. Convince me (Seedling, Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**C1 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Bramble says 1/5 is bigger than 1/3. Which reason convinces a friend?
- A. Cut equal strips into 5 and 3. Fifths are smaller. *(right)*
- B. My answer is different from hers, so hers can't be right. *(tests: judging by answers, not reasons)*
- C. Thirds are just bigger. Everyone in town knows that. *(tests: saying it louder, no reason)*
Hint: Picture the same strip cut two ways.
Explanation: More equal parts of the same whole means smaller parts. So 1/5 is less than 1/3.
Feedback A: Yes. The strips show it, and anyone can check.
Feedback B: A different answer hints at a problem. It doesn't show where or why.
Feedback C: "Everyone knows" is not a reason. Show it with a strip.

**C2 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Pip worked 71 − 26 = 55. Which step broke?
Pip's steps: 1. Ones: 6 − 1 = 5. 2. Tens: 7 − 2 = 5. 3. Answer: 55.
- A. Step 3, because my own answer came out as 45. *(tests: judging by answers, not steps)*
- B. Step 2, because the tens 7 − 2 don't make 5. *(tests: blaming a step that works)*
- C. Step 1: it's 1 − 6, so exchange first. *(right)*
Hint: Look at the ones. Which number is on top?
Explanation: 1 − 6 needs an exchange: 11 − 6 = 5, then 6 − 2 = 4 tens. 45.
Feedback A: Point to the first step that stops making sense.
Feedback B: 7 − 2 is 5. Look earlier.
Feedback C: Yes. He swapped the numbers to avoid the exchange.

**C3 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Dot says multiplying always makes bigger: 4 × 3 = 12. Best reply?
- A. Agreed. That example proves it for every number. *(tests: one example as proof)*
- B. 4 × 1/2 = 2, so not always. *(right)*
- C. We can't tell until we've tried a hundred examples. *(tests: asking for more evidence than "not always" needs)*
Hint: Try multiplying by something less than 1.
Explanation: One case that fails is enough to show a rule isn't always true.
Feedback A: One example shows it can happen. "Always" needs more.
Feedback B: Yes. One counterexample settles it.
Feedback C: To show "not always", one failing case is enough.

**C4 (Keeper's Desk, say why; spoken to a grown-up, not stored)**
Prompt: Convince a skeptic: odd + odd is always even.
Model answer for the grown-up: An odd number is pairs plus one left over. Two odd numbers have two left-over ones. Those two make one more pair. So the total is all pairs: even. Examples alone (3 + 5 = 8) convince yourself, not a skeptic.
Tests: one example as proof; "it just is".
Hint: Draw an odd number as pairs. What's left?
Explanation: A skeptic needs a reason that works every time, not one example.

**C5 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Bramble got 47 × 99 = 4,663. "It's near 4,700, so it's proved." Your reply?
- A. Near 4,700 shows the size only. Check another way. *(right)*
- B. Yes. Being close to the estimate proves every digit. *(tests: an estimate as proof)*
- C. Estimates are no use at all for checking. *(tests: throwing away a useful check)*
Hint: Could a different answer also be near 4,700?
Explanation: 4,700 − 47 = 4,653. An estimate catches big mistakes, not small ones.
Feedback A: Yes. 4,700 − 47 = 4,653, so 4,663 is 10 off.
Feedback B: 4,653 is near 4,700 too. Only one can be right.
Feedback C: The estimate is useful. It just can't check the last digits.

### Set D. Say it exactly (Seedling, Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**D1 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: A rug is 9 cm by 7 cm. What is its area?
- A. 63 *(tests: no unit)*
- B. 63 cm *(tests: a length unit for an area)*
- C. 63 cm² *(right)*
Hint: Area counts squares. What size is each square?
Explanation: 63 squares, each 1 cm on a side: 63 cm².
Feedback A: 63 what? Add the unit.
Feedback B: cm is a length. Area counts squares.
Feedback C: Yes. Squares, so cm².

**D2 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: The boat leaves at 15:45. Which is the same time?
- A. 3:45 pm *(right)*
- B. 15:45 pm *(tests: mixing two ways of telling time)*
- C. 5:45 pm *(tests: taking away 10, not 12)*
Hint: Take 12 from the hours after noon.
Explanation: 15 − 12 = 3, so 15:45 is 3:45 pm. 24-hour times never use am or pm.
Feedback A: Yes. 15 − 12 = 3.
Feedback B: 15:45 already means afternoon. Don't add pm.
Feedback C: A day has 12 hours before noon. Take away 12.

**D3 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which sentence about 6 and 12 is precise?
- A. 6 is a multiple of 12. *(tests: swapping factor and multiple)*
- B. 6 is a factor of 12. *(right)*
- C. 6 and 12 are factors of each other. *(tests: thinking the words work both ways)*
Hint: Which number fits into the other?
Explanation: 6 fits into 12, so 6 is a factor of 12. 12 is a multiple of 6.
Feedback A: 12 is in the 6 times table. So 12 is the multiple.
Feedback B: Yes. 6 fits into 12 exactly.
Feedback C: 12 doesn't fit into 6. The words go one way.

**D4 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: What does 5² mean?
- A. 5 × 2 = 10 *(tests: reading the small 2 as "times 2")*
- B. 5 × 5 = 25 *(right)*
- C. 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 32 *(tests: swapping the two numbers)*
Hint: Draw a square with sides of 5.
Explanation: 5² is "five squared": a square 5 by 5 holds 25.
Feedback A: The small 2 counts how many 5s are multiplied.
Feedback B: Yes. Five squared is 25.
Feedback C: That's 2⁵. In 5², the 5 is multiplied.

**D5 (Keeper's Desk, match)**
Prompt: Match each word to its example.
- expression → 3x + 5
- equation → 3x + 5 = 11
- formula → A = l × w
Tests: calling 3x + 5 an equation; thinking a formula is just any sum.
Hint: Look for an equals sign. What does it link?
Explanation: An expression has no equals sign. An equation says two things are equal. A formula links quantities.

**D6 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: A water tank is 50 cm by 40 cm by 30 cm. Volume, with unit?
Answer: 60,000 cm³ (60 liters).

Tests: 60,000 cm² (an area unit for a volume); 120 cm (adding the sides).
Hint: How many cubes fill one layer? How many layers?
Explanation: Volume fills cubes, so cm³. 1,000 cm³ is 1 liter, so 60 liters.

**D7 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: A pond's path circles 7 m across. Calculator: 21.99114858 m. Sign says?
- A. 21.99114858 m *(tests: more digits than anyone can measure)*
- B. Exactly 20 m *(tests: rounding, then calling it exact)*
- C. About 22 m *(right)*
Hint: Could anyone pace that path to a millionth of a meter?
Explanation: "About 22 m" is as exact as the job needs. The pond's radius is 3.5 m.
Feedback A: Nobody can walk it that exactly. Round to fit the sign.
Feedback B: 20 is too far off, and it's not exact.
Feedback C: Yes. Rounded, and marked "about".

- B. 167 × 3 *(tests: going mental on awkward numbers)*

Explanation: Four 25s make 100. The others are easier written out.
Feedback A: Yes. Four quarters of 100.

**E3 (Sapling, number)**

Answer: 4,653.

Tests: 4,601 (taking away 99, not 47); 4,606 (taking away two 47s).
Hint: 99 is one less than 100.
Explanation: 47 × 99 is 99 lots of 47: 100 lots, minus one lot.

**E4 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which way makes 25 × 36 easiest?
- A. Only long multiplication is safe. *(tests: always using the long method)*
- B. 25 × 30 + 6 *(tests: splitting but not multiplying both parts)*
- C. 25 × 4 × 9 *(right)*
Hint: 36 is 4 × 9. What is 25 × 4?
Explanation: 25 × 4 = 100, and 100 × 9 = 900.
Feedback A: It works, but a shortcut is quicker and checks it.
Feedback B: The 6 needs multiplying by 25 too.
Feedback C: Yes. 100 × 9 = 900.

**E5 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**

- A. 12% of $64, because 12 is bigger than 8. *(tests: comparing the numbers, not the amounts)*
- B. 1/8 of $64, because 1/8 is 12.5%. *(right)*

**E6 (Keeper's Desk, number)**

Answer: 12 sunflower seeds.

### Set F. Model it (Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**F1 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: 75 people go to market. Each boat holds 8. How many boats?
- A. 9.375 boats *(tests: leaving the bare answer)*
- B. 9 boats *(tests: rounding the usual way, leaving people behind)*
- C. 10 boats *(right)*

Explanation: 75 ÷ 8 = 9.375. Nine boats carry 72, leaving 3 people. They need a tenth boat.
Feedback A: Boats come whole. Round for the story.
Feedback B: Nine boats carry 72. Three people wait on the bank.
Feedback C: Yes. Round up so everyone goes.

**F2 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: Pack 75 apples in crates of 8. How many full crates?
Answer: 9 full crates (3 apples over).
Worked steps: 1. 75 ÷ 8 = 9.375. 2. A full crate needs 8. 3. 9 crates are full; 3 apples are left.
Tests: 10 (rounding up as for boats); 9.375 (the bare answer).
Hint: Is a crate with 3 apples full?
Explanation: Same numbers as the boats, different story: here you round down.

**F3 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: A honey cake for 6 uses 150 g flour. How much for 15?
Answer: 375 g.
Worked steps: 1. For 1 person: 150 ÷ 6 = 25 g. 2. For 15: 25 × 15 = 375 g. 3. Check: 15 is 2.5 times 6, and 150 × 2.5 = 375.
Tests: 159 (adding 9, not scaling); 2.5 (the scale factor, with no grams).
Hint: How much flour for one person?
Explanation: Scaling multiplies. 9 more people need 9 more shares, not 9 more grams.

**F4 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**

## "I'm stuck" worked examples

Step 2 of the ladder: an easier case, then how to start this one.

- **Plan items:** "Dot has 50 stamps. She uses 2 sheets of 10: 50 − 20 = 30. Start: what happens first?"
- **Estimate items:** "2 pies at $9.90 is about 2 × $10 = $20. Start: round each price."
- **Check items:** "10 − 4 = 6. Undo: 6 + 4 = 10. Start: add back what was taken."
- **Extra or missing items:** "'3 cats, each eats 2 fish. The cats are orange.' Color is extra. Start: which facts change the answer?"
- **Ratio items:** "1 cup of oats for 2 cups of milk. 4 cups of oats: 8 of milk. Start: how many lots?"
- **Forms items:** "2 boxes of 4 pens and 1 loose: 2 × 4 + 1 = 9. Start: point to each part in the bar."
- **Percent items:** "3 of 4 pies sold is 3/4, or 75%. Start: part ÷ whole."
- **Convince items:** "Pip says all birds fly. A penguin doesn't. One case settles it. Start: try to break the claim."
- **Broken-step items:** "In 52 − 17, 2 − 7 needs an exchange: 12 − 7. Start: check each step in order."
- **Unit items:** "A card 2 cm by 3 cm covers 6 cm². Start: are you counting lengths, squares or cubes?"
- **Volume items:** "A box 2 by 3 by 4 cm holds 24 cm³. Start: one layer, then the layers."
- **Tool items:** "50 × 2: in your head. 386 × 7: write it. Start: are the numbers friendly?"
- **Structure items:** "12 × 9 = 12 × 10 − 12 = 108. Start: what is this number close to?"
- **Rounding items:** "10 people, boats of 4: 2.5, so 3 boats. Start: can you have part of one?"
- **Model items:** "Guess 2 eggs a cake. Bakers used 3. Change the guess. Start: what did you assume?"

## Key terms (glossary)

- **multi-step problem**: a problem you solve in more than one step.
- **plan**: the steps you'll take, named before you start.
- **estimate**: a quick, rough answer that shows the size.
- **check**: a second way to see if an answer is right.
- **inverse**: an operation that undoes another, like + and −.
- **extra information**: a fact the problem gives that you don't need.
- **missing information**: a fact you need that the problem leaves out.
- **unknown**: the number you're trying to find.
- **argument**: reasons linked together to show something is true.
- **counterexample**: one case that shows a rule isn't always true.
- **skeptic**: someone who needs a good reason before they believe.
- **unit**: what you're counting or measuring in, like cm or dollars.
- **cm² (square centimeters)**: squares 1 cm on a side, for area.
- **cm³ (cubic centimeters)**: cubes 1 cm on a side, for volume.
- **approximately (≈)**: about; close, not exact.
- **factor**: a number that fits into another exactly.
- **multiple**: a number in another number's times table.
- **squared**: multiplied by itself, written with a small 2: 5².
- **expression**: numbers, letters and signs with no equals sign.
- **equation**: two amounts joined by =, which says they are the same.
- **formula**: a rule linking quantities, like A = l × w.
- **radius**: the distance from a circle's center to its edge.
- **diameter**: the distance across a circle through its center.
- **circumference**: the distance all the way around a circle.
- **ratio**: how much of one thing for each amount of another.
- **structure**: how a problem or number is built.
- **model**: math that stands for a real situation.
- **average (mean)**: add the amounts, then share the total equally: 26, 24, 27 and 23 kg average 25 kg.
- **assumption**: something you decide to treat as true for now.
- **Fermi estimate**: a rough answer from guessing pieces and multiplying.
- **power of ten**: 10, 100, 1,000 and so on, each ten times the one before.

**A Fermi question at home** (Sapling and Keeper's Desk): pick a question nobody can look up, like "How many slices of bread does our street eat in a week?" Break it into guesses together. Write each guess. Multiply. Then ask: which guess would change the answer most? *Grown-up note: talk about the street in general; no need to count real households or share family details.*

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** planning, doing and checking multi-step problems, including ones with extra or missing facts; estimating first and checking by undoing or another method; building and critiquing arguments (convince yourself, a friend, a skeptic; find the broken step); precise units, words and rounding; choosing a tool for the job; spotting structure that turns long sums into short ones; and moving one slip between a bar, an equation and a grid; and the modeling cycle, including rounding to fit the story and Fermi estimates.

**What it does not claim:** "Say why" answers are spoken and never graded. Spotting a broken step on screen is practice for, not proof of, explaining in your own words. Town prices, recipes and trips are made up. Volume, ratio and percent methods are recapped here and taught in their own packs.
