## Arriving

**Place title:** The Cabinet of Little Drawers

**Hook (first visit):** The storm shook an old cabinet in the library. More than a hundred little drawers, and every card on the floor.

**Scene, Short:** The librarian found a cabinet of 118 tiny drawers. Each has a letter or two on the front. The cards fell out. Can you put them back?

**Scene, Longer:** *A story from Willowbank.*
> Behind the library's storm-wet boxes stands a cabinet nobody remembers buying. It came from the old school, the librarian thinks. It has 118 little drawers, each with a letter or two on the front: H, O, Fe, Au.
>
> The storm rattled every card onto the floor.
>
> "Can you eat any of them?" asks Pip, holding the card marked Na.
>
> "That one is sodium," says the librarian. "On its own, it fizzes fiercely in water. Please don't eat it. Luckily, it's a card."
>
> Bramble's magpie lands on the top drawer with a shiny bead. "She only steals the metal ones," says Bramble.
>
> {guide} picks up a card. "Every drawer is one kind of tiny piece. Everything in Willowbank is built from them. Let's find the order."

**Keeper's note:** *A true thing from science.* Every material you know, from bread to the bridge, is made from atoms of about a hundred kinds. Chemists have named 118 elements. The cabinet has one drawer for each.

**Later visits:** The drawers are waiting. Which one shall we open?

**Drawer menu:** The Squeeze Box · The Snap Tray · Mendeleev's Cards · The Color Jar

## (a) The Squeeze Box

**Teach card 1:** Everything is made of particles far too small to see. In our model, gas particles are far apart. Between them is empty space.
- Worked example, one step per tap: 1. Here is a sealed syringe of air. 2. Push the plunger. 3. It goes in, from 60 to 30. 4. The particles got closer. None escaped.

**Teach card 2:** Try the water syringe. The particles already touch. There is almost no space to close, so it hardly moves.

**Counting window line:** Same stuff, three states. Count the dots in each little window.
**After counting:** Fewer particles in the same space means less heavy for its size. That's why gases are so light.

**Model line:** *A made-up model.* Real syringes have some friction, and the numbers are tidy.
**Sand syringe line:** The grains are solid. The air between them squashes a little.

**The Ice Jar, teach card:** Most liquids shrink when they freeze. Water grows. Its particles lock into an open pattern with gaps.
- Worked example: 1. 100 mL of water, 100 g. 2. Freeze it. 3. The level rises to about 109 mL. 4. The scale still reads 100 g. 5. Drop the cube in water. It floats.

**The Pan Curve, teach card:** Heat ice in a pan and draw its temperature. Watch for flat parts.
- Worked example: 1. Ice starts at −10 °C. 2. It warms to 0 °C. 3. The line goes flat: the ice is melting. 4. Water warms to 100 °C. 5. Flat again: it's boiling.
- **Picture line:** In the picture, the baker does the heating. You tap "next minute."
- **Model line:** *A made-up model with tidy numbers.* Real pans heat unevenly.

## (b) The Snap Tray

**Teach card 1:** An element has one kind of atom. A compound has two or more kinds, joined.
- Worked example: 1. Snap two H to one O. 2. The tray reads H₂O. 3. Two kinds joined: a compound. It's water.

**Teach card 2:** The small number counts the atom just before it. H₂O means 2 hydrogen, 1 oxygen.

**Teach card 3:** A compound can act nothing like its elements. Sodium fizzes in water. Chlorine is a poisonous gas. Together: table salt.

**Try to separate line (mixture):** The sieve worked. The sand and water were only mixed.
**Try to separate line (water):** Nothing came apart. Hydrogen and oxygen are joined, not mixed.

**Atom picture (Keeper's Desk):** A tiny center, the nucleus, with electrons around it. Not to scale: the real nucleus is far, far smaller.
**Model line:** *A model.* Real atoms are not colored balls.

## (c) Mendeleev's Cards

**Teach card:** Lay the cards in order of weight. When a property comes back, start a new row.
- Worked example: 1. Lithium: soft metal, fizzes in water. 2. Then beryllium, boron, carbon and the rest. 3. Sodium fizzes too. 4. New row, so sodium sits under lithium.

**Row and column labels:** Row: a period. Column: a group.
**Gap appears:** Nothing fits here. Should you squash the row up, or leave a gap?
**Gap prompt:** Look left, right, up and down. What should this missing element be like?
**Gap choices:** Metal · Non-metal. Its weight is between ___ and ___.
**After a prediction:** Written in the drawer. Now wait for a letter.

**Archive card: Mendeleev's gaps.** *A true thing from history.*
> In 1869 Dmitri Mendeleev made a table of the elements known then. Where nothing fit, he left gaps. In 1871 he described one missing metal under aluminum: a weight of about 68, and quite heavy for its size. Four years later, in France, a chemist found it.

**Archive card: The Curies.** *A true thing from history.*
> In Paris in 1898, Marie Curie and Pierre Curie announced two new elements. She named one polonium, after Poland, where she was born. She won Nobel Prizes in 1903 and 1911.

**Archive card: Dalton's symbols.** *A true thing from history.*
> John Dalton, in Manchester, wrote down his idea of atoms in 1803. In 1808 he published it in a book, with a little round symbol for each kind of atom he knew.

**Metal or not? teach card:** One clue is not enough. Metals usually shine, bend, carry heat and electricity, and are solid.
- Worked example: 1. Copper: shiny, bends, conducts. 2. Every clue says metal. 3. Copper is a metal.

**Exception cards:** Mercury: a metal, but liquid at room temperature. · Graphite (pencil "lead"): a non-metal that conducts. · Gallium: a metal that melts below body temperature.
**Cabinet shading line:** Metals on the left and middle. Non-metals up on the right. A staircase in between.
**Links:** Test conductors at the Switch Table (spec 163). Test magnets with the miller's sweep (spec 154).

## (d) The Color Jar

**Teach card 1:** Some juice from red cabbage changes color. Acids turn it pink. Alkalis turn it blue, green or yellow.
- Worked example: 1. Purple cabbage water. 2. Add lemon juice. 3. It turns pink. 4. Lemon juice is an acid.

**Teach card 2:** The pH scale runs 0 to 14. Below 7 is acidic. Exactly 7 is neutral. Above 7 is alkaline.

**Teach card 3:** pH tells acidity, not danger. Strong alkalis, like oven cleaner, can burn skin, just like strong acids.

**Neutralize line:** Drop by drop, the pink fades to purple. The acid and alkali are cancelling out toward 7.

**Thermometer teach card:** Warmer surroundings: energy came out. Colder surroundings: energy went in.
- Worked example: 1. Vinegar reads 20 °C. 2. Add baking soda. It fizzes. 3. Now 16 °C. 4. Colder, so energy went in.
- **Model line:** *Made-up readings.* Real ones depend on the amounts.

**Atom window line:** Melting: the molecules stay whole and move apart. Rusting: atoms regroup into something new.
**Balloon scale line:** *On screen only. Never seal a real fizzing bottle.*

### Letters up and down the river (Act III, chapter 4)

**Hook:** Two letters came for the cabinet. Each one fills a drawer.

**Short:** Nuria writes about tungsten, found in Spain. Léon writes about gallium, named after France. You file both cards.

**Longer:** *A story from Willowbank, with true things from history.*
> Dot hands over two envelopes.
>
> Nuria writes: "¡Hola! Hello! Our teacher says tungsten was first got out of its rock in Spain, in Bergara, in 1783, by two brothers called Elhuyar. It is very heavy. In Spanish we still call it *wolframio*."
>
> Léon writes: "Bonjour ! Hello! Here is the one for your gap. In 1875 a French chemist found a new metal. He named it gallium, after Gallia, the old Latin name for France. It was the metal Mendeleev had described."

>

**Keeper's note:** *A true thing from history.* Mendeleev had predicted a weight of about 68 and a density near 6. Gallium's weight is 69.7. Lecoq de Boisbaudran first measured a lower density; after rechecking he got 5.9.

### The pink soup (Act II, chapter 2)

**Hook:** The café's red cabbage soup just turned pink. Puff is alarmed.

**Short:** The orchard keeper splashed apple vinegar into the purple soup. It went pink. Nothing is wrong. It's chemistry.

**Longer:** *A story from Willowbank.*
> The garden's red cabbages are in. The café makes soup, deep purple.
>
> The orchard keeper adds a splash of her apple vinegar. The whole pot turns pink.
>

**Keeper's note:** *A true thing from science.* The color comes from anthocyanin in the leaves. *A true thing from history:* Søren Sørensen, at a laboratory in Copenhagen, gave us the pH scale in 1909.

### Before the evidence (Act IV, chapter 1, Keeper's Desk)

**Hook:** Bramble says one flood five years later proves nothing. Mendeleev's gaps show what a good prediction looks like.

**Short:** Mendeleev wrote his prediction down first. Then others checked it. That's how you test an idea fairly.

**Keeper's note:** A guess made after the result can always fit. A prediction made before it can fail. Mendeleev's could have failed. It didn't.

## Problem sets

Each item lists prompt, choices (right answer marked ✔), misconception tags, hint and explanation. Feedback for a wrong choice is the explanation's first sentence unless given.

### Set A. Tiny pieces (Sprout, Seedling, Sapling, Keeper's Desk)

**A1 (Sprout, guided).** Steps shown: 1. The syringe is sealed. 2. It holds air. 3. Push.
- Prompt: You push a sealed syringe of air. What happens?
- Choices: It won't move at all (`gas-cannot-squash`) · It goes in part way ✔ · Air leaks out the end (`gas-escapes`)
- Hint: The end is sealed. Can the air get closer together?
- Explanation: The air squashes into less space. Nothing leaks; the particles just get closer.

**A2 (Seedling).**
- Prompt: Why does air squash but water hardly does?
- Choices: Air particles are much smaller (`bigger-particles-in-solids`) · Water is heavier than air (`heavy-means-hard`) · Air has gaps between particles ✔
- Hint: Look at the particle windows. How are the dots spaced?
- Explanation: Gas particles are far apart, so there is room to push them closer. Water's already touch.

**A3 (Sapling, number).**
- Prompt: 100 mL of water freezes. About how much ice? What does it weigh?
- Answer: About 109 mL, still 100 g. Worked steps: water grows about 9% on freezing; 100 + 9 = 109; nothing was added or lost, so the mass stays 100 g.
- Misconception tested: `mass-changes-on-freezing` (an answer with a new mass).
- Hint: Water is the odd one out: it grows when it freezes.
- Explanation: Same particles, more spread out. The space grows; the mass doesn't.

**A4 (Sapling).**
- Prompt: Why does an ice cube float in water?
- Choices: It freezes into an open pattern ✔ · Ice is full of air bubbles (`ice-has-air`) · Ice particles are lighter than water's (`particles-change`)
- Hint: Look at the Ice Jar's particle window. What do you see?
- Explanation: The open pattern makes ice less heavy for its size. Even clear ice with no bubbles floats.

**A5 (Sapling, number).** Uses the Pan Curve.
- Prompt: The line is flat from minute 2 to minute 8. For how long?
- Answer: 6 minutes. Worked steps: 8 − 2 = 6.
- Keeper extension: From minute 8 to 18, water warms from 0 °C to 100 °C. How much per minute? 100 ÷ 10 = 10 °C per minute.
- Misconception tested: `temp-keeps-rising`.
- Hint: Count the minutes along the flat part.
- Explanation: For 6 minutes, the ice melted and the thermometer stayed at 0 °C.

**A6 (Sapling).**
- Prompt: Heat still goes in. Why does the line stay flat?
- Choices: The heat escapes into the air (`heat-escapes`) · Ice is too cold for heat (`freezing-adds-cold`) · The energy loosens the particles ✔
- Hint: What is changing during the flat part? Look at the particles.
- Explanation: The energy goes into breaking the pattern, not speeding particles up. When all is melted, it climbs again.

**A7 (Keeper's Desk, say why to a grown-up).**
- Prompt: A puddle dries on a cool day. A pot boils. Tell a grown-up two differences.
- What a good answer includes: evaporation happens at the surface, at any temperature; boiling happens all through the liquid at the boiling point; the bubbles are water vapor, not air.
- Misconceptions to listen for: `boil-is-evaporate`, `bubbles-are-air`.
- Hint: Where does the water leave from in each one?
- Explanation: Both make vapor. Evaporation is slow and from the top; boiling is fast and from inside.

### Set B. The cabinet (Seedling, Sapling, Keeper's Desk)

**B1 (Seedling, guided).** Steps shown: 1. H₂ means two H. 2. O alone means one O. 3. Add them.
- Prompt: How many atoms are in one H₂O?
- Choices: 3 ✔ · 2 (`counts-symbols-only`) · 4 (`subscript-multiplies-all`)
- Hint: The 2 belongs to the H only.
- Explanation: Two hydrogen plus one oxygen makes three atoms.

**B2 (Seedling).**
- Prompt: O₂ has two atoms, both oxygen. What is it?
- Choices: A compound (`two-atoms-means-compound`) · A mixture (`joined-is-mixture`) · An element ✔
- Hint: How many kinds of atom are there?
- Explanation: Only one kind of atom, so it's an element, even with two joined.

**B3 (Sapling, sort).** Sort into Element, Compound, Mixture: gold (Au) · water (H₂O) · salt (NaCl) · oxygen (O₂) · sand and water · carbon dioxide (CO₂).
- Answer: Element: gold, oxygen. Compound: water, salt, carbon dioxide. Mixture: sand and water.
- Misconceptions tested: `two-atoms-means-compound` (oxygen as compound), `compound-is-mixture` (salt as mixture), `element-is-atom`.
- Hint: Ask two questions: how many kinds of atom? Are they joined?
- Explanation: Elements have one kind. Compounds have kinds joined. Sand and water are only together, so a filter splits them.

**B4 (Keeper's Desk, number).**
- Prompt: Sugar is C₆H₁₂O₆. How many atoms? Then: how many H in 2H₂O?
- Answer: 24 atoms (6 + 12 + 6). In 2H₂O: 4 hydrogen and 2 oxygen (two molecules of H₂O).
- Misconception tested: `subscript-multiplies-all` (small numbers), `big-number-ignored` (the front 2).
- Hint: A small number counts one atom. A big front number counts whole molecules.
- Explanation: Add the small numbers for one molecule. A front number copies the whole molecule.

**B5 (Sapling).**
- Prompt: Pip says salt has chlorine in it, so it's poisonous. You say?
- Choices: True, it keeps chlorine's poison (`compound-keeps-element-properties`) · No, salt acts in new ways ✔ · Only until you sieve it out (`compound-separable`)
- Hint: Does table salt act like a green poisonous gas?
- Explanation: Joined atoms make a new substance. Salt acts nothing like sodium or chlorine.

**B6 (Sapling).**
- Prompt: Lithium, sodium and potassium all fizz in water. Where do they sit?
- Choices: In one column, a group ✔ · In one row, a period (`rows-are-families`) · In order of discovery (`ordered-by-discovery`)
- Hint: In Mendeleev's Cards, where did sodium go?
- Explanation: Elements that behave alike line up in a column. Rows go up in weight.

**B7 (Keeper's Desk, number).**
- Prompt: Mendeleev predicted about 68. Gallium weighs 69.7. How close? How many years?
- Answer: 1.7 apart (69.7 − 68), about 2% off. Predicted 1871, found 1875: 4 years.
- Misconception tested: `table-is-arbitrary`.
- Hint: Take away the smaller number. Then do the same with the years.
- Explanation: A prediction this close, made before anyone found it, is why the table is trusted.

**B8 (Sapling).** Bramble's magpie.
- Prompt: Bramble's magpie brings a shiny glass bead. Is it metal?
- Choices: Yes, because it shines (`shiny-means-metal`) · Yes, magpies take metal (`magpie-myth`) · Can't tell from shine ✔
- Hint: Does glass bend, or carry electricity?
- Explanation: Glass shines but is brittle and doesn't conduct. One clue isn't enough.
- **True thing from science** (after answering): In a 2014 study at the University of Exeter, magpies were wary of new objects, shiny or not. They were not drawn to shiny ones.

**B9 (Keeper's Desk, true / not true).**
- "All metals are solid." Not true (`all-metals-solid`): mercury is liquid at room temperature.
- "Graphite is a non-metal that conducts." True (`nonmetals-never-conduct` if marked not true).
- "Every metal sticks to a magnet." Not true (`all-metals-magnetic`): copper and aluminum don't.
- Hint: Check the three exception cards.
- Explanation: Metal and non-metal are strong patterns with real exceptions.

### Set C. The color jar (Sprout, Seedling, Sapling, Keeper's Desk)

**C1 (Sprout, guided).** Steps shown: 1. Purple cabbage water. 2. Add lemon juice. 3. Look at the color.
- Prompt: Lemon juice turns the cabbage water pink. Lemon juice is…
- Choices: an alkali (`swap-acid-alkali`) · neutral (`neutral-is-no-ph`) · an acid ✔
- Hint: Look back at the Color Jar's first card.
- Explanation: Pink shows an acid. Lemon juice is about pH 2.

**C2 (Seedling).**
- Prompt: Plain water reads 7. What does 7 mean?
- Choices: Neutral ✔ · No pH at all (`neutral-is-no-ph`) · A strong acid (`swap-acid-alkali`)
- Hint: Find 7 on the strip. What's on each side?
- Explanation: 7 is a real reading: neutral, between acid and alkali.

**C3 (Seedling).**
- Prompt: You add baking soda water to pink vinegar. Which way does pH go?
- Choices: Down toward 0 (`more-mixing-more-acid`) · Up toward 7 ✔ · It can't change (`indicator-only-color`)
- Hint: Watch the color. Pink is going purple.
- Explanation: The alkali cancels some acid, so pH rises toward neutral.

**C4 (Sapling).**
- Prompt: Vinegar was 20 °C. Add baking soda: 16 °C. Which kind of change?
- Choices: Endothermic: energy went in ✔ · Exothermic: energy went out (`exo-means-cold`) · It made cold stuff (`endo-makes-cold-stuff`)
- Hint: The surroundings got colder. Did energy go in or out?
- Explanation: It's 4 °C colder. The change took energy from its surroundings.

**C5 (Sapling).**
- Prompt: Bleach is about pH 12. Is it safe because it's not acid?
- Choices: Yes, alkalis are gentle (`alkali-is-safe`) · No, they can burn too ✔ · Yes, high pH is safe (`ph-measures-danger`)
- Hint: What did teach card 3 say about oven cleaner?
- Explanation: Both ends of the scale can burn. pH tells acidity, not safety.

**C6 (Sapling, sort).** Sort into Physical or Chemical: ice melting · sugar dissolving · iron rusting · bread toasting · a copper roof turning green · bending a spoon.
- Answer: Physical: melting, dissolving, bending. Chemical: rusting, toasting, the copper roof.
- Misconceptions tested: `looks-changed-means-chemical` (melting or bending as chemical), `dissolve-is-chemical` (sugar as chemical).
- Hint: Open the atom window. Do atoms regroup into something new?
- Explanation: Physical changes keep the same particles. Chemical changes make new substances, like rust.

**C7 (Keeper's Desk, number).**
- Prompt: Each pH step is ten times. How much more acidic is pH 3 than 5?
- Answer: 100 times. Worked steps: 5 to 4 is ×10; 4 to 3 is ×10 again; 10 × 10 = 100.
- Misconception tested: `ph-steps-add` (answering 2).
- Hint: Count the steps, then multiply by ten for each.
- Explanation: Two steps of ten make a hundred. Small pH gaps can be big differences.

**C8 (Keeper's Desk, number).** Uses the balloon scale.
- Prompt: Open bottle: 250 g, then 247 g after fizzing. Where did 3 g go?
- Answer: 3 g (250 − 247) left as carbon dioxide gas. With the balloon on, the reading stays 250 g.
- Misconception tested: `mass-changes-in-reaction`.
- Hint: What did the balloon catch in the closed bottle?
- Explanation: Atoms only regroup. Count the gas too, and the mass is all still there.

## Hints for the "I'm stuck" ladder

Every item: hint, then a worked example of an easier case with how to start, then "Skip for now. You can come back any time."

- **Squeeze Box stuck example:** "Two dots far apart can move closer. Two touching dots can't. Now, which syringe has gaps?"
- **Pan Curve stuck example:** "A flat line means the temperature is not changing. Start by finding where the line stops climbing."
- **Formula stuck example:** "CO: one C, one O, two atoms. Now try H₂O: the 2 belongs to the H."
- **Gap stuck example:** "Between a soft metal and another soft metal, guess a soft metal. Now look at this gap's neighbors."
- **Color Jar stuck example:** "Water stays purple: neutral. Pink means acid. Now look at your glass."
- **Thermometer stuck example:** "A hand warmer goes from 20 °C to 50 °C: energy came out. Now, did yours go up or down?"

## Key terms

- **particle:** a piece of matter far too small to see.
- **atom:** the smallest piece of an element.
- **element:** a substance made of one kind of atom.
- **compound:** two or more kinds of atom joined together.
- **molecule:** a group of atoms joined together.
- **formula:** letters and numbers that count the atoms, like H₂O.
- **mixture:** things together but not joined. You can separate them.
- **periodic table:** the chart of all the elements, in order.
- **group:** a column in the table. Its elements behave alike.
- **period:** a row in the table.
- **metal / non-metal:** two big families of elements, told apart by a cluster of clues.
- **melting point / boiling point:** the temperature where a substance melts, or boils.
- **physical change:** a change where the particles stay the same.
- **chemical change:** a change that makes a new substance.
- **acid / alkali:** below 7 or above 7 on the pH scale.
- **neutral:** exactly 7 on the pH scale.
- **indicator:** something that changes color to show acid or alkali.
- **exothermic:** gives out energy. Things nearby get warmer.
- **endothermic:** takes in energy. Things nearby get colder.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** the older side of matter. The particle model as an explanation (squashing, density, floating ice); heating curves; atoms, elements, compounds and formulas; the periodic table as a predictive pattern, with Mendeleev's gaps; metals and non-metals as a property cluster; physical versus chemical change with mass conserved; the pH scale and indicators; changes that warm or cool their surroundings.

**What it does not claim:** it does not teach electron shells, ions, hydrogen-ion definitions of acids, enthalpy numbers or reaction equations. Particle pictures and atom tokens are models. All readings on the syringes, Pan Curve, thermometer and balloon scale are made-up model numbers. The town, the cabinet and the letters are fiction; the history on archive cards is real and sourced.

**Sources in brief:** the American Chemical Society (red cabbage indicator; baking soda and vinegar temperature), NobelPrize.org and the Science History Institute (Curie; Sørensen), the Linda Hall Library and published histories (Mendeleev, Nilson, Winkler), the University of Exeter (magpies), and chemistry texts. The full list is in the source audit.

**An off-screen activity (adult-led only, optional):**
> **Red cabbage color jars.** A grown-up does every step. Use the safety notes in the published activity you follow, such as the American Chemical Society's "Red Cabbage Indicator." This game adds no safety advice of its own; it only narrows what is used: a grown-up handles any hot water and cutting; use only kitchen liquids (lemon juice, vinegar, baking soda in water, plain water); nothing is tasted; no cleaning products at all; baking soda and vinegar only in an open cup, never a closed bottle.

**Talking points:** "How do we know atoms are there if we can't see them?" "Why was leaving a gap braver than squashing the row up?" Answers are spoken, never saved.
