# The Bee Meadow: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 150](../specs/150-bee-meadow.md). Use as written, after independent review and a subject check. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion and is never gendered. Pip is a fox (he/him), Dot a ladybug (she/her), the orchard keeper she/her, Hob Pennick he/him, Bramble a girl witch (she/her) with a magpie. Hollis is they/them. Nothing here is scored.

## Labels used on every card

- **A true thing about insects** (real science, sourced in the audit)
- **A true thing from the history of science** (real people and dates)
- **A story from Willowbank** (fiction: the meadow, the hive, the town's numbers, Hollis's pages)
- **A made-up model** (a rule invented to make a bench work; real life is messier)
- **A simplified picture** (real science with parts left out, and the card says which)

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. The flattened meadow (Act I adapter)

**Hook:** The storm knocked the whole meadow flat. Pip has found the mower.

**Short:**
> The storm's wind flattened the meadow. Pip wants to mow it tidy. The orchard keeper asks him to wait one week and watch.

**Longer:**
> After the storm, the meadow lies flat, every stem blown the same way, like hair after a bath.
>
> Pip drags the old mower out of the shed. "I'll tidy it up," he says. "Then it'll look like a garden again."
>
> The orchard keeper puts a hand on the handle. "Wait one week," she says. "Then decide."
>
> Every morning, Pip comes back to check. On the second day, a few stems are standing. On the fourth, there are yellow flowers, then blue ones. On the sixth, a bee lands on Pip's nose and leaves again, quite politely.
>
> "Where did they all come from?" he asks.
>
> "They were here all along," says {guide}. "They were waiting for the flowers."
>
> Pip puts the mower back. "Maybe just the path," he says.

**Keeper's note:**
> A true thing about insects: long grass and wildflowers give insects food, shelter and places to lay eggs. Leaving some grass uncut and avoiding sprays are simple things that help. Willowbank's meadow and its fast recovery are made up; a real meadow can take longer.

### Scene 2. The dance on the comb (Act II adapter, core scene)

**Hook:** A bee comes home and starts to dance. Dot wants to know what it means.

**Short:**
> In the glass hive, a bee runs in a straight line, wiggling, then loops back and runs again. "She's telling them where the flowers are," says the orchard keeper.

**Longer:**
> Inside the shed, the glass hive hums. Dot presses her hat brim to the glass.
>
> One bee is doing something odd. She runs a short straight line up the comb, waggling her body side to side. Then she loops round and runs the same line again. Other bees crowd behind her.
>
> "Is she dancing?" asks Dot.
>
> "In a way," says the orchard keeper. "She found good flowers. The way her run points tells the others which way to fly. How long she waggles tells them how far."
>
> Pip squints. "Which way is she pointing?"
>
> "A bit to the right of straight up," says {guide}. "So the flowers are a bit to the right of the Sun."
>
> Bramble's magpie taps the glass, and the orchard keeper shoos it off. One bee leaves the crowd and heads out of the door. Then another.

**Keeper's note:**
> A true thing from the history of science: Karl von Frisch worked out what the honeybee dance means. In 1973 he shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Konrad Lorenz and Nikolaas Tinbergen. Other scientists doubted him at first; later studies confirmed the dance carries direction and distance.

### Scene 3. The lantern question (Act V adapter, night garden)

**Hook:** The new garden lantern is lovely. It is also full of moths.

**Short:**
> At dusk, moths circle the new garden lantern. "Should it stay on all night?" asks Ember. The librarian finds a study about lights and caterpillars.

**Longer:**
> The night garden is nearly finished. Ember has hung a bright new lantern on the gate, and everyone agrees it looks wonderful.
>
> By the time the stars come out, a cloud of moths is circling it. Some bump the glass. Some settle on the post and stay there.
>
> "They like it!" says Ember.
>
> "Do they?" says {guide}. "Or are they stuck?"
>
> The librarian comes down the path with a folder. "Scientists in England counted moth caterpillars along roads," they say. "Some places had street lights. Some didn't."
>
> "And?" says Pip.
>
> "Let's read it together," says the librarian. "Then the night garden can decide."

**Keeper's note:**
> A true thing about insects: in a 2021 study in southern England, hedgerows under street lights had 47% fewer moth caterpillars than unlit hedgerows, and grass margins 33% fewer. White LED lights showed stronger effects than older yellow lamps. Many moths are pollinators at night.

## Teach cards

Each worked example reveals one step per tap.

**Teach 1: The insect body plan.** (A true thing about insects)
> An adult insect has three body parts. All six legs join the middle part, the thorax.
- Worked example (Dot's cousin, a beetle):
  1. Front part: the head, with two antennae and eyes.
  2. Middle part: the thorax. Count the legs: six. Wings join here too.
  3. Back part: the abdomen. No legs.
  4. Its hard outside is its skeleton. It has no bones inside.

**Teach 2: Two ways to grow.** (A true thing about insects)
> Some insects change completely, with a pupa. Others grow gradually, with no pupa.
- Worked example (ladybug and grasshopper, side by side):
  1. Ladybug: egg, then larva. The larva looks nothing like a ladybug.
  2. Ladybug larva makes a pupa. Inside, its body is rebuilt.
  3. Grasshopper: egg, then nymph. The nymph looks like a small grasshopper.
  4. The nymph sheds its skin and grows. No pupa. Then it is an adult.

**Teach 3: Jobs in the hive.** (A true thing about insects)
> In a honeybee hive, different bees do different jobs. The queen lays the eggs.
- Worked example (one worker bee's life):
  1. Young: she cleans empty cells.
  2. A little older: she feeds the young bees.
  3. Older: she builds wax, stores food or guards the door.
  4. Oldest: she flies out to find flowers.

**Teach 4: The dance.** (A true thing about insects, with a made-up model)
> A bee's waggle run tells two things. Its angle gives the direction. Its length gives the distance.
- Worked example (Sun in the southeast):
  1. The run points straight up. Straight up means "toward the Sun".
  2. So the flowers are toward the southeast.
  3. The run lasts 1 second. In our model hive, that means 1 kilometer.
  4. Fly toward the Sun for 1 kilometer. Flowers!

**Teach 5: Pollen rides along.** (A true thing about insects)
> Bees visit flowers for food. Pollen sticks to their hairs and rubs off on the next flower.
- Worked example (two apple blossoms):
  1. A bee drinks nectar from blossom one.
  2. Yellow pollen sticks to her fuzzy body.
  3. She flies to blossom two, on the next apple tree. Some pollen rubs off.
  4. Now blossom two can grow into an apple.

**Teach 6: Hide, warn or copy.** (A true thing about insects)
> Insects have tricks that stop birds eating them. Each trick works on what the bird sees.
- Worked example (a hungry robin's morning):
  1. A green caterpillar on a green leaf: the robin misses it. That's hiding.
  2. A wasp with bold stripes: the robin remembers a sting. That's warning.
  3. A hoverfly with wasp stripes: the robin stays away. But hoverflies can't sting. That's copying.

**Teach 7: Keeping the meadow.** (A true thing about insects)
> Many insects are getting fewer. Each cause works in its own way, and some things help.
- Worked example (lights at night):
  1. Bright lights pull moths in at night.
  2. Moths circling a lamp aren't feeding or laying eggs.
  3. A study found fewer caterpillars under street lights.
  4. So: point lights down, or switch them off.

## Station activities: child-facing lines

### Body Bench

**Intro:** Build an insect. Then sort the meadow cards.

**Build prompt:** Put the parts together. Where do the six legs go?

**Build feedback:**
- Legs all on the thorax: "Yes. All six legs join the thorax, the middle part."
- Some legs on the head or abdomen: "Close. Real insect legs all join the middle part. Try moving them."
- Wings on, or off: "Wings are fine either way. Some adult insects have none."

**Guided item (visible steps):** Is this ant an insect?
1. Count the body parts. (Three.)
2. Find where the legs join. (All on the middle part.)
3. Count the legs. (Six.)
4. So the ant is an adult insect.

**Sort prompt:** Sort each card. Adult insect, young insect, or not an insect?

**Sort bins:** Adult insect · Young insect · Not an insect · I can't tell from this picture

**Sort feedback (one line per card):**
- Ant, ladybug, hoverfly, grasshopper: "Three parts and six legs on the thorax. An adult insect."
- Caterpillar: "A young insect. Its back 'legs' are soft prolegs. It will grow into a moth or butterfly."
- Garden spider: "Eight legs and two body parts. A spider is not an insect."
- Woodlouse: "Fourteen walking legs. You met it in the body key. Not an insect."
- Harvestman: "Eight legs and one oval body. It's related to spiders, but it isn't one. Not an insect."
- I can't tell: "That's an honest answer. Which part would you need to see?"

**Link:** Want the leg-count key again? Visit Body clues.

### Two Ways Board

**Intro:** Two insects, two ways of growing up. Fill in both tracks.

**Track prompt:** Put the ladybug's stages in order.

**Guided item (visible steps):** Sort the dragonfly.
1. Is there a pupa? (No.)
2. Does the young look like the adult? (Yes, roughly: six legs, big eyes, no wings yet.)
3. No pupa, young looks like the adult: grows gradually.

**Sort questions on each card:** Is there a pupa? · Does the young look like the adult?

**Sort feedback:**
- Dragonfly: "No pupa. Its nymph lives underwater, then climbs out and sheds one last skin. Grows gradually."
- Hoverfly: "Its larva is a legless maggot that eats aphids. It makes a pupa. Changes completely."
- Honeybee: "Egg, larva, pupa, adult, all inside a wax cell. Changes completely."
- Cricket: "Its young looks like a small cricket. No pupa. Grows gradually."

**Link:** The small white butterfly changes completely too. Visit Life stages.

**Honeybee strip (A simplified picture):** Worker bee: egg about 3 days. Egg to adult about 21 days.

### Hive Window

**Intro:** Tap a bee to see her job.

**Job cards:**
- Queen: "I lay the eggs, often 1,500 or more a day in early summer. My scent helps the hive. I don't give orders."
- Cleaner: "I'm young. I clean empty cells so they can be used again."
- Nurse: "I feed the young bees in their cells."
- Builder: "I make wax and build the comb."
- Guard: "I stand at the door and check who comes in."
- Forager: "I fly out for nectar and pollen. I'm one of the oldest workers."
- Drone: "I'm a male. I don't collect food. My job is to mate with a queen."

**Note under the window:** A simplified picture. Real workers often change jobs when the hive needs it.

**What if? card:** A week of rain keeps the foragers home. What happens to the food?
- A: "The hive eats its stored honey." **Right.** Feedback: "Yes. Stored honey gets the hive through days with no foraging."
- B: "The queen goes out to find flowers." (queen-does-everything) Feedback: "The queen stays in. Foragers find food."
- C: "The drones fetch food instead." (drones-work) Feedback: "Drones don't collect food. The hive uses its stores."

**Solitary bee card:** Most bees don't live in hives. A solitary bee mother builds her own nest and finds all the food herself.

### Dance Floor

**Model label, always on screen:** A made-up model. Straight up on the comb means "toward the Sun". Right of up means right of the Sun. Here, 1 second of waggling means 1 kilometer. Real bees are less tidy.

**Read prompt:** Watch the dance. Tap the patch she found.

**Make prompt:** Set the run so a bee finds the clover.

**Read feedback:**
- Right patch: "Yes. Right direction and right distance."
- Right direction, wrong distance: "Good direction. Now check how long the run lasts."
- Wrong direction: "Look at the run's angle from straight up. Find the Sun first."

**Patch names (fiction):** Orchard blossom · Hob Pennick's clover · Lavender by the mill · The far meadow

### Pollen Path

**Intro:** Steer the bee from flower to flower. Watch the yellow dots.

**Guided item (visible steps):** Help blossom two make an apple.
1. Tap blossom one. The bee drinks nectar.
2. Look at her legs and body. Pollen!
3. Tap blossom two, on the next tree. Some pollen rubs off.
4. In autumn, blossom two has an apple.

**Feedback:**
- Bee visits only one blossom: "She fed well. But no pollen reached another blossom."
- Bee carries pollen to a clover head: "Apple pollen can't help a clover. It needs another apple blossom."
- Hoverfly run: "The hoverfly carried pollen too. Bees aren't the only pollinators."

**Picture label:** A simplified picture. One visit is shown as enough, and summer is skipped.

**Link line:** Why must apple pollen come from a different tree? Visit the Orchard Year.

### Hedge of Tricks

**Intro:** You're a hungry robin. Tap every insect you can spot.

**After spotting:** You found {n} of 6. Some were hard to see. That was their trick.

**Sort prompt:** Hide, warn or copy? Sort each insect.

**Close look prompt:** Count the wings on each insect.

**Close look feedback:** "The wasp has two pairs of wings. The hoverfly has one pair. Hoverflies are flies."

**Sort feedback:**
- Green caterpillar, moth on bark, meadow grasshopper in grass: "Hide. It looks like what it sits on."
- Wasp: "Warn. Its stripes go with a real sting."
- Ladybug: "Warn. Birds learn that ladybugs taste bad."
- Hoverfly: "Copy. It looks like a wasp but can't sting."

### Keeping the Meadow

**Intro:** Choose three changes for Willowbank's meadow and lantern.

**Action cards (each says why):**
- Leave a corner long: "Long grass gives insects food and shelter."
- Plant flowers for every season: "Bees need food from spring to autumn."
- Don't spray: "Many sprays harm insects they weren't aimed at."
- Leave a log pile: "Beetle grubs and many others live in old wood."
- Point the lantern down, or switch it off: "Fewer moths get pulled away from food."
- Count insects with a grown-up: "Counts help scientists see if numbers change."

**After choosing:** Those three would help. Every meadow can use a few.

## Problem sets

Each item: prompt, choices or answer, misconception tags, hint, explanation. Feedback for each wrong choice is in quotes.

### Set A: Bodies and growing up

**A1 (Sprout).** Where do an insect's six legs join its body?
- A: The head. (legs-anywhere) "The head has antennae. Look at the middle part."
- **B: The thorax.** "Yes. All six join the thorax."
- C: The abdomen. (legs-on-abdomen) "The back part has no legs. Look at the middle."
- Hint: It's the middle part.
- Explanation: An adult insect's six legs all join the thorax, the middle of its three body parts.

**A2 (Sprout).** How many body parts does an adult insect have?
- **A: Three.** "Yes. Head, thorax and abdomen."
- B: Two. (spider-plan) "Two parts is a spider. Count again on the beetle."
- C: Six. (legs-as-parts) "Six is its legs. Count the body parts."
- Hint: Head, middle, back.
- Explanation: Every adult insect has a head, a thorax and an abdomen.

**A3 (Seedling).** A spider has eight legs and two body parts. Is it an insect?
- A: Yes, it's small and it crawls. (any-crawly-is-insect) "Lots of small things crawl. Count its legs."
- B: Yes, it lives in the meadow. (habitat-is-identity) "Living in a meadow doesn't make an insect."
- **C: No, it isn't an insect.** "Right. Eight legs and two parts: a spider."
- Hint: Check the insect rule. Six legs? Three parts?
- Explanation: Spiders are arachnids. They have eight legs and two body parts.

**A4 (Seedling, number).** Four ladybugs and two spiders sit on a leaf. How many legs?
- Answer: 40.
- Worked steps: 4 ladybugs × 6 legs = 24. 2 spiders × 8 legs = 16. 24 + 16 = 40.
- Hint: Ladybugs first, then spiders.
- Explanation: Insects have six legs; spiders have eight. Add the two groups.

**A5 (Seedling).** This caterpillar has more than six "legs". Is it an insect?
- A: No, it has too many legs. (counts-prolegs) "Its back 'legs' are soft prolegs. It has six real legs."
- **B: Yes, it's a young insect.** "Yes. It will grow into a moth or butterfly."
- C: No, it has no wings. (wings-define-insects) "Wings come later. Young insects don't have them."
- Hint: What will it grow into?
- Explanation: The six-leg rule is for adults. A caterpillar is a young insect, a larva. Its six true legs are near its head.

**A6 (Sapling, order).** Put the ladybug's life in order.
- Cards: adult · egg · pupa · larva
- Answer: egg, larva, pupa, adult.
- Misconception watched: larva and pupa swapped (pupa-before-larva). Feedback: "The larva eats and grows first. Then it makes a pupa."
- Hint: Which one eats aphids and grows?
- Explanation: Ladybugs change completely: egg, larva, pupa, adult.

**A7 (Sapling).** A young cricket looks like a small adult. No pupa. How does it grow?
- A: It changes completely. (all-insects-pupate) "Changing completely needs a pupa. Crickets have none."
- B: It doesn't change at all. (no-change) "It sheds its skin several times as it grows."
- **C: It grows gradually.** "Yes. Egg, nymph, adult, with no pupa."
- Hint: Ask the two questions. Pupa? Looks like the adult?
- Explanation: Crickets grow gradually. The nymph molts as it grows and gets wings at the end.

**A8 (Sapling, number).** A worker grows for 21 days. The egg part is 3. How many are left?
- Answer: 18 days.
- Worked steps: Whole time 21 days. Take away the egg's 3 days. 21 − 3 = 18.
- Hint: Take away the egg days.
- Explanation: The other 18 days are spent as a larva and then a pupa.

**A9 (Keeper's Desk, number and say why).** Queens grow about 16 days, drones about 24. How many more for drones?
- Answer: 8 days.
- Worked steps: 24 − 16 = 8.
- Hint: Bigger number take away smaller.
- Explanation: Queens, workers and drones grow at different speeds: about 16, 21 and 24 days.
- **Say why (to a grown-up):** Why is a pupa doing a lot, even though it seems still?

### Set B: The hive and the dance

**B1 (Sprout).** Who lays the eggs in a honeybee hive?
- A: The workers. (workers-lay) "Workers feed and build. Look for the bee with the long body."
- B: The drones. (drones-work) "Drones are males. They don't lay eggs."
- **C: The queen.** "Yes. Laying eggs is the queen's job."
- Hint: There is only one of her.
- Explanation: The queen lays the eggs. Workers do almost everything else.

**B2 (Sprout).** Who does most of the work in a hive?
- **A: The workers.** "Yes. They clean, feed, build, guard and forage."
- B: The queen. (queen-is-boss) "The queen lays eggs. She doesn't do the other jobs."
- C: The drones. (drones-work) "Drones don't collect food or build."
- Hint: Their name is a clue.
- Explanation: Worker bees are female, and they do the hive's jobs.

**B3 (Seedling, true or not true from the job cards).** "The queen tells the other bees what to do."
- Answer: Not true.
- Misconception: queen-is-boss. Feedback if "true": "Read the queen's card again. She lays eggs. She doesn't give orders."
- Hint: Read the queen's job card.
- Explanation: No bee is in charge. The queen's scent matters, but the workers sort out the jobs.

**B4 (Seedling, order).** Put one worker's jobs in order, youngest first.
- Cards: fly out to find flowers · clean empty cells · guard the door · feed young bees
- Answer: clean empty cells, feed young bees, guard the door, fly out to find flowers.
- Misconception watched: young bees forage (young-forage). Feedback: "The oldest workers fly out. Young ones work inside."
- Hint: Which job is outside, far from home?
- Explanation: A worker's jobs roughly follow her age. Real bees switch when the hive needs it.

**B5 (Sapling).** The waggle run points straight up. Where are the flowers?
- **A: Toward the Sun.** "Yes. Straight up means toward the Sun."
- B: Away from the Sun. (up-means-away) "Straight down would mean away from the Sun."
- C: Straight above the hive. (up-means-sky) "Bees don't mean the sky. Up stands for the Sun's direction."
- Hint: Check the model card.
- Explanation: On the comb, straight up stands for the Sun's direction.

**B6 (Sapling, number, made-up model).** In our model hive, the run lasts 3 seconds. How far?
- Answer: 3 kilometers. Part two: a half-second run? 500 meters.
- Worked steps: 1 second means 1 km. 3 seconds means 3 × 1 = 3 km. Half a second means half a kilometer: 1,000 ÷ 2 = 500 m.
- Hint: Each second is one kilometer here.
- Explanation: Longer runs mean farther flowers. The 1-second rule is made up for this hive.

**B7 (Sapling).** One bee's run is longer than another's. What does that tell you?
- A: Her flowers have more nectar. (length-means-amount) "Run length shows distance. It doesn't measure the nectar."
- **B: Her flowers are farther away.** "Yes. A longer run means farther away."
- C: She is a better dancer. (length-means-skill) "Every bee's run shows distance. It isn't a contest."
- Hint: What does run length stand for?
- Explanation: The angle gives direction and the length gives distance.

**B8 (Sapling, number).** Suppose the queen lays 1,500 eggs each day for 10 days. How many eggs?
- Answer: 15,000.
- Worked steps: 1,500 × 10 = 15,000.
- Hint: Times ten adds a zero.
- Explanation: A busy queen can lay about 1,500 to 2,000 a day in early summer. A steady rate is a made-up simplification.

**B9 (Keeper's Desk).** The Sun moves 30° right. The flowers stay. What does a 90°-right dance become?
- A: Still 90° right. (dance-fixed) "The run follows the Sun. If the Sun moves, the angle changes."
- B: 120° right. (add-the-move) "The Sun moved toward the flowers. The gap got smaller."
- **C: 60° right.** "Yes. The Sun moved 30° toward the flowers, so 90 − 30 = 60."
- Hint: Draw the Sun and the flowers. Did the gap grow or shrink?
- Explanation: The angle is between the Sun and the flowers. Morning: Sun due east, flowers due south, 90°. Later: Sun at 120°, gap 60°.

**B10 (Keeper's Desk, true or not true, then say why).** Bramble says, "One second of waggling always means one kilometer."
- Answer: Not true.
- Misconception: model-is-real. Feedback if "true": "That's our made-up rule. Real bees differ, bee to bee and hive to hive."
- Hint: Read the model label.
- Explanation: Longer runs always mean farther, but scientists find each bee's timing differs.
- **Say why (to a grown-up):** How could scientists find out what one bee's seconds mean?

### Set C: Flowers, tricks and keeping the meadow

**C1 (Sprout).** Why does a bee visit a flower?
- **A: To find food.** "Yes. Nectar and pollen are bee food."
- B: To help the flower on purpose. (pollinates-on-purpose) "The bee is feeding. Helping happens by accident."
- C: To sleep inside it. (flower-as-home) "Bees sleep in their nest. They visit flowers to feed."
- Hint: What does the bee drink?
- Explanation: Bees feed on nectar and pollen. Pollen they carry helps the plant as a side effect.

**C2 (Sprout).** A green caterpillar sits on a green leaf. What is its trick?
- **A: Hide.** "Yes. It looks like the leaf, so birds miss it."
- B: Warn. (green-is-warning) "Warning colors are bold, like stripes. This one blends in."
- C: Copy. (copy-vs-hide) "Copying means looking like a dangerous animal. A leaf isn't dangerous."
- Hint: Can you see it easily?
- Explanation: Camouflage helps an insect hide against its background.

**C3 (Seedling).** Where must pollen go so an apple can grow?
- A: Into the bee's stomach as food. (pollen-only-food) "Bees eat some pollen. The apple needs pollen on its blossom."
- B: Down into the soil. (pollen-is-seed) "Pollen isn't a seed. It has to reach a blossom."
- **C: To another apple blossom.** "Yes. Apple pollen has to reach an apple blossom."
- Hint: Look back at the Pollen Path.
- Explanation: Pollen has to reach a flower of the same kind of plant: apple to apple.

**C4 (Seedling, true or not true).** "Only bees carry pollen."
- Answer: Not true.
- Misconception: only-bees-pollinate. Feedback if "true": "Remember the hoverfly run. Butterflies, moths and beetles carry pollen too."
- Hint: Who else visited the blossoms?
- Explanation: Many insects pollinate. Bees are among the busiest.

**C5 (Seedling).** A wasp has bold yellow and black stripes. What do they tell a bird?
- A: I'm hiding here. (stripes-are-camouflage) "Bold stripes are easy to see. They aren't for hiding."
- **B: I can sting you.** "Yes. Birds learn the pattern and stay away."
- C: I'm good to eat. (bright-means-tasty) "The stripes say the opposite. Stay away."
- Hint: What can a wasp do?
- Explanation: Warning colors go with a real defense. Birds learn to avoid them.

**C6 (Sapling).** How can you tell a hoverfly from a wasp?
- A: Count the legs. (legs-differ) "Both are insects. Both have six legs."
- B: Look at the stripes. (stripes-differ) "The hoverfly copied the stripes. Look somewhere else."
- **C: Count the wings.** "Yes. A hoverfly has one pair. A wasp has two."
- Hint: Use the close look.
- Explanation: Hoverflies are flies, with one pair of wings. Wasps have two pairs.

**C7 (Sapling, number, then say why).** Scientists checked 115 leading food crops. 87 depend on animals for pollination. How many don't?
- Answer: 28.
- Worked steps: 115 − 87 = 28.
- Hint: Take the 87 away from all 115.
- Explanation: Most leading crops need animal pollinators for some or all of their harvest.
- **Say why (to a grown-up, Keeper's Desk follow-up):** By weight of food grown, pollinator crops are only about 35%. How can both be true?

**C8 (Sapling, number).** The weight fell by 76%. From 100 grams, how much is left?
- Card first: A study in Germany weighed the flying insects caught in traps over 27 years.
- Answer: 24 grams.
- Worked steps: 76% of 100 grams is 76 grams. 100 − 76 = 24.
- Hint: Percent means "out of 100".
- Explanation: The traps caught about a quarter of the weight they did 27 years before.

- A: Flying insects got lighter in those traps. (underclaim-mistaken-for-overreach) "The study measured that. It's well supported."

## Key terms

- **Insect:** a small animal with six legs, three body parts and a hard outside, when grown.
- **Head, thorax, abdomen:** an insect's three body parts: front, middle, back.
- **Antennae:** two feelers on the head. They are not legs.
- **Exoskeleton:** a hard skeleton on the outside of the body.
- **Larva:** a young insect that looks very different from the adult. A caterpillar, grub or maggot.
- **Pupa:** the stage where a larva's body is rebuilt into an adult.
- **Nymph:** a young insect that already looks a bit like the adult.
- **Molt:** to shed an old outer skin so the body can grow.
- **Colony:** a big family of insects that live and work together.
- **Queen, worker, drone:** the egg-layer; the female bees who do the jobs; the males.
- **Waggle dance:** a run on the comb that shows other bees the way to flowers.
- **Nectar:** sweet liquid in flowers. Bee food.
- **Pollen:** fine powder from flowers. Plants need it moved to make seeds.
- **Pollination:** moving pollen from flower to flower.
- **Camouflage:** looking like your background, so you're hard to see.
- **Warning colors:** bold colors that say "I sting" or "I taste bad".
- **Mimicry:** a harmless animal looking like a dangerous one.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches.** The adult insect body plan and what is not an insect; complete and gradual metamorphosis; roles in a honeybee colony; the waggle dance as a signal for direction and distance; pollination as a side effect of feeding; camouflage, warning colors and mimicry from a predator's point of view; evidence about insect decline and actions that help. It builds on the Living Garden's body clues, life stages and food links without repeating them, and leaves the plant's side of pollination (flower to fruit, apple varieties) to the Orchard Year.

**What it does not claim.** The dance rule "1 second means 1 kilometer" is a made-up model; real bees' timing varies from bee to bee. The worker job order is a tendency, not a timetable. The meadow, hive and all Willowbank numbers are fiction. The German insect study measured the weight of flying insects in protected areas of one country; it is not a world count. No item is scored and nothing is a test.

**Sources in brief.** Nobel Prize press release (1973); university beekeeping extension pages; Klein and others (2007); Hallmann and others (2017); Boyes and others (2021); Australian Museum. Full list in the source audit.

**Off-screen (optional, grown-up-led).** A slow walk along a meadow edge, park verge or garden on a warm, calm day, looking only. Stay on paths. Do not touch, catch or collect any insect; never approach a hive or nest. Anyone with a sting allergy should keep well away from bees and wasps and follow their own medical advice. Watch a bee on a flower for one minute and talk about what it is doing. Nothing from the walk is recorded in the game. Grown-ups who want to help real science can look up a citizen-science survey such as Spipoll in France (photographing the insects on one flower for 20 minutes); that happens outside the game.
