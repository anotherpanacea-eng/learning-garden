## Page 1. Built to last

**Teach card 1:** A pyramid was a tomb for a king. Egyptians built huge ones of stone. The biggest is at Giza.

**Teach card 2:** The Great Pyramid was built for King Khufu, about 4,500 years ago. Giza's pyramids are the only Ancient Wonder still standing.

**Teach card 3:** Beside it lies the Great Sphinx: a lion's body with a human head. It was carved from the rock itself.

**Worked example: tomb shapes** (one step per tap):
1. A **mastaba**: a flat-topped tomb. The name means "bench".
2. A **step pyramid**: flat layers stacked, each smaller than the one below.
3. The **Bent Pyramid**: builders changed the slope partway up.
4. A **smooth pyramid**: straight sloping sides to a point. The Great Pyramid is one.
5. Much later, kings were buried in tombs **hidden in a valley's rock**.

**Size strip:** When new, the Great Pyramid stood about 146 m tall: about 15 of our mills stacked up. Today it's about 138 m. Most outer stones were taken away.

**Facts card:** Base sides about 230 m. More than two million blocks, by most estimates. The Sphinx: about 73 m long, 20 m high, face probably King Khafre's.

## Page 2. Who built them, and how

**Teach card 1:** Near the pyramids, archaeologists found a workers' town. It had bakeries, storerooms and houses. Workers' tombs were close by.

**Worked example: what each find suggests** (one step per tap):
1. **Bakeries and storerooms.** Lots of bread was made here. Workers were fed.
2. **Houses.** People lived right by the work.
3. **Workers' tombs, with job titles.** Workers were buried near the king's pyramid, with care.
4. **So:** the evidence suggests organized crews, fed and housed by the king.

**Teach card 2:** Archaeologists think many workers were called up for a few months, in turns. A smaller crew of skilled builders worked all year.

**Teach card 3:** A real logbook survives on papyrus. Merer, in charge of about 40 boatmen, wrote down trips carrying stone to Giza.

**Teach card 4:** Big stones rode on wooden sledges, pulled with ropes. A later painting shows someone pouring water on the sand in front.

**Sledge bench, guided first pull** (one step per tap):
1. "Same block, same sledge. Only the sand changes. Pull on dry sand."
2. "The model needs 20 haulers. See the sand heap up in front?"
3. "Now damp sand. Pull again. 10 haulers. The sand stays flat and firm."
4. "Now soaked sand. 16 haulers. Too much water made it soft again."

**Teach card 5:** To raise blocks, builders probably used ramps. Nobody knows their exact shape. Archaeologists still argue, and that's honest.

**Hatnub clue card:** At an old stone quarry, archaeologists found a steep ramp from Khufu's time, with posts to help haul. It's a clue, from a quarry.

## Page 3. Stories Egyptians told

**Teach card 1:** Egyptians believed in many gods and goddesses. They told stories about them and built temples as their homes.

**Worked example: reading a god** (one step per tap):
1. **Look.** A man with a bird's head. The bird has a long curved beak: an ibis.
2. **Role.** Egyptians believed this god looked after writing and records.
3. **Name.** We say **Thoth**. In Egyptian, something like **Djehuty**.

**Teach card 2:** Many names we use are Greek. Egyptian writing left out vowels, so we can only guess how names sounded.

**Ma'at card:** Ma'at was a goddess, and also an idea: truth and right order. Her sign was a feather.

**Teach card 3:** Egyptians believed the king stood between the gods and the people. Officials, led by the vizier, did the daily ruling.

**Boat contrast card:** A real boat of Lebanese cedar, about 43 m long, was buried beside Khufu's pyramid. Ra's sky boat is a story.

## Page 4. A journey after death

**Teach card 1:** Egyptians believed life could go on after death. So they filled tombs with food, furniture and favorite things.

**Worked example: why bread in a tomb?** (one step per tap):
1. **The belief.** Life went on after death.
2. **The need.** In that life, a person would still need food.
3. **The object.** So bread and drink went into the tomb.

**Tomb packing card:** Tap a thing. · *Bread:* food for the life they believed in. · *A chair:* comfort, as at home. · *Sandals:* for walking on. · *A game board:* for fun, forever. · *A closed coffin:* to keep the body safe.

**Teach card 2:** Stone tombs cost a great deal. Only a few people could have one. Most people had much simpler graves.

**Teach card 3 (Seedling and up):** Embalmers dried the body with a natural salt, natron. Then they wrapped it in linen and laid it in a coffin.

**Teach card 4 (Seedling and up):** Four organs were dried and kept in four canopic jars. The heart stayed in the body.

**Teach card 5 (Sapling and up):** Egyptians believed the heart was weighed against Ma'at's feather. A heart that balanced could go on.

**Weighing scene taps (Sapling and up):** *Anubis:* in the story, he works the scale. · *The feather:* truth and right order. · *Thoth:* in the story, he writes down the result. · *Osiris:* in the story, he welcomes those who pass. · *The papyrus:* this scene is painted on a real papyrus made for a man named Hunefer, about 1285 BCE. It's in the British Museum.

**Keeper's note (Keeper's depth):** The whole process took about 70 days, about 40 of them drying in natron. The brain was removed and not kept. The heart was left in place, because Egyptians believed it held thought and feeling. Small charms called amulets went between the wrappings. After about 1000 BCE, organs were often wrapped and put back in the body. Animals, such as cats, were mummified too.

**Ammit line (Keeper's depth only):** In the story, a creature named Ammit waited by the scale: part crocodile, part lion, part hippo. People believed she swallowed a heart that failed. On Hunefer's papyrus, his heart passes.

## Page 5. Who did what

**Teach card 1:** Books often draw Egypt's people as a pyramid. The king is at the top. Farmers, the most people, are at the base.

**Worked example: placing a farmer** (one step per tap):
1. Most Egyptians farmed.
2. The widest layer holds the most people.
3. So the farmer goes at the base.

**Limit cards: what the drawing leaves out** (flip each):
1. **It's a modern drawing.** Egyptians didn't draw their society this way.
2. **Some people rose.** A clever scribe or craftworker could move up. It was rare.
3. **Women had rights.** Records show women owning property, inheriting and going to court.
4. **Workers spoke up.** About 1157 BCE, tomb-builders stopped work because their grain was late. A scribe wrote it down.

**Reading card:** Very few Egyptians could read and write. Scribes could, which made them important.

## Page 6. Trade and plenty

**Teach card 1:** Good harvests made extra grain. Extra grain fed people who didn't farm: builders, priests, scribes and craftworkers.

**Teach card 2:** For a long time Egypt had no coins. People swapped goods of equal value, counted in deben, a weight of copper.

**Worked example: a fair swap** (one step per tap):
1. A basket is worth 6 deben. A sack of grain is worth 2.
2. One sack is 2. Two sacks are 4. Three sacks are 6.
3. So 3 sacks of grain for 1 basket. Both sides are worth 6.

**Teach card 3:** Egypt had few tall trees. Ships brought cedar logs from the coast of Lebanon, for beams and boats.

**Teach card 4:** Hatshepsut, a woman who ruled as king, sent ships to Punt. They brought back incense, and living incense trees.

**Teach card 5:** Where was Punt? Probably near the southern Red Sea. Scholars still argue about the exact place.

**Keeper's note:** Egypt sent out grain, papyrus and gold, and brought in cedar, incense and blue lapis lazuli from as far away as Afghanistan. One New Kingdom deben of copper weighed about 91 g. A plain linen garment could be worth about 5 deben.

## Page 7. Looking with care (Keeper's Desk, open to all)

**Teach card 1:** A mummy was a person. Museums choose how to show the dead. Some show only wrapped coffins.

**Teach card 2:** Some museums take mummies off display. Some write "mummified person" on labels, so visitors remember. People disagree.

**Label choice card:** A closed painted coffin in a case. Label A: *Mummy, Egypt.* Label B: *Coffin of a mummified person, Egypt. Please be respectful.* What does each label tell a visitor? (Nothing is checked.)

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches.** Pyramids and tomb shapes; who built them, by current evidence; what is known and argued about moving stone; gods as beliefs; afterlife belief, a brief account of mummification and the weighing of the heart; the "social pyramid" and its limits; surplus and trade. At the deepest level: how museums show the dead.

**What it does not claim.** Gods and the afterlife are presented as beliefs, never as fact. Mummification is brief; fuller detail is only at the Keeper's depth, and no image of a body appears. "Fed and organized" does not mean the work was freely chosen: most workers were probably called up, owing labor to the state. How blocks were raised is still argued. The sledge bench, mill height and prices are made up and labeled. The river, calendar, scribes and Rosetta Stone are in the Nile drawer. Willowbank, Vadoalto's museum and its loan box are fiction.

**Sources in brief.** Harvard Digital Giza, AERA, Britannica, PBS NOVA, the University of Amsterdam, the University of Liverpool, the British Museum, the Australian Museum, the Museo Egizio and UCL Digital Egypt. Full list in the source audit.

Use clean play sand; wash hands after; an adult handles any water near electronics. Talk about which pull felt easier. It is a model of one idea, friction, and says nothing about real pyramid loads.
