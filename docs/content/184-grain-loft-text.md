## Story scenes

### Scene 1. What rolls away (Act I, chapter 2: deliver and explain)

**Hook:** The storm tipped the loft over. The miller needs it tidy before the seed sacks go out.

**Short:**
> The loft is a jumble. "Anything that rolls goes away from the hatch," says the miller. Pip puts an apple on a crate. It rolls off.

**Longer:**
> The storm blew the loft hatch open. Now everything is in a heap: sacks, crates, tins, a ball, a wedge of cheese, a pointed grain scoop.
>
> "Before the seed sacks go out," says the miller, "anything that rolls goes far from the hatch. I've lost two tins down there already."
>
> Pip sets an apple on top of a crate. It rolls off and bounces down the ladder.
>
> "Apples roll," says Pip, to nobody.
>
> {guide} turns a tin on its side. It rolls. Then {guide} stands it on its end. It stays.
>
> "So a tin is both," says Pip. "A roller and a stander."
>
> "Look at what touches the floor," says {guide}. "Curved rolls. Flat stays."
>
> The cat opens one eye, decides none of this matters, and closes it again.

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> A curved surface can roll. A flat face can slide or stand still, and flat faces let shapes stack. A tin has both, so it does both.

### Scene 2. The cat's flour prints (Act I, chapter 4: mend the village)

**Hook:** The loft shelves are mended. The cat knocks the shape blocks into spilled flour and walks across the floor.

**Short:**
> The cat walks through flour, pushing blocks. The floor fills with prints: circles, squares, triangles. "Every print is flat," says the miller.

**Longer:**
> The new loft shelves are up. The miller sets her wooden shape blocks on the top one, to keep them safe. The cat watches this, very interested.
>
> By morning, the blocks are on the floor, and so is a sack of flour. Every block has left a print where it landed.
>
> "That round print is a cylinder," says Bramble.
>
> "Is it?" asks {guide}. "Can you pick up the print?"
>
> Bramble tries. Her fingers go white. "No. It's flat. It's a circle." She picks up the tin. "The tin is the cylinder. Its end is the circle."
>
> Pip finds four triangles and a square in a ring. "Something with a point did this."
>
> The miller holds up the little pyramid. "Four triangles and one square. All flat. All from one solid."

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> Each flat face of a solid is a flat, 2-D shape. A solid can have several kinds. Curved surfaces make no clean print; they roll a smudge.

### Scene 3. The hidden blocks (Act II, chapter 2: a garden across the seasons)

**Hook:** The harvest comes in. Seed goes into small wooden seed blocks, packed into crates.

**Short:**
> Pip counts the seed blocks he can see in the crate. "Nineteen!" The miller lifts the top layer. There are more underneath.

**Longer:**
> The beans are in, and the peas, and the first apples. The miller keeps next year's seed in small wooden blocks, each the same size, packed tight into crates.
>
> "How many blocks in this one?" she asks.
>
> Pip walks around the crate and counts every block he can see: the top, the front, the side. "Nineteen!"
>
> The miller lifts out the top layer: nine blocks, three rows of three. Underneath is another layer of nine, and under that, another.
>
> "Nine, nine and nine," says Dot, from the doorway. "That's twenty-seven."
>
> "Some were hiding," says Pip. "One was right in the middle. It never saw daylight."
>
> "Count one layer," says the miller. "Then count the layers."

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> Volume is how many unit cubes fill a solid, including the ones you can't see. One layer of 9, three layers: 27 cubic units.

### Scene 4. Packing for Vadoalto (Act III, chapter 2: the seed exchange)

**Hook:** Apple seedlings must go up the river to Vadoalto. The boat's hold is small, and everything must fit.

**Short:**
> Nuria's boat has a box-shaped hold. "Crates, not sacks," she says. "Sacks squash." How many crates fit?

**Longer** (Spanish line for a fluent reader's check):
> Nuria ties her boat at the mill landing. The hold is a wooden box: 6 feet long, 4 feet wide, 2 feet deep.
>
> *"Cajas, no sacos,"* she says. (Crates, not sacks.) "Sacks squash the seedlings."
>
> The miller's seedling crates are 2 feet on every edge.
>
> "Forty-eight cubic feet in the hold," says Bramble. "Two feet a crate. Twenty-four crates!"
>
> "Two feet is an edge," says {guide}. "How much space does one whole crate take?"
>
> Pip builds one crate out of seed blocks, one foot each. Two by two by two. "Eight blocks."
>
> "So divide by eight," says Bramble slowly. "Six crates." She frowns. "Fine. Six."
>
> Six crates go down into the hold, three along, two across, one deep. They fit exactly.

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> Hold: 6 × 4 × 2 = 48 cubic feet. One crate: 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 cubic feet. 48 ÷ 8 = 6. The edges also fit: 3 along, 2 across, 1 deep.

### Scene 5. Chalk on the beam (Keeper's Desk, Act III, chapter 2)

**Hook:** Wren Halloway finds an old table chalked on a loft beam: three columns of numbers and no heading.

**Short:**
> Old chalk on the beam: rows of three numbers. Wren copies them out. "Someone was counting something," she says. "Every row hides a pattern."

**Longer:**
> Wren Halloway climbs to the loft with her ledger. On the big beam, under years of dust, someone chalked a table long ago. Three numbers to a row. No heading.
>
> 8, 12, 6. 6, 9, 5. 5, 8, 5.
>
> "Corners, edges, faces," says the miller. "Of my shape blocks. Look: the cube, the wedge, the little pyramid."
>
> "Who chalked it?" asks Pip.
>
> "Someone who looked after this valley before us," says Wren. "The old Keeper, maybe. They counted everything."
>
> She runs her finger along a row. "Corners take away edges, add faces. Always two." She looks up. "Every row. Try one."

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> For a solid with only flat faces, vertices − edges + faces = 2. Euler wrote this down in 1750. You can test it on any such solid.

## Teach cards

Each card is under 30 words. Worked steps appear one tap at a time.

**T1. Solids have names** (A true thing about math)
> A solid takes up space. A ball is a sphere. A box is a cuboid. A tin is a cylinder. A scoop is a cone.
Worked example: 1. Every face of the seed block is a square. 2. A cuboid with all square faces is a cube. 3. The seed block is a cube.

**T2. Roll, slide or stack** (A true thing about math)
> A curved surface can roll. A flat face can slide or sit still. Flat tops and bottoms let solids stack.
Worked example: 1. A tin on its side touches with its curve. 2. It rolls. 3. Stand it on its flat end: it stays.

**T3. Plan your build** (A true thing about math)
> Before you place a solid, think: will it sit flat? Put flat bottoms down. Points and curves go on top.
Worked example: 1. A tin bin stands on its flat end. 2. A cone sits on top, point up. 3. A grain tower: cylinder and cone.

**T4. Faces are flat shapes** (A true thing about math)
> A face is a flat surface of a solid. Every face is a 2-D shape. Press one in flour and see its print.
Worked example: 1. Press the tin's end. 2. The print is a circle. 3. The tin is a cylinder; its end is a circle.

**T5. Faces, edges, vertices** (A true thing about math)
> A face is flat. An edge is where two faces meet. A vertex is a corner where edges meet. More than one: vertices.
Worked example: 1. Touch one face of the seed block. 2. Run a finger along an edge. 3. Tap a vertex.

**T6. Count them all** (A true thing about math)
> Mark each one as you count. Turn the solid over to find the hidden ones on the back and bottom.
Worked example: 1. Seed block, top face up: 4 vertices on top. 2. Turn it over: 4 on the bottom. 3. 8 vertices.

**T7. Curved solids** (A true thing about math)
> Here, a curved surface is not a face. A tin has 2 flat faces and 1 curved surface. A ball has one curved surface.
Worked example: 1. Tin: two flat circle ends. 2. One curved side. 3. 2 faces, 1 curved surface.

**T8. Sort by one rule** (A true thing about math)
> Pick one rule. Check every solid against it. Then sort the same solids again by a different rule.
Worked example: 1. Rule: has a curved surface. 2. Ball, tin, cone: yes. 3. Cube, crate, pyramid, wedge: no.

**T9. Drawings of solids** (A true thing about math)
> A flat drawing can show a solid. Look at the faces, not the overall look. Count shapes; name from them.
Worked example: 1. The drawing shows rectangle faces, two of them long. 2. Not all squares. 3. A cuboid, not a cube.

**T10. Prisms and pyramids** (A true thing about math)
> A prism has two matching ends joined by rectangles. A pyramid has one base and triangles meeting at a point.
Worked example: 1. Cheese wedge: two triangle ends. 2. Three rectangles between. 3. A triangular prism.

**T11. A slice makes a new face** (A true thing about math)
> Cut a solid straight through. The cut is a new flat face. Its shape depends on which way you cut.
Worked example: 1. Cheese wheel, cut straight across: a circle. 2. Cut from top to bottom: a rectangle. 3. Same solid, two shapes.

**T12. Volume counts cubes** (A true thing about math)
> Volume is how much space a solid fills. Count the unit cubes that fill it. Write cubic units: cm³ or ft³.
Worked example: 1. A crate holds 2 rows of 2 blocks. 2. Two layers. 3. 4 + 4 = 8 cubic units.

**T13. Layers, then multiply** (A true thing about math)
> Count one layer: length × width, the base area. Count the layers: the height. Volume = length × width × height = base area × height.
Worked example: 1. One layer: 4 × 3 = 12 blocks. 2. 5 layers tall. 3. 12 × 5 = 60 cubic units.

**T14. Volume adds up** (A true thing about math)
> Split a big shape into boxes that don't overlap. Find each volume. Add them. Any fair split gives the same total.
Worked example: 1. Step bin: bottom 6 × 3 × 2 = 36. 2. Top 3 × 3 × 2 = 18. 3. 36 + 18 = 54.

**T15. Euler's pattern** (A true thing about math; Keeper's Desk)
> For a solid with only flat faces: vertices take away edges, add faces. You always get 2.
Worked example: 1. Cube: 8 vertices, 12 edges, 6 faces. 2. 8 − 12 = −4. 3. −4 + 6 = 2.

## Real-world and history cards

**H1. Granaries on a scroll** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> More than 3,500 years ago, an Egyptian scribe named Ahmes copied a scroll of math problems. Some ask how much grain a granary holds.
Keeper's note:
> It is called the Rhind papyrus, after a Scottish collector who bought it in Luxor, Egypt, in 1858. Most of it is in the British Museum. Its granary problems include round granaries and box-shaped ones. The problems use cubits, an Egyptian length based on the forearm.

**H2. Euler's letter** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> In 1750 Leonhard Euler wrote a letter to his friend Christian Goldbach. He said: in a solid with flat faces, faces plus corners is two more than edges.
Keeper's note:
> Euler's proof had a gap, and others fixed it later. Today it is written V − E + F = 2. The chalk on the loft beam is a story. The letter is real.

### Set D. Counting cubes (Seedling to Keeper's Desk)

**D1 (Seedling, number)**

**D2 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: A crate 4 blocks long, 3 wide, 2 high. How many blocks?

## Key terms (glossary)

- **solid (3-D shape)**: a shape that takes up space. You can hold it.
- **flat shape (2-D shape)**: a shape with no thickness, like a print.
- **sphere**: a ball shape, curved all over.
- **cube**: a solid with six square faces.
- **cuboid**: a box shape with six rectangle faces.
- **cylinder**: a tin shape: two circle ends and a curved side.
- **cone**: a circle base and a curved side up to a point.
- **pyramid**: one flat base and triangles meeting at a point.
- **prism**: two matching ends joined by rectangles.
- **face**: a flat surface of a solid.
- **edge**: where two faces meet.
- **vertex (vertices)**: a corner where edges meet.
- **curved surface**: a surface that is not flat. It can roll.
- **cross-section**: the new flat face made by a straight cut.
- **unit cube**: a cube 1 unit on every edge.
- **volume**: how much space a solid fills, counted in unit cubes.
- **cubic unit (cm³, ft³)**: the space of one unit cube.
- **layer**: one flat level of cubes.
- **base area**: the area of the bottom face; one layer of unit cubes.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** naming solids; rolling, sliding and stacking; building with solids; the flat 2-D faces of a solid; counting faces, edges and vertices, hidden ones included; sorting solids by one rule and re-sorting; recognizing solids from drawings and their face sets; cross-sections; volume by counting unit cubes in layers, length × width × height, and composite volumes; Euler's pattern for flat-faced solids (Keeper's Desk).

**What it does not claim:** nets and folding are taught in the Shape Bench's parcel boxes (spec 126), not here. Curved solids follow one stated convention (a curved surface is not a face); schools differ. Volume uses whole units only. It does not teach volume of cylinders, cones or spheres.
