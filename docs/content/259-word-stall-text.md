## Story scenes

### Scene 1. Take a word, leave a word (Act II, the weekly market)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** The librarian sets up a stall between the apples and the bread. Its crates hold words, not food. Pip is suspicious.

**Short:**
> The librarian's stall sorts words like goods. Pip takes *enormous* and uses it on everything. Then he checks what it means.

**Longer:**
> Between the orchard keeper's apples and the baker's bread stands a new stall. Its crates are labeled FRUIT, BREAD, TOOLS and CLOTHES. Every crate holds word cards.
>
> "Take a word, leave a word," says the librarian.
>
> Pip takes *enormous*. He likes how it sounds.
>
> "An enormous apple," he says. "An enormous crumb. An enormous ladybug."
>
> Dot looks down at herself. "I'm a normal size, thank you."
>
> "What does enormous mean?" asks {guide}.
>
> Pip thinks. "Big. Really, really big."
>
> "Then is a crumb enormous?"
>
> Pip looks at the crumb on his paw. "Not really."
>
> He points at the mill wheel turning by the brook. "That's enormous."
>
> The librarian nods and hands him a second card: *colossal*.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> Words that name things sort into kinds, the way goods sort into crates. Asking what a new word means, and linking it to a word you already know, is one way readers learn new words.

### Scene 2. Warm, hot or scorching? (summer, Market Fair)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** The fair is so hot the baker's butter slides off its dish. Pip says the day is warm. Bramble says it is scorching.

**Short:**
> Pip calls the day warm. Bramble calls it scorching. The melted butter says Bramble picked the closer word.

**Longer:**
> The Market Fair bakes in the sun. The baker's butter has slid right off its dish.
>
> "Nice and warm," says Pip, fanning himself with a bun.
>
> "Warm?" says Bramble. "It's scorching."
>
> "Scorching is just a show-off word for hot."
>
> {guide} sets three cards on the word stall's ladder: *warm* at the bottom, *hot* in the middle, *scorching* at the top. "They all mean heat. They don't mean the same amount."
>
> Pip looks at the butter. He looks at the bench, too hot to sit on.
>
> "Fine," he says. "Scorching."
>
> Bramble paints the baker a sign: GIGANTIC BUNS. The buns are ordinary buns.
>
> "That one isn't true," says Pip.
>
> Bramble frowns at the buns. She paints over it: WARM, GOLDEN BUNS.

### Scene 3. ¿Qué significa? (Act III, the floating market)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** Vadoalto's boats tie up at the bank. A trader hangs a board that says ABIERTO. Pip thinks it is somebody's name.

**Short:**
> A Vadoalto trader's board says ABIERTO, then CERRADO: open, then closed. Pip learns that words in two languages are often close, not exact.

**Longer:**
> The boats from Vadoalto bump against the bank. A trader hangs a board on the mast: ABIERTO.
>
> "Who's Abierto?" asks Pip.
>
> "It means open," says the trader, and flips the board. "CERRADO. Closed."
>
> "Opposites," says Dot. "Like the hooks on the word stall."
>
> Under the sign, the trader has chalked the drinks: *frío, tibio, caliente*.
>
> "Cold, warm, hot," Pip reads.
>
> "Close," says the trader. "*Tibio* is only a little warm. Lukewarm."
>
> Then the trader points at the word stall's ladder. "¿Qué significa *scorching*? What does scorching mean?"
>
> Pip stands up tall. "Very, very hot. Hotter than hot. Like the Summer Fair."
>
> The trader laughs and chalks *scorching* above *caliente*.

**Spanish lines in this scene (fluent check):** ABIERTO (open) · CERRADO (closed) · *frío, tibio, caliente* (cold, lukewarm, hot) · ¿Qué significa *scorching*? (What does scorching mean?)

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> In Spanish, *abierto* means open and *cerrado* means closed. *Tibio* means only slightly warm, like the English word lukewarm. Words in two languages are often close in meaning, not exact matches. *(fluent check)*

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer; worked-example steps appear one tap at a time. All cards are **A true thing about language**.

**Card 1. Words come in kinds**
> Many words name things, and things come in kinds. Plums, pears and apples are all fruit. Words of one kind go in one crate.

Worked example: *Where does* rake *go?*
1. What is a rake? Something you use in the garden.
2. Is it fruit, bread, a tool or clothing?
3. A tool. It goes in the TOOLS crate.

**Card 2. You choose the rule**
> You can sort the same words by different rules: by kind, by color, by size. Say your rule first. Then sort.

Worked example: *A red cherry and a red scarf.*
1. Rule: kind. Cherry goes with fruit. Scarf goes with clothes.
2. Rule: color. Both are red, so they go together.
3. Same cards, new rule, new crates.

**Card 3. Opposites**
> An opposite is the other end: full and empty, push and pull. Some opposites have steps between them: hot, warm, cool, cold.

Worked example: *Full and empty.*
1. A full basket has no room left.
2. Take some plums out. Now it is half full.
3. Take them all out. Empty: the other end.

**Card 4. Which meaning?**
> Some words have two meanings, so they have two opposites. A light room is the opposite of a dark room. A light box is the opposite of a heavy box.

Worked example: *A right answer, a right hand.*
1. A right answer: its opposite is a wrong answer.
2. Your right hand: its opposite is your left hand.
3. Check the meaning first. Then find the opposite.

**Card 5. Ask about a new word**
> Meet a new word? Ask: what does it mean? Look at the picture and the words around it. Is it like a word you know?

Worked example: *The sign says* Skeins of wool.
1. Ask: what is a skein?
2. Look: the picture shows coils of yarn.
3. Link: it is like a ball of yarn, wound loosely.

**Card 6. Try out a new word**
> Use a new word where its meaning fits. Enormous means very, very big. An enormous mill wheel fits. An enormous crumb does not.

Worked example: *Pip's new word,* colossal.
1. The librarian says colossal is like huge.
2. Is a pebble colossal? No, it's small.
3. Is a mountain colossal? Yes. The word fits.

**Card 7. Shades of meaning**
> Some words mean nearly the same thing but differ in how much: warm, hot, scorching. Put them on a ladder, from least to most.

Worked example: *Damp, soaked, wet.*
1. Damp: a little bit wet.
2. Wet: plainly wet.
3. Soaked: wet all the way through. Ladder: damp, wet, soaked.

**Card 8. Too close to call**
> Some words are too close to rank. Huge, enormous and gigantic all mean much bigger than big. Dictionaries give them almost the same meaning, so they can share a rung.

Worked example: *Big, huge, enormous.*
1. Big goes on the bottom rung.
2. Huge and enormous both mean much bigger.
3. They sit side by side on the top rung.

**Card 9. Kind, then feature**
> To say what a word means, name its kind. Then add a feature. A spade is a tool for digging.

Worked example: *What is a scarf?*
1. Kind: clothing.
2. Feature: you wrap it around your neck.
3. A scarf is clothing you wrap around your neck.

**Card 10. Does something else fit?**
> Check your feature. If something else of that kind fits too, add another feature. A duck is a bird that swims. So is a swan.

Worked example: *A duck, not a swan.*
1. A duck is a bird that swims.
2. A swan swims too. Add a feature.
3. A duck is a swimming bird with a short neck.

**Card 11. Close, not the same**
> Synonyms are words close in meaning, like talk and speak. They are rarely exact matches. Each one carries its own size, speed or feeling.

Worked example: *Jog and sprint.*
1. Both mean running.
2. Jog: a steady, easy pace.
3. Sprint: as fast as you can. Close, not the same.

**Card 12. Using a thesaurus**
> A thesaurus lists words close in meaning. Many words have more than one meaning, so find the meaning you need first. Then try each word in your sentence.

Worked example: *A thin line of paint ran slowly down the sign.*
1. Which meaning of run? Here, run means flow.
2. Look in the flow group: drip, pour, stream, trickle.
3. A thin, slow line? Trickled fits.

**Card 13. The vivid word**
> A vivid word helps a reader picture something exactly. Ask what you want the reader to see or feel. Then check the word is still true.

Worked example: *Pip went past Bramble's stall.*
1. What should the reader see? Pip being very quiet.
2. Went says nothing about quiet.
3. Pip tiptoed past Bramble's stall.

## Benches

Every bench screen shows: *Words can fit more than one way. Tap* Why? *to see the reason.* No score, timer, lock or mastery label. Drags have tap equivalents.

### (a) The Crates

**Header:** Put each word card in its crate.

**Crates:** FRUIT · BREAD · TOOLS · CLOTHES

**Cards:** apple · pear · plum · cherry · loaf · bun · spade · rake · trowel · scarf · mitten · cap

**Card feedback (wrong crate, any card):** Ask: what kind of thing is it? Try that crate.

**Why together? (after a crate fills):** Fruit grows on plants and holds seeds. Bread is baked. Tools help you work. Clothes are worn.

**Two rules (Sapling):** Now sort by color. Make your own crate labels.
- Cards (pictures colored): red cherry · red apple · red scarf · green pear · green mitten · green cap · brown loaf · brown bun · brown rake
- After sorting: Same cards, new rule, new crates. The rule decides.

**Odd one out:** Tap the word that doesn't belong. Then tap why.
- Set 1: apple · pear · spade · plum
- Set 2: scarf · cap · bun · mitten
- Set 3: rake · trowel · loaf · spade

**Keeper's note (A true thing from history, Two rules):**
> Plant scientists call a tomato a fruit, because it grows from a flower and holds seeds. Cooks use it like a vegetable. In 1893, a US court called it a vegetable for a tax law. The rule decided.

### (b) The Opposites Hooks

**Header:** Hang each word on the hook beside its opposite.

**Pairs:** full / empty · push / pull · buy / sell · early / late · open / closed · arrive / leave · hot / cold · wet / dry

**Hook feedback (wrong pair):** Those are different, but not opposite ends. Try another.

**Steps between:** Some opposites have words between them. Hang the in-between cards.
- hot · warm · cool · cold
- full · half full · empty
- early · on time · late

**Two sides of one swap:** Buy and sell are opposites in a different way. At a stall, you buy a plum. The stallholder sells it.

**Which meaning?** Pick the meaning first. Then hang the opposite.
- a light box → heavy · a light room → dark
- a right answer → wrong · your right hand → left

**The floating market card (Act III, fluent check):** ABIERTO / CERRADO hang beside OPEN / CLOSED. *In Spanish too, a sign can flip to its opposite.*

### (c) The Shades Ladder

**Header:** Put the words on the ladder. Least goes at the bottom, most at the top.

**Ladders:**
- Heat: warm · hot · scorching
- Wet: damp · wet · soaked
- Sound: whisper · talk · shout
- Speed: walk · jog · sprint
- Rain: drizzle · rain · downpour
- Size: big · huge · enormous · gigantic (huge, enormous and gigantic may share the top rung)
- Four rungs (Sapling): cool · warm · hot · scorching

**Ladder feedback (two cards swapped):** Read both in one sentence. Which says more?

**Side by side:** Some words are too close to rank. Put them side by side on one rung.

**Side by side feedback:** Any order of huge, enormous and gigantic fits, as long as big stays below them.

**Which walk?** Tap the walking word that fits each sentence.
- Pip ___ past the sleeping cat. → tiptoes
- The band ___ through the fair, all in step. → marches
- Bramble ___ through the puddles, splashing hard. → stomps
- Dot ___ along the river, enjoying the sun. → strolls

**Which walk? feedback (any wrong word):** Picture it. Would that walk wake the cat, or splash, or keep in step?

### (d) The Label Table

**Header:** New goods need labels. First find out what the word means.

**New words:**
- *Fresh damsons* (picture: a basket of small, dark purple fruit)
- *Skeins of wool* (picture: loose coils of yarn on the clothes stall)
- *colossal* (the librarian: "Colossal is like huge.")

**Ask card (shown first, every word):** What does ___ mean? Look at the picture. Read the words around it. Is it like a word you know?

**Link lines (after the child picks):**
- A damson is a kind of plum: small and dark purple.
- A skein is a loose coil of yarn.
- Colossal means very, very big, like huge.

**Try it out:** Which thing is colossal? Which basket holds damsons? (tap a picture)

**Build a label:** Choose a kind tile, then a feature tile.
- spade: a tool / for digging
- scarf: clothing / that you wrap around your neck
- duck: a bird / that swims
- pear: a fruit / that grows on trees

**So is a...? (Sapling):** Does anything else fit your label? Then add a feature.
- duck: So is a swan! Add: with a short neck.
- pear: So is an apple! Add: that narrows toward the stem.
- spade: So is a trowel! Add: with a long handle.

### (e) The Sign Board (Sapling)

**Header:** Make each sign vivid. Pick the word that shows what the reader should see.

**Say it first:** Say the sign aloud with each word. Then choose.

**Signs:**
- Baker: *Nice bread.* Should look fresh from the oven. Bank: crusty · nice · fine. → *Crusty loaves, warm from the oven.*
- Orchard keeper: *Good apples.* Should sound crunchy. Bank: crisp · good · okay. → *Crisp apples from the orchard.*
- Pip's note: *Pip went past Bramble's stall.* Should feel quiet. Bank: tiptoed · stomped · went. → *Pip tiptoed past Bramble's stall.*

**True as well as vivid:** Bramble's first sign said GIGANTIC BUNS. The buns were ordinary. A vivid word must still be true.

### (f) The Stall Thesaurus (Keeper's Desk)

**Header (A made-up model, written for this pack):** A thesaurus groups words by meaning. Here is the stall's own entry for *run*.

**The entry:**
> **run** (verb)
> 1. move fast on your feet: dash, jog, race, scamper, sprint, trot
> 2. be in charge of: keep, look after, manage
> 3. flow: drip, pour, stream, trickle
>
> *Close, not the same. Try each word in your sentence.*

**Pick the meaning:** Which meaning of *run* is in each sentence?
- Pip runs to catch the ferry. → 1
- Dot runs the post office. → 2
- Rain runs off the stall roof. → 3

**Pick the word:** Swap *ran* for the word that fits best.
- Pip ran as fast as he could. → sprinted
- A thin line of paint ran slowly down the sign. → trickled
- A mouse ran under the stall in tiny, quick steps. → scampered

**Close, not the same (cards):**
- jog / sprint: Both are running. A jog is easy. A sprint is as fast as you can go.
- trickle / pour: Both are flowing. A trickle is a little. Pouring is a lot.
- whisper / announce: Both are ways of saying. A whisper is for someone close by. An announcement is for everyone.

**Where the word comes from (A true thing about language):** *Thesaurus* comes from a Greek word for a treasure or a storehouse: a store of words.

**A true thing from history:** Peter Mark Roget published his *Thesaurus of English Words and Phrases* in London in 1852. He grouped words by their ideas, not by the alphabet.

**A3 (Seedling).**

**E4 (Keeper's Desk).**

- Hint: How much paint is it?
- Explanation: A thin, slow line trickles. The thesaurus words are close, not the same.

- Hint: Which word is the quietest?
- Explanation: A vivid word shows the reader exactly what you mean. Tiptoed shows quiet.

**E6 (Sapling).**

## "I'm stuck" ladder

**Step 1, hint:** the item's own hint.

**Step 2, an easier case and how to start:**
- Sorting items: "Try this one first: a cherry. It grows on a tree, so it's fruit. Now ask what kind yours is."
- Opposite items: "Try this one first: up and down. Each is the other end. Now find the other end of yours."
- New-word items: "Try this one first: a sign says *pears* over a basket of pears. The picture tells you. Now look at yours."
- Ladder items: "Try this one first: a puddle and a lake. A lake is bigger. Now ask which of yours says more."
- Label items: "Try this one first: a cat is an animal that purrs. Kind, then feature. Now do yours."
- Close-word items: "Try this one first: nibble and gobble. Both are eating. One is slow and small. Now compare yours."

**Step 3:** Skip for now. You can come back any time.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Kind:** a group of things that belong together, like fruit or tools.
- **Feature:** something about a thing that helps tell it apart.
- **Opposite (antonym):** a word that means the other end, like full and empty.
- **Shades of meaning:** small differences between words that mean nearly the same, like warm and hot.
- **Synonym:** a word close in meaning to another, like talk and speak.
- **Thesaurus:** a book or list that groups words close in meaning.
- **Meaning:** what a word says. Some words have more than one meaning.
- **Vivid:** clear and bright, so a reader can picture it.
- **Trowel:** a small hand tool for digging.
- **Skein:** a loose coil of yarn.
- **Damson:** a small, dark purple plum.
- **Lukewarm:** only a little warm.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** sorting words by kind and by other rules; opposites, including opposites with steps between and words whose opposite depends on their meaning; asking about a new word and linking it to known words; trying a new word where it fits; shades of meaning on a ladder; saying what a word means by its kind and a feature that sets it apart; synonyms as close, not exact; choosing from a thesaurus by meaning, then by fit; and the vivid word that is also true.

**About shades and synonyms:** every ladder in this pack uses words whose order most speakers agree on. Where they don't (huge, enormous, gigantic), the bench accepts any order on a shared rung. Synonyms are rarely exact: they differ in degree, manner, feeling (connotation) or formality. This pack teaches degree and manner; the Magpie pack (265) goes further into feeling, and the To the Council pack (271) into formal and friendly words.

**About the thesaurus:** the *run* entry was written for this pack. It is simpler than a printed thesaurus, which lists many more words and meanings.

**What it does not claim:** the sample sentences, signs and stalls are made up. Spanish lines need a fluent reader before build. Nothing a child says aloud or writes on paper is recorded.

**UK terms:** English national curriculum materials use the same words, synonym and antonym, and ask Year 5 and 6 pupils to use a thesaurus.
