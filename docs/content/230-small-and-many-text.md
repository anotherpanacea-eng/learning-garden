# Small and Many: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 230](../specs/230-small-and-many.md). Use as written, after independent review and a subject check. `{guide}` is the chosen companion; the explorer is "you". Pip is a fox (he/him), Dot a ladybug (she/her), Bramble she/her, the librarian they/them, the orchard keeper she/her, Nuria an otter (she/her). US spelling, no em-dashes. Facts marked [check] stay out of the build until the source audit clears them.

**House rules for this text:** every body fact is about a drawn figure, a cast animal or a real minibeast. No line asks about the explorer's own body, home, family, feelings or where they live. The "you" in prompts is the explorer doing the task. Real minibeasts are looked at off screen only, with a grown-up, and nothing from that is recorded.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. The fallen woodpile (Act I, chapter 3: investigate and plant)

**Hook:** The storm knocked the café woodpile flat. Pip wants it tidy. Dot wants it gentle.

**Short:** Pip grabs a log. "I'll sweep out all the creepy-crawlies!" Dot folds her arms. "Some of my best neighbors are creepy-crawlies."

**Longer:** The storm had rolled the café woodpile across the yard. Pip rolled up his sleeves, which foxes do not have, and reached for the broom. "I'll sweep out all the creepy-crawlies," he said. Dot landed on the nearest log. "Some of my best neighbors are creepy-crawlies," she said. "Lift slowly." {guide} tipped the log back. Underneath, the ground was dark and damp. Six woodlice trundled away from the light. "Why do they hide under there?" asked Pip. "They're not hiding," said Dot. "It's damp. They dry out in the sun." "So we stack it back the way it was," said {guide}, "with gaps at the bottom." Pip looked at the broom. Then he put it away.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** Woodlice are not insects. They are crustaceans, like crabs. Their shells hold water poorly, so they live where the air is damp.

### Scene 2. Counting kinds (Act II, chapter 2: a garden across the seasons)

**Hook:** The town keeps records now, "so next time we'll know". The librarian starts a page: every kind of small creature in the garden.

**Short:** Pip finds an ant nest. "Two hundred!" he shouts. The librarian writes one line. "Two hundred ants," they say. "One kind."

**Longer:** The librarian had a fresh page headed *Small Neighbors*. "One line for each kind," they said. Pip lay on his stomach by the path. "Ants! Ten. Fifty. Two hundred!" The librarian wrote one line: *ants*. "That's not fair," said Pip. "There are loads." "Loads of ants," said the librarian. "But they're all the same kind. A kind is called a species. I count kinds." Dot found a shiny green beetle on a leaf, then a black one under a stone, then a tiny striped one in the grass. "Three kinds," she said. "All beetles." By teatime the page had twenty-two lines. Nine were beetles. "Why so many beetles?" asked Pip. "That," said the librarian, "is a very good question."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** About a million kinds of insect have names. About 400,000 of them are beetles. Scientists think millions more kinds are still waiting to be named.

### Scene 3. Champions of the Small (Act II, chapter 1: the weekly market, at the summer Market Fair)

**Hook:** A poster at the Market Fair says *Champions of the Small*. Pip reads it and says he can beat them all.

**Short:** "A flea jumps a hundred times its length? Easy." Pip jumps. He lands one fox-length away. The flea is not impressed.

**Longer:** The poster was nailed to the cider stall. *Champions of the Small*, it said, with four pictures: a beetle, a flea, a dragonfly and another beetle with a puff of spray. "A flea can jump more than a hundred times its own length," read Dot. "Easy," said Pip. He crouched, wiggled and jumped. He landed one fox-length away, in a basket of plums. "One length," said {guide}. "It's harder when you're big," said Pip, picking a plum out of his ear. "That's exactly it," said {guide}. "Being tiny is the trick." Bramble's magpie stole the plum.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** In 2010, scientists tested horned dung beetles. A male could pull about 1,141 times his own weight. No insect yet tested beats that, for its size.

### Scene 4. The night garden lens (Act V, path 1: a night garden)

**Hook:** The orchard keeper lends you her hand lens. At dusk, the hedge has visitors.

**Short:** A moth uncurls a long tongue like a spring and sips. "It can't bite," says the orchard keeper. "It only drinks."

**Longer:** The orchard keeper's hand lens was old and heavy and wrapped in a cloth. "Hold it close to your eye," she said, "and bring the leaf to it." At dusk a moth landed on the hedge. Through the lens, a long tongue uncurled like a watch spring and dipped into a flower. "Can it bite?" asked Pip, from a safe distance. "No jaws," said the orchard keeper. "Only that straw. It drinks." On a grass stem nearby hung something like a grasshopper made of paper. Pip poked it gently. It was empty. "Its old skin," said {guide}. "It climbed out and grew." "Out of its own skin?" said Pip. He looked at his paws for a long time.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** An insect's skeleton is on the outside. It can't stretch. To grow, the insect makes a new, soft skin underneath, climbs out of the old one, swells, and lets the new one harden. This is called molting.

### Scene 5. The walking twig (Act III, chapter 3: the route by the high ford)

**Hook:** On the old path to the high ford, you stop by a hedge. One of the twigs starts to walk.

**Short:** A twig walks along a branch. "It turned itself into a twig to hide," says Bramble. {guide} isn't so sure.

**Longer:** Bramble's ribbons led the way along the old path. At the hedge by the ford, everyone stopped for a drink. "That twig moved," said Pip. It had. It stepped, swayed, and stepped again. It had six thin legs. "A stick insect," said Bramble. "Clever thing. It turned itself into a twig to hide from birds." Her magpie stared straight at it and saw nothing at all. "Did it turn into one?" said {guide}. "Or was it born that shape?" Bramble frowned. "Born, I suppose. Its mother looked like a twig too." "So who decided it should look like a twig?" "Nobody," said Bramble slowly. "I'll have to think about that." The twig walked on.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** More than 3,000 kinds of stick and leaf insect are known, mostly in warm places. Many sway like twigs in the wind. None of them chose its shape.

### Scene 6. Apples and reeds (Act III, chapter 2: the seed exchange)

**Hook:** Nuria brings Vadoalto's reeds downstream. The orchard keeper offers apples. Who carries the pollen for each?

**Short:** "¿Quién lleva el polen del carrizo?" asks Pip, practicing. (Who carries the reed's pollen?) Nuria laughs. "El viento. The wind."

**Longer:** Nuria set a bundle of tall reeds on the table. The orchard keeper set down a basket of apples. Pip had been practicing. "¿Quién lleva el polen del carrizo?" he said. (Who carries the reed's pollen?) "El viento," said Nuria. "The wind. Reeds and grasses let it blow." "Mine need the bees," said the orchard keeper. "And the hoverflies, and some beetles. No visitors, no apples." "And bread?" asked Pip, who was worried about bread. "Wheat is a grass," said Nuria. "The wind does that too." {guide} looked at the two tables. "So if the bees went, the bread would stay. But the apples..." The orchard keeper held up one apple. "Fewer of these."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** Wheat, rice and maize are pollinated by wind or by themselves. Many fruits, nuts and vegetables need insects. Losing pollinators would cost variety and harvest, not all our food.

## Teach cards

Each worked example reveals one step per tap.

**T1. A small place that fits** (A true thing about science). Different minibeasts live in different small places. Each place gives them what they need.
Worked example: (1) Under the log: damp and dark. (2) Who lives there? (3) Woodlice and worms.

**T2. Damp keeps a woodlouse safe.** A woodlouse dries out in the sun. Under a log, it stays damp. It doesn't choose like picking a house. It just lives best there.
Worked example: (1) Lift the log. (2) Feel the damp ground. (3) The woodlice are there.

**T3. Gentle hands.** Look first. If a grown-up says yes, help it onto a tray with a soft brush. Never grab. Never squash.
Worked example: (1) Brush in front of the beetle. (2) It walks onto the tray. (3) Look closely.

**T4. Put it back.** When you finish looking, put the creature back where you found it. Lay the log back the way it lay.
Worked example: (1) Tip the tray by the log. (2) The woodlouse walks home. (3) Lay the log down gently.

**T5. Every one has a job.** Worms help make soil. Woodlice break up dead leaves. Bees carry pollen. Ladybugs eat aphids. Birds eat beetles.
Worked example: (1) A worm pulls a leaf down. (2) The leaf rots into soil. (3) Plants grow.

**T6. Insect or not?** An adult insect has six legs and three body parts. A spider has eight legs and two parts. Count to check.
Worked example: (1) Count the ant's legs: six. (2) Count its parts: three. (3) It's an insect.

**T7. Kinds, not bodies.** A kind of living thing is called a species. A nest of a million ants is still one kind.
Worked example: (1) One nest: many ants. (2) All the same kind. (3) One species.

**T8. The most kinds of all.** About a million kinds of insect have names. About 400,000 are beetles. Mammals have about 6,700 kinds.
Worked example: (1) Beetle kinds: 400,000. (2) Mammal kinds: 6,700. (3) 400,000 ÷ 6,700 is about 60.

**T9. Why so many?** Small, so they need little food. Fast breeding. Wings to reach new places. A tough outside skeleton that suits many lives.
Worked example: (1) Small. (2) So one leaf can feed many. (3) So many places can each hold a different kind.

**T10. Still being named.** About 1 million insect kinds have names. One careful estimate says about 5.5 million exist.
Worked example: (1) Estimate: 5.5 million. (2) Named: 1 million. (3) 5.5 − 1 = 4.5 million not yet named.

**T11. For its size.** An insect record is a record for its size. A flea jumps far for a flea. A person still jumps farther.
Worked example: (1) Flea: 2 mm long. (2) Jump: 30 cm = 300 mm. (3) 300 ÷ 2 = 150 body lengths.

**T12. Tiny is the trick.** A tiny, light body with a hard outside skeleton can do huge things for its size. Make it giant, and the trick stops working.
Worked example: (1) Make the flea bigger. (2) Its weight grows faster than its strength. (3) Its jump shrinks.

**T13. Eyes of many lenses.** An insect's compound eye is made of many small parts, each with its own lens. A worker honeybee has about 5,000 in each eye.
Worked example: (1) Zoom in on the eye. (2) See the tiny lenses. (3) Each one sees a small piece.

**T14. Breathing holes.** Insects breathe through small holes along the body, called spiracles. Air goes in, then along branching tubes. Not through the mouth.
Worked example: (1) Find the row of dots on the beetle's side. (2) Air goes in there. (3) Tubes carry it inside.

**T15. Mouths fit food.** A beetle chews with jaws. A butterfly sips through a coiled straw. A housefly mops with a sponge pad.
Worked example: (1) A leaf needs cutting. (2) Jaws can cut. (3) So the beetle eats the leaf.

**T16. Growing out of your skeleton.** An outside skeleton can't stretch. So a growing insect makes a soft new skin underneath, sheds the old one, swells, and hardens. This is molting.
Worked example: (1) The old skin splits. (2) The insect climbs out, soft. (3) It swells. (4) The new skin hardens.

**T17. Copying insects.** Engineers copy insect designs. This is called biomimicry. A domed camera copied many-lens eyes to see very wide.
Worked example: (1) An insect eye has many lenses on a dome. (2) Engineers put tiny lenses on a dome. (3) The camera sees very wide.

**T18. Born that shape.** A stick insect is born twig-shaped. Its young are twig-shaped too. It never turned itself into a twig.
Worked example: (1) A twig-shaped mother. (2) Twig-shaped eggs hatch. (3) Twig-shaped young.

**T19. Who is left changes the mix.** Birds find plain insects more easily. Twiggy ones are left to have young. Next time, more are twiggy.
Worked example: (1) Half twiggy, half plain. (2) Birds find more plain ones. (3) The young are mostly twiggy.

**T20. Many generations.** In this model, each insect keeps its twiggy or plain trait as it grows. The mix changes over generations; real insects can change form during development.
Worked example: (1) Tag one twiggy insect. (2) It stays in the twiggy category as it grows. (3) Its young inherit that trait.

**T21. Two steps** (Keeper's Desk). Pollination: pollen is carried to the flower's stigma. Fertilization: later, inside, a pollen cell joins an egg cell. Then a seed forms.
Worked example: (1) A bee brings pollen to the stigma. (2) Later, inside, cells join. (3) A seed starts.

**T22. Wind or insects?** Wheat, rice and maize are pollinated by wind or by themselves. Many fruits, nuts and vegetables need insects.
Worked example: (1) Wheat: wind. (2) Apples: insects. (3) No bees: bread stays, apples fall.

**T23. What we would lose.** Losing pollinators would cost food variety and harvest size. It would not take away all our food.
Worked example: (1) Few pollinators. (2) Fewer apples, cherries and almonds. (3) The bread is still there.

**T24. What helps, slowly.** Flower-rich edges, hedges and less spraying help pollinators. They build back over seasons, not overnight.
Worked example: (1) Plant a flower margin. (2) Next season: more pollinators. (3) The season after: more again.

**T25. Why not by hand?** People can pollinate by hand. But crops have billions of flowers, open only a few days, over huge areas.
Worked example: (1) A helper does 10 trees a day. (2) Bloom lasts 5 days: 50 trees each. (3) 600 trees need 600 ÷ 50 = 12 helpers.

## History cards

**Citrus ants (A true thing from history).** An old book about the plants of southern China tells how orange growers put ants' nests in their trees. The ants guarded the fruit from other insects. People even sold the ants at market. The book is said to be about 1,700 years old, though scholars still debate its age. It may be the oldest record of using one creature to protect a crop.

**Hooke's flea (A true thing from history).** In 1665, Robert Hooke in London drew a flea and a fly's eye through a microscope. The drawings were huge and folded out of the book. Readers could see every hair on a flea's legs.

**Charles Henry Turner's bees (A true thing from history).** In 1910, Charles Henry Turner, a Black American scientist and teacher, trained honeybees to visit colored boxes. He tested carefully. Bees could see and remember colors. Racism kept him from a university job. He kept doing science anyway.

**Edmond Albius's vanilla (A true thing from history, Keeper's Desk).** Vanilla vines were brought to Réunion, an island in the Indian Ocean, far from their home in Mexico. They flowered, but almost no pods grew, because the insects that pollinate them at home were missing. In 1841, a boy of about twelve named Edmond Albius, who was enslaved, found a quick way to pollinate each flower by hand, with a thin stick and his thumb. Growers still use his way today. In 1848, slavery ended on the island and he was free. Some people asked for a reward for him, but he never received one.

**The Eastgate Centre (A true thing from history).** In 1996, in Harare, Zimbabwe, architect Mick Pearce finished a building cooled by air moving through it and out of tall chimneys. He took the idea from termite mounds. Scientists still study exactly how the mounds move air.

**A story about beetles (A story people tell).** People say a scientist once joked that whoever made the world must love beetles, because there are so many kinds. Nobody can find where he said it. Even the first printing said it might be made up.

### Set 1. Under the log (Sprout)

**1.1 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Where does a woodlouse live best?
- A. On a sunny wall *(misconception: animals-live-anywhere)*
- B. Under a damp log *(right)*
- C. In the pond water *(misconception: habitat mixed up with another creature's)*
Hint: Does a woodlouse like damp or dry?
Feedback: A: In the sun it would dry out. B: Yes. Damp and dark suits it. C: Woodlice live on land, near damp.
Explanation: A woodlouse needs damp. Under a log it stays damp.

**1.2 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Why does the woodlouse stay under the log?
- A. It's damp, so it won't dry out. *(right)*
- B. It picked that log as its favorite house. *(misconception: chooses-like-a-house)*
- C. It's dirty, so it hides. *(misconception: dark-means-dirty)*
Hint: What happens to a woodlouse in the sun?
Feedback: A: Yes. The damp keeps it safe. B: It doesn't pick a house. It lives best there. C: Damp places aren't dirty. They're what it needs.
Explanation: The damp under the log is what a woodlouse needs.

**1.3 (Sprout, match)**
Prompt: Put each creature in the place that suits it.
Cards: worm · pond skater · aphid · woodlouse
Places: soil · pond water · sunny leaf · under the log
Answer: worm → soil; pond skater → pond water; aphid → sunny leaf; woodlouse → under the log.
Misconception tested: the same creatures live everywhere.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Minibeast:** a small animal with no backbone, like a beetle, worm or snail.
- **Habitat:** the place where a living thing lives and finds what it needs.
- **Micro-habitat:** a very small habitat, like the ground under one log.
- **Damp:** a little wet.
- **Woodlouse:** a small gray land animal with a hard, curved back and fourteen legs. It is not an insect.
- **Aphid:** a tiny insect that drinks juice from plants.
- **Pond skater:** an insect that stands and walks on the top of still water.
- **Hoverfly:** a fly with stripes like a bee that hovers in the air. It has no sting.
- **Insect:** an animal with six legs and three body parts when grown.
- **Arachnid:** an animal like a spider, with eight legs and two body parts.
- **Crustacean:** an animal like a crab or a woodlouse, with a hard shell.
- **Species:** one kind of living thing.
- **Pollinator:** an animal that carries pollen from flower to flower.
- **Nectar:** sweet juice inside a flower.
- **Body length:** how long an animal is, from front to back.
- **For its size:** compared with how big the animal is.
- **Compound eye:** an eye made of many small parts, each with its own lens.
- **Lens:** a clear, curved part that helps an eye see.
- **Spiracle:** a breathing hole along an insect's body.
- **Mandibles:** an insect's chewing jaws.
- **Proboscis:** a long tube a butterfly or moth sips through.
- **Exoskeleton:** a hard skeleton on the outside of the body.
- **Molting:** shedding an old outside skin to grow a bigger one.
- **Biomimicry:** copying a design from nature.
- **Mosaic:** a picture made of many small pieces.
- **Generation:** parents, then their young, then their young's young: each step is a generation.
- **Mimic:** a living thing that looks like a different one.
- **Pollination:** carrying pollen to a flower's stigma.
- **Stigma:** the sticky top in the middle of a flower that catches pollen.
- **Fertilization:** inside the flower, a pollen cell joins an egg cell to start a seed.
- **Harvest:** the crop that is picked.
- **Pesticide:** a spray used against pests. Some harm helpful insects too.
- **Flower margin:** a strip of wild flowers along the edge of a field.
- **Enslaved:** forced to work for someone else, with no freedom and no pay.

## Off-screen invitations (grown-up-led, nothing recorded)

1. **Lift, look, put back.** With a grown-up, lift a log or stone slowly. Look at who is there. Lay it back exactly as it lay. (The grown-up chooses the log and lifts it.)
2. **Count, don't catch.** With a grown-up, watch a minibeast walk. Count its legs from a step away. Insect or not?
3. **Flower watch.** On a sunny day, watch one patch of flowers from a step away with a grown-up. Who visits? Bees, hoverflies, butterflies or beetles?

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** that minibeasts live in small places that suit their needs, and are looked at gently and put back, for real reasons; insects as one group among minibeasts, with more kinds than any other animal group, and why; insect records as records for their size; insect parts shaped for their jobs, and molting; how a population's mix of inherited twiggy/plain traits changes over generations while each insect keeps its category in this model; and, at the Keeper's Desk, why losing pollinators would cost food variety and harvest, not all food.

**What it does not claim:** the benches are made-up models with stated rules. The flea's size, the dragonfly's length, the twig-insect numbers and the orchard's apples are simplified pictures, not measurements. The peppered moth (the real, measured case of natural selection) is told in the Like and Unlike pack. Hand pollination in some Sichuan pear and apple orchards is real, but people disagree about why it started and why it continues.

**Safety:** Real minibeasts are for gentle looking, with a grown-up. Never grab, squash or keep one. Put everything back the way it was.

**Privacy:** nothing here asks about your child's body, home or family, and nothing they find outdoors is recorded. The drawn figure in the Hall of Records is a drawing, not your child.
