# Sparks and Switches: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 163](../specs/163-sparks-and-switches.md). Use as written, after independent spec review and a physics subject check. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion and is never gendered. Moss has no pronouns; Pip is he/him; Bramble is she/her and has a magpie; Dot is she/her; the librarian is they/them; Wren Halloway is she/her. Sources are in the [source audit](../source-audits/163-sparks-and-switches.md). Nothing here is scored or timed.

## Labels used on every card

- **A story from Willowbank**: the town, the lanterns and every character are made up.
- **A true thing about science**: checked science, explained simply.
- **A true thing from history**: something people really did or wrote.
- **A made-up model**: a simple game rule, shown on the card. It is not a real measurement.
- **A safety rule**: a fixed rule. No model or game decides it.

## The safety rule (shown on arrival, on every bench and in the Grown-up panel)

> **A safety rule.** Wall sockets, plugs and anything that plugs in are never for experiments. Never go near a fallen wire outdoors; tell a grown-up. Experiment only with AA or AAA cells, and only with a grown-up.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. What can mend a wire? (Act I, chapter 4: mend the village)

**Hook:** A lantern wire snapped in the storm. Everyone has something to mend it with. Only some of it will work.

**Short:**
> Pip offers string. Bramble offers a ribbon. Dot offers a spoon. Moss clips each into the tester. Only the spoon makes the bulb glow.

**Longer:**
> The storm snapped the wire on the post office lantern. Moss lays the two broken ends on the table.
>
> "Join them with this," says Pip, holding out a piece of string.
>
> "Or this," says Bramble. She unties a red ribbon. "It's prettier."
>
> Dot takes off her hat, thinks, and puts it back on. Then she fetches a spoon from the café.
>
> Moss sets up a tester: a cell, a bulb and two clips with a gap between them. "If the bulb glows, the thing in the gap lets electricity through."
>
> The string: dark. The ribbon: dark. Bramble frowns at it.
>
> The spoon: the bulb glows.
>
> "Metal," says {guide}. "Let's try everything in the basket."

### Scene 2. The string that went dark (Lantern Night, winter festival)

**Hook:** Lantern Night. Five lanterns hang along Willow Bridge. Moss closes the switch. Nothing happens at all.

**Short:**
> One lantern cracked in the storm, and the whole string went dark. Moss rewires it so each lantern has its own path.

**Longer:**
> Five lanterns hang along Willow Bridge, one for each post. Moss closes the switch. Every lantern stays dark.
>
> "They're all broken!" says Pip.
>
> "Maybe only one," says {guide}.
>
> You check them one by one. The third lantern's bulb has a snapped filament. Moss swaps it, and all five glow.
>
> "One bad bulb, and the whole bridge goes dark," says the miller. "That can't happen on the night itself."
>
> Moss draws on the back of a flour bag: a square for the old string, then a ladder, each lantern on its own rung. "One path before. Own paths now."
>
> They rewire. Bramble unscrews the third bulb to test it. The other four keep glowing.
>
> "I'd have wired it that way from the start," says Bramble. This time, she's right.

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about science):
> Old strings of holiday lights were often wired on one path, so one failed bulb could darken them all. Those lights plug into the wall, so they are never for experiments.

### Scene 3. The spark and the storm (Act VI, chapter 3: the long rain)

**Hook:** Rain for days. Everyone waits indoors at the café. Bramble is bored, and she has found a balloon.

**Short:**
> Bramble rubs a balloon on her hair. Her hair stands up. Outside, lightning flashes. "Same thing," says the librarian. "Much bigger."

**Longer:**
> The long rain drums on the café roof. Nobody goes outside.
>
> Bramble rubs a balloon on her hair, fast. When she lifts it away, her hair rises after it. Ember laughs so hard a puff of smoke comes out.
>
> She holds the balloon near a pile of paper scraps. They jump up and cling to it.
>
> A white flash fills the window. The librarian starts counting under their breath. "One, two, three..." At nine, the thunder rolls in.
>
> "That flash was a spark," says the librarian. "Like the tiny one you'd get from your balloon. Only enormous."
>
> "Can we go and look?" asks Pip.
>
> "No," says the librarian. "If you can hear thunder, you stay in." They write the count in the *Willowbank Gazette*: nine seconds, about three kilometers away.

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about science):
> Lightning can heat the air it passes through to about 50,000 °F (about 28,000 °C). That's five times hotter than the Sun's surface. The air bursts outward, and that bang is thunder.

### Scene 4. A magnet that lets go (Keeper's Desk)

**Hook:** The miller's magnet holds the storm nails so tightly that nobody can get them off.

**Short:**
> Wren Halloway opens the Coil and Nail on the desk. A coil of wire round an iron nail. Switch on: clips jump. Switch off: they fall.

## Teach cards

Each card is one idea. Worked examples reveal one step per tap.

**T1. Remember the path** (Seedling and up, A true thing about science)
> At the Lantern Circuit Bench, a bulb glowed only on a complete path: cell, wire, bulb, wire, and back to the cell.

Link: *Look again at the Lantern Circuit Bench.* (No new example here.)

**T2. Through or stopped** (Sprout and up)
> A conductor lets electricity through. An insulator stops it. Most metals are conductors. Wood, plastic and rubber are insulators.

Worked example: *The tester.*
1. Put a steel nail across the gap. The path is complete. The bulb glows.
2. Swap in a wooden peg. The bulb stays dark.
3. Wood is an insulator. The gap stays open.

**T3. Inside a wire** (Seedling and up)
> A wire has a metal middle and a plastic coat. Electricity goes through the metal. The coat keeps it in.

Worked example: *One wire, two places to touch.*
1. Clip onto the plastic coat. Dark.
2. Clip onto the bare metal ends. It glows.
3. Same wire. Only the metal part lets electricity through.

**T4. A gap you can close** (Sprout and up)
> A switch is a gap in the path. Close it, and metal bridges the gap. Open it, and the path breaks.

Worked example: *Two pins and a paper clip.*
1. The clip touches one pin. The switch is open. Dark.
2. Swing the clip to touch both pins. Closed. It glows.
3. Swing it away. Open again. Dark.

**T5. Open means off** (Seedling and up)
> In a circuit, open means the path is broken: off. Closed means complete: on. An open shop means the opposite.

Worked example: *Moving the switch.*
1. The switch sits beside the cell. Open it. Dark.
2. Move it beside the bulb. Open it. Still dark.
3. A break anywhere in the loop stops the whole loop.

**T6. Circuit symbols** (Seedling and up)
> Symbols are short names for parts. A cell is a long line and a short line. A lamp is a circle with a cross.

Worked example: *Making a key.*
1. Cell: the long line is the plus end.
2. Two cells joined make a battery: two pairs of lines.
3. A switch is drawn open, with a lifted line, or closed.

**T7. A map, not a picture** (Sapling and up)
> A circuit diagram shows what joins to what. Wires are straight lines. Slide a symbol along the loop: same circuit.

Worked example: *Moss's tangled bridge wires.*
1. Follow the wire from the cell to the lamp.
2. Then on to the switch, and back to the cell.
3. Draw a square: cell, lamp, switch. Same joins, tidy lines.

**T8. One path** (Seedling and up)
> In a series circuit, everything sits on one path. If one bulb breaks, the path breaks. Every bulb goes dark.

Worked example: *Three lanterns on one path.*
1. All three glow.
2. The middle bulb's filament snaps.
3. Now the path has a gap. All three go dark, even the first.

**T9. Own paths** (Sapling and up)
> In a parallel circuit, each lamp has its own path to the cell. One broken lamp darkens only its own path.

Worked example: *Three lanterns on own paths.*
1. All three glow.
2. The middle bulb's filament snaps.
3. The other two paths are still complete. They keep glowing.

**T10. Rubbing moves charge** (Seedling and up)
> Rub a balloon on hair. Tiny bits of electric charge move from the hair to the balloon. None are made. They just move.

Worked example: *The charge tally.*
1. Before rubbing: balloon and hair are even.
2. One rub: the balloon gains 2 minus marks. The hair is left with 2 plus marks.
3. Charges made: 0. Charges moved: 2.

Sapling line: *The tiny bits are called electrons. Charge that stays put like this is static. Current is charge flowing round a loop.*

**T11. Push or pull** (Seedling and up)
> Opposite charges pull together. Matching charges push apart. Both work across a gap, without touching.

Worked example: *Two tests.*
1. Balloon near hair: the hair lifts toward it. Opposite charges pull.
2. Two rubbed balloons on threads: they swing apart. Matching charges push.
3. Only a push proves two things carry the same charge.

Keeper's Desk line: *A rubbed balloon sticks to a plain wall too. It pushes the wall's nearest electrons back a little, so a pull alone proves nothing.*

**T12. Sparks and lightning** (Seedling and up)
> When enough charge builds up, it can jump across the air. That jump is a spark. Lightning is a giant spark.

Worked example: *Small and huge.*
1. Pip shuffles across the café rug. Charge builds up on him.
2. Reach for a metal handle. Snap: a tiny spark.
3. A storm cloud does the same, enormously. The heated air booms: thunder.

**T13. A magnet you can switch off** (Sapling and up)
> Wrap wire round an iron nail many times. While current flows, the nail is a magnet. Switch off, and it lets go.

Worked example: *Clips in a dish.*
1. Switch on. Clips jump to the nail.
2. Switch off. The clips drop.
3. More turns of wire, or another cell: more clips.

**H1. Volta's pile** (Sapling and up, A true thing from history)
> In 1800, Alessandro Volta stacked zinc and copper discs with salty wet cloth between them. A wire from top to bottom carried a steady current.

Worked example: *Building the pile.*
1. Zinc, wet cloth, copper. Then again, and again.
2. The stack grows into a tall pile.
3. Join top and bottom with a wire: a steady current. The volt is named after him.

**H2. Ørsted's needle** (Keeper's Desk, A true thing from history)
> In 1820, Hans Christian Ørsted saw a compass needle swing when current flowed in a nearby wire. Electricity could make magnetism.

Keeper's Desk line: *It happened during a lecture. Stories differ about whether it was an accident; his own later account says he planned the test. Everyone agrees the needle moved.*

## Activity (a): The Tester

**Instruction:** Pick something. Will the bulb glow? Predict, then test it.

**Prediction choices:** Glow · Stay dark

**Guided first item:**
1. Put the steel nail in the gap. Predict, then test.
2. Now the wooden peg. Predict, then test.
3. Which column does each one go in? *(nail: lets it through; peg: stops it)*

| Object | Result | Feedback |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Steel nail | Glows | Steel is a metal. It lets electricity through. |
| Copper wire | Glows | Copper is what most wires are made of. |
| Kitchen foil | Glows | Thin, but metal. Thin metal still conducts. |
| Brass key | Glows | Brass is a metal, so the path is complete. |
| Coin | Glows | A metal coin completes the path. |
| Pencil graphite | Glows | A surprise! Graphite isn't a metal, but it conducts. With real pencil lead, a bulb glows dimly. |
| Wire, bare ends | Glows | The metal ends touch the clips. |
| Wire, plastic coat | Stays dark | The coat is plastic. It keeps electricity in the metal. |
| Wooden peg | Stays dark | Wood is an insulator. |
| Plastic button | Stays dark | Plastic is an insulator. That's why wires wear it. |
| Rubber band | Stays dark | Rubber stops electricity. |
| Glass marble | Stays dark | Shiny, but glass is an insulator. |
| Paper | Stays dark | Paper is an insulator. |
| Wool thread | Stays dark | Wool won't mend a wire. |
| Bramble's ribbon | Stays dark | Pretty, but cloth stops electricity. |
| Cork | Stays dark | Cork is an insulator. |

**Results table headings:** Lets electricity through · Stops electricity

**Model line:** A made-up model. It shows what real testers do; it doesn't test a real object.

## Activity (b): The Diagram Desk

**Instruction:** Fill the four slots. Can you make the request card work?

**Tiles:** cell · lamp · buzzer · switch (tap to open or close) · wire · gap

**Status lines:**
- *Open path:* "There's a gap or an open switch. The path isn't complete."
- *No push:* "The path is complete, but there's no cell to push."
- *Paused:* "Our simple model does not calculate this arrangement. Add a lamp or buzzer. Real cells joined by bare wire get hot."
- *Works:* "Complete path with a cell. Lamps glow; buzzers buzz."

**Guided first item:** *Make one lamp you can switch off.*
1. Put a cell in the first slot.
2. Put a lamp in the next slot.
3. Put a switch and a wire in the last two. Close the switch.
4. The lamp glows. Now open the switch. *(dark)*

**Picture or diagram? panel:** Here are Moss's tangled wires. Which tidy diagram has the same joins?

**Model line:** A made-up model. It shows on or off only, not how bright or how much current.

## Activity (c): The Lantern String

**Instruction:** Choose one path or own paths. How many lanterns will glow?

**Layout labels:** One path (series) · Own paths (parallel)

**Lantern states (tap to change):** working · dead bulb · missing

**Guided first item:** *Three lanterns on one path.*
1. All three work. Predict, then light the bridge. *(3 glow)*
2. Tap the middle lantern to make its bulb dead. Predict again.
3. Light the bridge. *(0 glow)* "One gap, and the only path is broken."

**Feedback lines:**
- One path, all working: "One complete path through every lantern. All glow."
- One path, any break: "The only path has a gap. Every lantern goes dark."
- Own paths: "Each lantern with a complete path of its own glows. The rest stay dark."

**Model line:** A made-up model: on or off only. Real bulbs on one path share the push: each glows dimmer than one alone, and with many lanterns they may not glow at all.

## Activity (d): The Static Corner

**Instruction:** Rub the balloon. Then bring it close to something.

**Buttons:** Rub · Bring it near Bramble's hair · Near a second balloon · Near paper bits · Near the wall · Touch the door handle

**Guided first item:**
1. Rub three times. Watch the tally. *(balloon 6 minus, hair 6 plus)*
2. Predict: will her hair move toward it, away, or stay?
3. Bring it near. "Her hair lifts toward it. Opposite charges pull."

**Feedback lines:**
- Second balloon: "Both balloons carry the same charge. They push apart."
- Paper bits: "The bits jump up. Charged things can pull plain things too."
- Wall (Keeper's Desk): "It sticks, though the wall isn't charged. A pull alone proves nothing."
- Door handle: "Snap! The extra charge jumps across. That was a tiny spark."

**Tally line:** Charges made: 0. Charges moved: {n}.

**Model line:** A simplified picture. Real balloons move many millions of electrons.

## Activity (e): The Coil and Nail (Sapling and up)

**Instruction:** Choose turns, cells and a core. Switch on. How many clips?

**Settings:** Turns: 10 · 20 · 30 | Cells: 1 · 2 | Core: iron nail · steel bolt · copper rod · no core

**Guided first item:**
1. 10 turns, 1 cell, iron nail. Switch on. *(2 clips)*
2. Switch off. *(all clips drop)* "No current, no magnet."

**Feedback lines:**
- Copper rod or no core: "No clips. Copper isn't magnetic, so it doesn't help."
- Steel bolt, switched off: "One clip stays. Steel keeps a little magnetism. Iron lets go."
- Compass: "The needle swings while current flows. That's what Ørsted saw."
- Change one thing: "Change only one setting. Then you know what made the difference."

**Model line:** A made-up model. Real electromagnets aren't this tidy, and a coil on a real battery gets hot. This one is on screen only.

## Problem sets

### Set 1. Testers and switches (Sprout and Seedling)

**1.1 (Sprout, sort)**
Prompt: Glow or dark? Sort the things for the tester.
Cards: steel nail · wooden peg · copper wire · rubber band · foil · plastic button
Answer: *Glow:* nail, copper wire, foil. *Dark:* peg, rubber band, button.
Tests: thin things can't carry electricity (foil); anything long like a wire works (rubber band).
Hint: Which ones are metal?
Explanation: The metal things let electricity through. The others are insulators.

**1.2 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which one could mend Moss's broken wire?
- A. Bramble's red ribbon *(tests: anything long and thin works like a wire)*
- B. A strip of kitchen foil *(right)*
- C. A piece of wool thread *(tests: it looks like a wire, so it works)*
Hint: What did the spoon have that the string didn't?
Explanation: Foil is metal, so it conducts. Ribbon and wool are insulators.

**1.3 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: The switch is open. What does the lamp do?
- A. It stays dark. There's a gap. *(right)*
- B. It glows. Open means it's on. *(tests: everyday "open")*
- C. It glows a little, halfway on. *(tests: a switch sets how much)*
Hint: Is there a gap in the path?
Explanation: Open means the path is broken, so the lamp is off.

**1.4 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Why does a wire have a plastic coat?
- A. The plastic carries the electricity along. *(tests: the coat conducts)*
- B. The plastic helps electricity go faster. *(tests: insulators change speed)*
- C. The metal carries it; plastic keeps it in. *(right)*
Hint: What happened when the clips touched the coat?
Explanation: Plastic is an insulator. It keeps electricity in the metal and off fingers.

**1.5 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Moss moves the open switch next to the bulb. What now?
- A. Still dark. A gap anywhere breaks the path. *(right)*
- B. It glows. Switches only work by the cell. *(tests: switch position matters)*
- C. It glows dimly, because it's farther away. *(tests: current is used up along the way)*
Hint: Is there still a gap somewhere in the loop?
Explanation: One loop, one gap, wherever it is. The bulb stays off.

**1.6 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Pip says the switch makes the electricity. Is he right?
- A. Yes. Closing it makes a little electricity. *(tests: a switch generates)*
- B. No. The cell pushes. A switch closes a gap. *(right)*
- C. Yes, but only when it sits by the cell. *(tests: switch position matters)*
Hint: What does a switch do to the gap?
Explanation: The cell does the pushing. A switch only completes or breaks the path.

### Set 2. Diagrams and words (Seedling and Sapling)

**2.1 (Seedling, match)**
Prompt: Match each symbol to its name.
Symbols: long and short line · circle with a cross · line lifted between two dots · line flat between two dots · plain straight line
Names: cell · lamp · open switch · closed switch · wire
Answer: in the order given.
Tests: a lamp is drawn as a little bulb picture; open and closed look alike.
Hint: Which one has a gap?
Explanation: The open switch has a gap, the closed switch none. Symbols are short names.

**2.2 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which one is a battery?
- A. A single cell on its own *(tests: one cell is a battery)*
- B. A lamp joined to one cell *(tests: any group of parts is a battery)*
- C. Two cells joined together *(right)*
Hint: How many cells make a battery?
Explanation: A battery is two or more cells joined. One alone is a cell.

**2.3 (Sapling, build on the desk)**
Prompt: Build a loop that lights one lamp. Make it switch off.
Answer: cell, lamp, switch and wire in the four slots, in any order, switch closed to light and open to switch off.
Tests: parts must sit in a set order; a lamp needs a cell right beside it.
Hint: Start with the cell. What else does a complete path need?
Explanation: Any order works. Diagrams care about joins, not places.

**2.4 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Two diagrams: same parts, lamp drawn in a different place. Same circuit?
- A. No. The lamp has moved, so it changed. *(tests: a diagram is a picture)*
- B. Yes. Every part joins the same parts. *(right)*
- C. No. The lamp nearest the cell gets more. *(tests: current is used up)*
Hint: Trace the loop in each one. Same order?
Explanation: The joins match, so it's the same circuit. Position on the page doesn't matter.

**2.5 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Pip's loop has one cell and three wires. What does the desk do?
- A. It pauses. There is no lamp in the loop. *(right)*
- B. It works, with nothing there to light up. *(tests: a complete path means "works")*
- C. It shows an open path with a gap. *(tests: no lamp means a gap)*
Hint: Is there a lamp or buzzer anywhere?
Explanation: The model pauses. Real cells joined by bare wire get hot: a fixed safety fact.

### Set 3. Lantern strings (Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**3.1 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Five lanterns on one path. One bulb breaks. How many glow?
- A. Four. The others don't need that bulb. *(tests: each bulb is separate)*
- B. Two, the ones before the break. *(tests: current reaches early bulbs first)*
- C. None. The only path now has a gap. *(right)*
Hint: Is there still a complete path?
Explanation: On one path, a single break darkens every lantern, even the first.

**3.2 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: Five lanterns on own paths. One bulb breaks. How many glow?
Answer: **4**.
Worked steps: 1. Each lantern has its own path. 2. Only the broken one loses its path. 3. 5 − 1 = 4.
Tests: thinking parallel acts like series (0).
Hint: Which paths are still complete?
Explanation: Own paths mean one broken bulb darkens only itself.

**3.3 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Four lanterns on one path. Moss opens the switch by the last one.
- A. Three glow. Only the last goes dark. *(tests: a switch controls only what's next to it)*
- B. None glow. The whole path is broken. *(right)*
- C. All four glow. It's far from the cell. *(tests: switches only work by the cell)*
Hint: How many paths are there?
Explanation: One path, one gap. Every lantern goes dark.

**3.4 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: Six lanterns, own paths. Switches 2 and 5 open; lantern 3 dead. How many glow?
Answer: **3**.
Worked steps: 1. Start with 6; the main switch is closed. 2. Lanterns 2 and 5 are switched off: 6 − 2 = 4. 3. Lantern 3 is dead: 4 − 1 = 3.
Tests: forgetting the dead bulb (4); treating it as series (0).
Hint: Check each lantern's own path, one at a time.
Explanation: Lanterns 1, 4 and 6 have complete paths. Those three glow.

**3.5 (Keeper's Desk, say why, spoken to a grown-up)**
Prompt: Why might the town want the bridge lanterns on own paths?
Answer to listen for: one dead bulb won't darken the rest; each lantern can be switched alone. A good answer may add a cost: more wire.
Hint: Think back to Lantern Night.
Explanation: Own paths keep the bridge lit when one bulb fails.

### Set 4. Sparks, coils and history (Seedling to Keeper's Desk)

**4.1 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Bramble's hair lifts toward her rubbed balloon. Why?
- A. They now have opposite charges, which pull. *(right)*
- B. Rubbing turned the balloon into a magnet. *(tests: static is magnetism)*
- C. Rubbing made wind that blew her hair up. *(tests: an air push, not a gap force)*
Hint: Where did the tiny bits of charge go?
Explanation: The balloon gained charge; her hair lost it. Opposite charges pull across a gap.

**4.2 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: Each rub moves 2 electrons from hair to balloon. Bramble rubs 3 times.
Answer: balloon **6** extra electrons; hair **6** missing; electrons made: **0**.
Worked steps: 1. 3 rubs × 2 = 6. 2. The hair lost the same 6. 3. 6 gained, 6 lost: none made.
Tests: rubbing makes charge (12 in total); both get the same charge.
Hint: Where did the balloon's electrons come from?
Explanation: Rubbing moves charge. One thing gains exactly what the other loses.

**4.3 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Two rubbed balloons hang side by side. What happens?
- A. They pull together. Charged things always pull. *(tests: all charges attract)*
- B. Nothing, unless they touch each other. *(tests: charge needs touching)*
- C. They swing apart. Matching charges push. *(right)*
Hint: Do they carry the same charge, or opposite?
Explanation: Both gained electrons. Matching charges push apart, across the gap.

**4.4 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice with steps)**
Prompt: The librarian counts 9 seconds from flash to thunder. How far away?
- A. About 9 km, one per second *(tests: sound covers a km each second)*
- B. About 3 km, a km per 3 seconds *(right)*
- C. About 27 km, three per second *(tests: multiplying instead of dividing)*
Worked steps: 1. Sound takes about 3 seconds per km. 2. 9 ÷ 3 = 3. 3. About 3 km.
Hint: How many seconds does sound take to travel 1 km?
Explanation: An estimate only. If you can hear thunder, go indoors.

**4.5 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: 10 turns and 1 cell lift 2 clips. Predict 30 turns and 2 cells.
Answer: **12** clips (six times as many, in this model).
Worked steps: 1. 30 turns is 3 times 10: 2 × 3 = 6. 2. Two cells double it: 6 × 2 = 12.
Tests: adding instead of multiplying (2 + 3 + 2 = 7); counting only the turns (6).
Hint: Change one setting at a time in your head.
Explanation: In this made-up model, turns and cells each multiply the clips. Real magnets are less tidy.

**4.6 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which pair of tests shows whether turns matter?
- A. 10 and 30 turns, both 1 cell, iron *(right)*
- B. 10 turns with 1 cell, 30 turns with 2 *(tests: changing two things at once)*
- C. 10 turns with iron, 30 with copper *(tests: a different core is fair)*
Hint: Which pair changes only one thing?
Explanation: Keep cells and core the same. Then only the turns can make the difference.

**4.7 (Keeper's Desk, true / not true from these cards)**
Prompt: Read H1 and H2. True or not true, from these cards?
1. Volta's pile used zinc and copper discs with salty wet cloth. *(True)*
2. Ørsted saw a compass needle swing near a wire with current. *(True)*
3. The cards say Ørsted's discovery was an accident. *(Not true: the card says stories differ)*
4. How many years from Volta's pile to Ørsted's needle? *(1820 − 1800 = **20**)*
Tests: treating a disputed story as settled; reading more than the card says.
Hint: Look for the words "stories differ".
Explanation: The cards agree on what was seen. How it happened is less certain.

## "I'm stuck" worked examples

- **Tester:** Easier case: one nail in the gap glows. *Start this one:* ask "is it metal?"
- **Switches:** Easier case: one switch, one loop, switch open, dark. *Start this one:* look for any gap.
- **Diagrams:** Easier case: cell, lamp, wire, wire, all joined. *Start this one:* trace the loop with a finger.
- **Lantern strings:** Easier case: two lanterns on one path, one breaks, none glow. *Start this one:* count the paths first.
- **Static:** Easier case: one rub moves 2 bits; balloon minus 2, hair plus 2. *Start this one:* say who gained.
- **Coil:** Easier case: 20 turns, 1 cell, iron: 4 clips. *Start this one:* change one setting at a time.
- **History:** Easier case: "Volta used zinc." The card says so: true. *Start this one:* find the sentence on the card.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Circuit:** a complete path from one end of a cell, through parts, back to the other end.
- **Component:** any part of a circuit, including the cell and the wires.
- **Lamp:** the circuit-diagram word for a bulb.
- **Cell:** one unit that pushes electricity round a circuit.
- **Battery:** two or more cells joined together.
- **Conductor:** a material that lets electricity through, like most metals.
- **Insulator:** a material that stops electricity, like wood, plastic or rubber.
- **Switch:** a gap in the path that you can close or open.
- **Open:** a switch with a gap. Off. **Closed:** no gap. On.
- **Circuit diagram:** a map of what joins to what, drawn with symbols.
- **Series:** everything on one path. **Parallel:** each lamp on its own branch of the path.
- **Current:** the flow round the loop. **Voltage:** the push from the cells. (Exact meanings come later.)
- **Static electricity:** charge that has built up and stays put.
- **Electron:** a tiny bit of negative charge that can move.
- **Spark:** charge jumping across the air.
- **Lightning:** a giant spark from a storm cloud.
- **Electromagnet:** a coil of wire that is a magnet only while current flows.
- **Turns:** the loops of wire wound round one coil.

## Off-screen invitations (optional, grown-up-led)

- **Balloon and paper.** A grown-up blows up the balloon. Rub it on a wool sweater. Hold it over tiny paper scraps. Then hang two rubbed balloons on threads, side by side.
- **Tester kit.** With a grown-up and a store-bought battery-and-bulb kit, make a tester with a gap. Try a spoon, a pencil lead, a peg and an eraser.
- **Thunder count.** In a storm, from indoors, count from flash to thunder with a grown-up. Divide by 3 for kilometers. The count is an estimate, never a safety tool.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** conductors and insulators; switches as gaps; circuit symbols and diagrams; series and parallel on and off; static as moved charge, sparks and lightning; at older depth, electromagnets and two history cards. It builds on the Lantern Circuit Bench and does not repeat it.

**What it does not claim:** every bench is a made-up model. None shows brightness, current values or heat, and none tests a real object. Series and parallel are on and off only. Fields, Ohm's law and motors are left to later work.

**Real history, briefly:** Volta's letter of 20 March 1800 describing his pile; Ørsted's compass needle, 1820, with the disputed story labeled. Sources are in the source audit.

**Safety rule (fixed, shown word for word):** Wall sockets, plugs and anything that plugs in are never for experiments. Never go near a fallen wire outdoors; tell a grown-up. Experiment only with AA or AAA cells, and only with a grown-up. No number or model changes this rule. Treat every fallen or storm-damaged outdoor wire as live; keep well back and call the power company or emergency services. Off-screen work uses only AA or AAA cells in a store-bought kit, with a grown-up leading. Never join a battery's two ends with only a wire: it heats quickly. Stop if anything gets warm. Keep button and coin cells away from small children; swallowed, they cause serious burns, so seek urgent care at once. Keep water away from every circuit. The coil-and-nail stays on screen. Balloons: a grown-up blows them up and ties them; uninflated balloons and burst pieces are a choking and suffocation hazard for young children, so collect pieces at once. In storms: if you can hear thunder, go indoors, and wait 30 minutes after the last thunder. Nothing from these activities is recorded in the game.
