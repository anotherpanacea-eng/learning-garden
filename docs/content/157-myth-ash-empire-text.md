## Teach cards

**Teach 1. What kind of thing is it?** *(A true thing from history)*
> Before you trust something, ask what kind of thing it is. A story? Something found in the ground? Something written or made much later?

Worked example, one step per tap:
1. Twins raised by a wolf. That is a story people told.
2. Holes for hut posts on the Palatine Hill. Those were found in the ground.
3. A Roman scholar gave the story a date, 753 BCE. That was written much later.
4. A bronze wolf statue. Tests suggest it was made much later than the story.

**Teach 2. What an object can and can't tell** *(A true thing from history)*
> An object from the past tells us some things. It leaves other things out. Say both.

Worked example:
1. What you see: a burnt round loaf from Pompeii.
2. What it might tell us: people there baked bread like this.
3. What it can't tell us: what it tasted like, or whether anyone liked it.

**Teach 3. Words have families** *(A true thing from history)*
> Many words grew from one older word. A shared root often shows in the spelling, even after centuries.

Worked example:
1. Latin *aqua* means water.
2. Spanish *agua*. French *eau*. English *aquarium*.
3. All grew from *aqua*.
4. English *water* means the same, but grew from a different root.

## Station 1. Story or ground?

**The story, short version** *(A story people told)*
> Twin babies were left by a river. A wolf cared for them. A shepherd raised them. They built a city. The brothers quarreled. Romulus named the city Rome.

**Keeper's note** *(A story people told)*
> In most versions of the legend, Remus dies in the quarrel. Roman writers such as Livy told the story centuries after the date it gives.

**The huts** *(A true thing from history)*
> On the Palatine Hill, archaeologists found holes where wooden posts held up huts. People lived in a hut village there for centuries, from about 1000 to 700 BCE.

**The bronze wolf, two ages** *(A true thing from history)*
> Front: For a long time, experts thought this wolf was made about 2,500 years ago.
> Flip: Tests on its clay core point to about 1021 to 1153 CE. The twins were added in the late 1400s. Some experts still disagree.

**Per-card feedback**
- Twins left by a river, or the wolf: "Yes, a story people told."
- Hut holes: "Found in the ground. This is evidence about early Rome."
- Varro's date: "Written later. A date can belong to a story."
- The bronze wolf: "Made much later. The story is older than the statue."
- Any card in the wrong tray: "Look again. Ask: did this happen in a story, or was it found or made?"

## Station 3. The Temple on the Rock

**Card** *(A true thing from history)*
> The Parthenon is a temple on a rocky hill, the Acropolis, in Athens. It was built for the goddess Athena, from 447 to 432 BCE.

**Counting columns:** "Tap each column to count it." At the end: "46 columns. The corner ones count once."

**Inside** *(A true thing from history)*
> Inside stood a giant statue of Athena, made of gold and ivory over wood. With its base, it was about 12 meters tall. It is lost.

**When new** *(A picture of what experts think)*
> Its carvings were painted. Scientists have found traces of blue and purple on them. The white you see is what time left. Some colors are guesses.

**A long life** *(A true thing from history)*
> First a temple. Later a church, then a mosque. In 1687, during a siege, gunpowder stored inside exploded. Now it is a ruin people care for.

**Keeper's note** *(A true thing from history)*
> Some of the Parthenon's carvings are in the Acropolis Museum in Athens and some in the British Museum in London. People disagree about where they belong.

## Station 4. What the Ash Kept

**Card 1, all depths** *(A true thing from history)*
> In 79 CE, the volcano Vesuvius in Italy erupted. Ash and light stones called pumice covered the Roman town of Pompeii, several meters deep.

**Card 2, all depths** *(A true thing from history)*
> The ash kept the town as it was: streets, walls, ovens, even bread. People began digging it out in 1748.

**Keeper's note** *(A true thing from history)*
> Many people fled; many others died. What survived lets us see an ordinary Roman town on an ordinary day.

**Objects (What you see / What it might tell us / What it can't tell us)**

1. **A loaf.** See: a round loaf, burnt black, its top marked into segments. Might tell us: (a) people baked bread like this **(right)** · (b) Romans ate only bread · (c) it was baked for a party. Can't tell us: what it tasted like, or who was going to eat it.
2. **The oven.** See: 81 loaves in one bakery oven. Might tell us: (a) a family's lunch · (b) a bakery baking lots, probably to sell **(right)** · (c) bread was free. Can't tell us: how much a loaf cost.
3. **A painted notice.** See: a painted wall asking people to vote for a man. Might tell us: (a) he won · (b) he was a painter · (c) someone backed him in a town election **(right)**. Can't tell us: who won.
4. **The amphitheater.** See: a big oval of stone seats, built about 70 BCE. Might tell us: (a) it was a market · (b) people gathered here to watch shows **(right)** · (c) everyone went every day. Can't tell us: what each person in the seats thought.

**Gladiators card** *(A true thing from history)*
> Gladiators fought in arenas like this one. Most were enslaved people, prisoners of war or condemned criminals. A few free men signed up.

**Colosseum line** *(A true thing from history)*
> Rome's biggest arena, the Colosseum, opened in 80 CE. Tens of thousands could watch.

**Keeper's Desk: the date puzzle** *(A true thing from history)*
> For centuries, books said the eruption began on 24 August. That date comes from copies of letters by Pliny the Younger, an eyewitness who wrote years later.
>
> In 2018, diggers found a charcoal note dated 17 October. If written in 79 CE, the eruption came later, maybe 24 October.
>
> Then the park wrote its own charcoal note on the same wall. Ten months later it was still clear. So the old note might be a year older. The question is still open.

## Station 6. The Thinkers' Bench (older depth)

**Socrates** *(A true thing from history)*
> Socrates taught by asking questions. He wrote nothing down. We know him through others, above all his student Plato. Plato later started a school, the Academy, where Aristotle studied.

**Aristotle** *(A true thing from history)*
> Aristotle watched animals closely, including sea creatures in a lagoon on the island of Lesbos. He sorted them into groups and wrote it all down.

**Hippocrates** *(A true thing from history)*
> Hippocrates, from the island of Kos, taught that illness has natural causes, such as food, air and surroundings. Many people then blamed the gods.

**Flip: right and wrong** *(A true thing from history)*
> Right: look for natural causes, and watch carefully. Wrong: one Hippocratic book said health means balancing four body fluids. Good methods can still give wrong answers.

## Station 7. The Slow Ending

**Card** *(A true thing from history)*
> In 476 CE, a general named Odoacer removed the last emperor in the west. He was a boy named Romulus Augustulus.

**One day or slow change?** *(A true thing from history)*
> 476 is the date people use. But change came over many years: attacks on the borders, fights over who ruled, money trouble, and an empire too big to hold.

**The east** *(A true thing from history)*
> The eastern half, ruled from Constantinople, went on for nearly a thousand years, until 1453.

**Two historians** *(A true thing from history)*
> Some historians say Rome fell: towns shrank, and trade and building dropped. Others say Rome changed into something new. Both point to evidence.

**Christianity card** *(A true thing from history)*
> Christianity spread through the empire over centuries. In 313 CE, two emperors agreed that Christians could worship freely. In 380 CE, it became the empire's official religion.

**Keeper's note** *(A true thing from history)*
> Few people at the time seem to have treated 476 as the end of anything. An eighteenth-century historian, Edward Gibbon, helped make it the famous date.

## Glossary

- **Legend:** an old story people told, which may or may not hold some truth.
- **Myth:** a story people told about gods and heroes.
- **Archaeologist:** someone who studies the past from things found in the ground.
- **Temple:** a building made to honor a god.
- **Acropolis:** the rocky hill in Athens where the Parthenon stands.
- **Ash and pumice:** tiny bits of rock and light, holey stones thrown out by a volcano.
- **Latin:** the language of the Romans.
- **Root:** an older word that newer words grew from.
- **Romance language:** a language that grew from Latin, like Spanish or French.
- **Philosopher:** someone who asks big questions and tests the answers.
- **Empire:** many lands ruled by one ruler.
- **Historian:** someone who studies the past from evidence and explains it.
- **BCE and CE:** years before and after year 1. There is no year zero.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** to ask what kind of source something is; Greek and Roman gods and how they were linked; the Parthenon and its long life; Pompeii as a record of ordinary life, and what objects cannot tell; Latin roots in Spanish, French and English; Greek thinkers' method and its limits; and the western empire's slow, debated end.

**What it does not claim:** it does not teach the Roman Republic, Sparta or a full myth retelling. "About" dates are uncertain, and the eruption's month is still argued. Willowbank is fiction; the languages are real.

**Handled with care:** Pompeii is taught through bread, walls and notices. The only sentence about deaths ("Many people fled; many others died") is in an older-depth note. There are no casts, bodies or victim images. Gladiators get two factual sentences and no fight images. Christianity and Greek and Roman religion are described factually, without judging any belief.

**Sources in brief:** the Acropolis Museum, King's College London and the British Museum (Parthenon), the Pompeii Archaeological Park, Britannica and Etymonline, World History Encyclopedia, and others in the source audit.
