Case ID `seed-exchange`. Appears with spec 120's Act III chapter *The seed exchange*.

## 1. Why this case

Act III is about exchange, and spec 120 gives it a real trade: Vadoalto's reed for Willowbank's apples, at "2 bundles of reed for 3 baskets". Sapling's job in that chapter scales the rate once. The desk takes the same ledger further: scaling in both directions, sharing a joint harvest in a stated ratio, and then a question where the arithmetic is easy and the judgment is hard: two towns' planting results, with very different sample sizes, that look as if they settle something and don't.

## 4. Step 1: How many bundles for how many baskets?

**Question panel:**

> The agreed rate, in Nuria's handwriting: **2 bundles of reed for every 3 baskets of apples.**
>
> | Baskets of apples | 3 | 9 | 12 | ? |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | Bundles of reed | 2 | ? | ? | 10 |

**Learn card:**

> "For every" means groups. Every time 3 baskets go up the river, 2 bundles come down.
>
> To trade 6 baskets, that's two groups of 3, so two groups of 2: 4 bundles. You multiply both rows by the same number.
>
> | Baskets | 3 | 6 |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | Bundles | 2 | 4 |
> | | | × 2 for both |
>
> Adding the same number to both rows doesn't work. 3 baskets and 2 bundles, plus 3 each, gives 6 baskets for 5 bundles, which is a different deal.

**Guided first task (1a):** "How many bundles for 9 baskets?" Working lines shown unless folded: `9 ÷ 3 = ? groups`, `? groups × 2 = ? bundles`.

Tasks (Check my working for each):

- **1a.** 9 baskets. *Answer: 9 is 3 groups of 3, so 3 × 2 = **6 bundles**.*
- **1b.** 12 baskets. *Answer: 4 groups, **8 bundles**.*
- **1c.** Vadoalto sends 10 bundles. How many baskets go back? *Answer: 10 is 5 groups of 2, so 5 × 3 = **15 baskets**.*
- **1d.** The orchard keeper has 7 baskets left. "Bundles come tied. Vadoalto won't split one. What's the most I can trade with nothing left over?" *Answer: **6 baskets for 4 bundles**; 1 basket stays home.*
- **1e.** Bramble reads the rate upside down: *"So a bundle of reed is worth less than a basket of apples."* Choices:
  - "Yes. 2 is less than 3, so reed is worth less."
  - "No. Fewer bundles buy the same apples, so a bundle is worth more: one and a half baskets." ← fits the rate
  - "We can't tell anything about worth from a trade."

  Reply to the third choice: "A trade can't tell you what anything is *really* worth, but it does say what the two towns agreed. Here, 2 bundles match 3 baskets, so how many baskets match 1 bundle?"

**I'm stuck (1a):**

1. *Hint:* "How many groups of 3 baskets are in 9 baskets?"
2. *Similar example:* "For 6 baskets: 6 is 2 groups of 3. Each group gets 2 bundles. 2 × 2 = 4 bundles. Now try 9: how many groups of 3?"
3. **Skip for now.**

**Replies to likely errors** (Marble research for Ratio Problems and Proportion: additive scaling, scaling one quantity only, and the wrong direction):

| Entered | Misconception | Reply |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 8 for 1a | Adding the same amount to both rows (3 + 6 = 9, so 2 + 6 = 8) | "Adding 6 to both changes the deal. 9 baskets is 3 groups of 3. How many bundles is 3 groups of 2?" |
| 27 or 18 for 1a | Multiplying only one row, or by the wrong number | "Each group of 3 baskets gets 2 bundles. How many groups of 3 are in 9?" |
| 13.5 for 1a | Using the rate the wrong way round (9 × 3 ÷ 2) | "That's more bundles than baskets. The rate always gives fewer bundles than baskets: 2 for every 3." |
| 7 for 1c | Adding 5 to both | "10 bundles is 5 groups of 2 bundles. Each group goes with 3 baskets." |

## 5. Step 2: Sharing the nursery

**Question panel:**

> The two towns started a seedling nursery together at the mill: young apple trees for Vadoalto's new orchard and for Willowbank's own. Vadoalto planted **5 trays** and Willowbank planted **3 trays**, so they agreed to share the seedlings in the same ratio.
>
> **Vadoalto : Willowbank = 5 : 3**
>
> This spring the nursery grew **40 seedlings**.

**Learn card** (bar model first, as the Marble research for Dividing Quantities by Ratio recommends):

> Draw the share as a bar of equal blocks: 5 blocks for Vadoalto, 3 for Willowbank. That's **8 blocks** in the whole bar, not 5 and not 3.
>
> ```
> Vadoalto    [ ][ ][ ][ ][ ]
> Willowbank  [ ][ ][ ]
>             8 blocks share 40 seedlings
> ```
>
> Share the total between all 8 blocks first. Then each town takes its blocks.
>
> Check: the two shares must add back to the total.

**Guided first task (2a):** `5 + 3 = ? blocks`, `40 ÷ ? = ? seedlings in each block`, `Vadoalto: 5 × ?`, `Willowbank: 3 × ?`.

Tasks:

- **2a.** How many seedlings for each town? *Answer: 8 blocks; 40 ÷ 8 = 5 each; Vadoalto 5 × 5 = **25**, Willowbank 3 × 5 = **15**. Check: 25 + 15 = 40.*
- **2b.** What fraction of the seedlings does Willowbank get? *Answer: **3/8** (15 of 40). As a percentage, 37.5%, which is optional.*
- **2c.** Next spring, a note says Vadoalto received **35** seedlings. How many did the nursery grow in all, and how many went to Willowbank? *Answer: 35 ÷ 5 = 7 per block; 8 × 7 = **56** in all; Willowbank 3 × 7 = **21**. Check: 35 + 21 = 56.*
- **2d.** Wren asks: "Is 5 : 3 fair?" Not marked. Choices, each with its own reply:
  - "Yes. Vadoalto planted more trays, so it should get more trees."
  - "Not quite. Willowbank waters them every day, and that counts too."
  - "It's fair if both towns agreed to it before they started."

  The same closing line follows each: "A ratio can't decide what's fair. People decide, and then the ratio keeps the promise exactly."

**I'm stuck (2a):**

1. *Hint:* "How many blocks are in the whole bar?"
2. *Similar example:* "Share 24 shells 1 : 2. That's 3 blocks. 24 ÷ 3 = 8 in each block. One person gets 1 × 8 = 8, the other 2 × 8 = 16. Check: 8 + 16 = 24. Now: how many blocks does 5 : 3 make?"
3. **Skip for now.**

**Replies to likely errors:**

| Entered | Misconception | Reply |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 8 for Vadoalto (40 ÷ 5) | Dividing by one part instead of all the parts | "That's 40 shared between 5 blocks. The bar has 5 blocks *and* 3 more. How many altogether?" |
| 20 and 20 | Sharing equally between the two towns | "Equal shares would be 1 : 1. Vadoalto's part of the bar is longer." |
| 3/5 for 2b | Part-to-part read as part-to-whole | "3 : 5 compares Willowbank with Vadoalto. A fraction of all the seedlings compares Willowbank with all 8 blocks." |
| 35 + 3 = 38 for 2c | Adding instead of finding one block | "35 is Vadoalto's 5 blocks. What's in one block?" |

## 6. Step 3: Whose reed did better?

**Question panel:**

> Last spring, both towns planted reed cuttings from the same Vadoalto beds.
>
> | Where | Cuttings planted | Took root |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | Willowbank, by the mill pond | 40 | 30 |
> | Vadoalto, by the river | 25 | 20 |
>
> Nuria's note says: *Ours did better, though.*
>
> The orchard keeper says: "Thirty is more than twenty. Ours did better."

**Learn card** (Marble research for Understanding and Calculating Percentages: always ask "percent of what?"):

> When two groups are different sizes, counts can't be compared directly. Willowbank planted more, so of course more took root.
>
> A **percentage** turns each result into "out of 100", so both are measured the same way.
>
> 30 out of 40: each cutting is 100 ÷ 40 = 2.5 percent. 30 × 2.5 = 75%.
> Or: 30/40 = 3/4, and 3/4 is 75 out of 100.
>
> Two bars of the same length, one for each town, each split into its own cuttings: Willowbank's bar has 40 thin blocks, Vadoalto's has 25 wider ones. Shade the ones that took root.

**Guided first task (3a):** `30 ÷ 40 = ?` as a fraction (`3/4`), then `3/4 = ?/100`.

Tasks:

- **3a.** What percentage took root at Willowbank? *Answer: 30/40 = 3/4 = **75%**.*
- **3b.** At Vadoalto? *Answer: 20/25 = 4/5 = **80%**.*
- **3c.** "So Nuria's right?" Choices:
  - "Yes. 80% is more than 75%, so Vadoalto's ground grows reed better."
  - "Too close to call. Change one Vadoalto cutting and its result moves 4 points." ← fits the evidence
  - "No. Thirty cuttings took root at Willowbank and only twenty at Vadoalto."

  Reply to the first: "80 is more than 75. Before deciding it means something, try 3d: how much does one cutting change each result?" Reply to the third: "Willowbank planted 15 more cuttings, so it had more chances. Percentages compare the two fairly."
- **3d.** Use the "what if" table: if **one more** Vadoalto cutting had failed, what would its percentage be? And if one more Willowbank cutting had failed? *Answers: Vadoalto 19/25 = **76%**; Willowbank 29/40 = **72.5%**.* The table shows each town's result for one cutting either way: Vadoalto 76% / 80% / 84%, Willowbank 72.5% / 75% / 77.5%.
- **3e.** "What else was different between the two plantings, besides the town?" Free choice of any, each with an authored reply; none is marked wrong:
  - Willowbank's cuttings travelled a day down the river first; Vadoalto's were planted fresh.
  - They were planted by different people, maybe in different weeks.
  - The mill pond and Vadoalto's river bank have different soil and water.
  - Nothing else was different. *Reply: "Read the panel again. Both came from the same beds, but where did each set spend the day before planting?"*

**I'm stuck (3a):**

1. *Hint:* "Write it as a fraction first: how many took root, out of how many planted?"
2. *Similar example:* "12 out of 16 seeds sprouted. 12/16 = 3/4, and 3/4 = 75/100 = 75%. Now: 30 out of 40 is what fraction?"
3. **Skip for now.**

**Replies to likely errors:**

| Entered | Misconception | Reply |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 30% for 3a | Reading the count as the percentage | "30 is how many cuttings took root, out of 40. Percent means out of 100." |
| 10% for 3a | Using the number that failed | "10 cuttings didn't take. The question asks about the ones that did." |
| 7.5% for 3a | Decimal slip: 0.75 read as 7.5 | "30/40 is 0.75. To make a percentage, that's 75 out of 100, not 7.5." |
| 20% for 3b | Reading the count as the percentage | "20 took root out of 25 planted. What is that out of 100?" |

## 7. Step 4: A fair trial for next year

**Question panel:**

> Nuria wants to try again next year, "and do it properly this time". Wren wants one page to send up the river with {courier}.
>
> What you've kept so far: *[generated from kept answers, or "not yet" for each open step]*

The plan has four parts. Each has **Write it yourself** (free text) and **Choose and adapt**.

1. **What are we trying to find out?** Supplied: "Does reed take root better at Vadoalto or at Willowbank?"; "Does a day on the river hurt reed cuttings?"; "Does reed grow better in wet ground or very wet ground?" The explorer can write their own.
2. **What will both towns keep the same?** Choose at least three, and say why for each; supplied reasons appear beside each choice. Candidates: cuttings from the same bed on the same day; the same number of cuttings in each town; the same planting week; how deep they are planted; nobody travels with cuttings for one town only; how and when we count. (The Marble research for Controlling variables: name several controls and justify each.)
3. **How many cuttings?** Choices: 25 each; 40 each; 100 each. The replies show what one cutting is worth at each size (4, 2.5 and 1 percentage points) and what each costs Vadoalto's beds. None is marked wrong; the reply to 100 notes it's the clearest test and the most reed to spare.
4. **What will this still not tell us?** Choose or write. Supplied: "One year might be unusually wet or dry."; "Two places can't tell us about every place."; "It tells us about reed, not about apples."

**Keep this plan** saves it. **Send it to Nuria** shows the page as she'd read it.

**Nuria's reply (the same for every plan):**

> *Got it. My abuela read it twice, which she never does. We'll cut the reed on the same morning. Same time next year? Nuria*
>
> *¡Recibido! Mi abuela lo leyó dos veces, y eso no lo hace nunca. Cortaremos el carrizo la misma mañana. ¿Hasta el año que viene? Nuria*

**Supported route:** every part can be finished with Choose and adapt alone, and steps 1 to 3 can stay open. The same reply follows.
