## 1. Why this case

This case is the **Keeper's Desk depth** of the Valencia archive pack that spec 120 outlines (companion text, section 10, *The River That Moved / El río que se mudó*). The archive pack keeps its Sprout, Seedling and Sapling jobs. This file writes the desk job in full and follows that outline's timeline, slogan and care rules.

Spec 120's Act IV puts three plans for the low fields in front of the town: **the Wall**, **the Sponge** and **Some of Each**. Valencia gives the explorer a real city that chose a fourth answer: after the flood of 14 October 1957 it **moved its river**. It dug a new channel south of the city and later turned the dry old riverbed into a park about 9 km long. The case asks what that choice protected, what it cost and who decided, and what Willowbank can and can't learn from it.

It proposes substantial Spanish reading alongside the River Market proposal; no shipping order or first-built claim is implied. Every reading panel offers **English**, **Spanish** or **both side by side**, at three amounts, chosen separately from math depth (the same pattern as Nuria's letters in spec 120 and the French Paris visits).

**Story placement:** Act IV, after the fourth parcel page (the Sponge drained in year 22), and before the town meeting about the low fields. Spec 120 also lists the Valencia pack for Act V. If spec 120 moves it, the hook below is the only line that changes.

## 4. Step 1: What happened in October 1957?

### 4.1 Reading panel

**English, short**

> In October 1957 there was very heavy rain upstream of Valencia, in Spain. On 14 October the River Turia burst its banks. Water ran through the city streets. It came twice: once in the early morning, and again around midday. The second wave was larger. Many people died, and thousands lost their homes or work.

**Spanish, short (corto)**

> En octubre de 1957 llovió muchísimo río arriba de Valencia. El 14 de octubre, el río Turia se desbordó. El agua corrió por las calles de la ciudad. Llegó dos veces: una de madrugada y otra hacia el mediodía. La segunda crecida fue mayor. Murieron muchas personas, y miles perdieron su casa o su trabajo.

**English, medium**

> In October 1957, storms dropped very heavy rain on the hills and valleys upstream of Valencia. Early on 14 October the River Turia burst its banks where it ran through the city. Around midday a second, even larger wave of water arrived. Large parts of the city were flooded. The official count was 81 people dead; some historians think the real number was higher.
>
> Afterwards, the government decided to move the river away from the city. The project was called the **Plan Sur** (the South Plan): a new channel, about 12 kilometres long, that carries the Turia round the south of Valencia to the sea.

**Spanish, medium (medio)**

> En octubre de 1957, unas tormentas descargaron lluvias muy fuertes en los montes y valles río arriba de Valencia. En la madrugada del 14 de octubre, el río Turia se desbordó a su paso por la ciudad. Hacia el mediodía llegó una segunda crecida, todavía mayor. El agua inundó gran parte de la ciudad. La cifra oficial fue de 81 muertos, pero algunos historiadores creen que fueron más.
>
> Después, el gobierno decidió alejar el río de la ciudad. El proyecto se llamó **Plan Sur**: un cauce nuevo, de unos 12 kilómetros, que lleva el Turia por el sur de Valencia hasta el mar.

**English, long** adds two paragraphs:

> The old riverbed, now dry, ran right through the middle of the city. The government planned to build a motorway along it. Many people in Valencia disagreed. They campaigned with a slogan in Valencian, the language many people in Valencia speak alongside Spanish: *El riu es nostre i el volem verd*, "The river is ours, and we want it green." At the end of the 1970s the city decided that the old riverbed would become a park. The first parts opened in the 1980s. Today the Turia Garden runs for about 9 kilometres, with paths, trees, playgrounds and sports fields where the river used to be.
>
> The new channel protected the city, but it had costs too. It was dug through farmland, the *huerta*, and it left some neighbourhoods and villages on the far side of a wide concrete channel from the rest of the city.

**Spanish, long (largo)** adds:

> El antiguo cauce, ya seco, atravesaba el centro de la ciudad. El gobierno quería construir allí una autopista. Mucha gente en Valencia no estaba de acuerdo. Hicieron campaña con un lema en valenciano, la lengua que mucha gente en Valencia habla además del español: *El riu es nostre i el volem verd*, «El río es nuestro y lo queremos verde». A finales de los años setenta, la ciudad decidió que el antiguo cauce sería un parque. Las primeras partes se abrieron en los años ochenta. Hoy el Jardín del Turia mide unos 9 kilómetros, con caminos, árboles, parques infantiles y campos de deporte donde antes pasaba el río.
>
> El nuevo cauce protegió la ciudad, pero también tuvo costes. Se excavó a través de la huerta, y dejó algunos barrios y pueblos al otro lado de un ancho cauce de hormigón, separados del resto de la ciudad.

## 5. Step 2: The numbers that disagree

**Question panel:**

> Nuria's folder has several sources, and they don't all give the same numbers. Hollis would have said: before you decide someone is wrong, check what each one is measuring.

| Question | What the sources say |
| --- | --- |
| How much water at the peak? | About **3,700** m³ each second (a Spanish newspaper's account, and an encyclopedia). About **3,600** (another newspaper). |
| How many people died? | **81** (the official count). Some later historians and journalists suggest the real number was higher, with estimates up to about **300**. |
| How wide is the new channel? | **175 m** (a water-industry news site). **200 m** (the width specified in Article 2 of the 1961 law; a design dimension). **250 m at the base** (another newspaper). |
| When was the new channel finished? | **1969** (officially opened 22 December 1969). **1973** (when the works ended). |
| When did the city decide on a park? | **1978** (one history). **1979** (several others). |
| When did the garden open? | **1986** (one source). **1987** (another). |

Tasks:

- **2a.** For each row, choose what best explains the difference. Cards: *rounded or estimated differently*; *measuring different things or places*; *a different event or date counted as "finished"*; *someone counted, someone estimated*; *we can't tell from these sources*. More than one may fit. Authored readings, shown after choosing, not marked:
  - Peak flow: 100 m³/s is less than 3% of either figure. That arithmetic does not establish a measured uncertainty interval. Different estimates or rounding are possibilities; check how each source obtained its figure.
  - Width: design width, built width and base width could refer to different things. The law specifies a 200 m design width; the other reports' definitions and measurement locations still need checking. Do not assert that this resolves the discrepancy.
  - Finished: opening and completion of all works can be different events. Verify what the 1969 and 1973 sources actually date before calling the reports compatible.
  - Park decision: we can't tell from these sources. A council vote, a plan and an announcement can each be called "the decision". Step 4c shows why the exact date matters.
  - Garden: different sections or opening milestones could explain different dates. Check the cited histories rather than assume this is the explanation.
  - Deaths: an official count and later estimates can differ for several reasons. See 2b.
- **2b.** "Why might the official count (81) and later estimates differ?" Choose any; each has a reply; none is marked wrong:
  - Some people were missing and never officially counted. *(Reply: "Possible. Missing people are hard to count after a flood.")*
  - Some deaths came later, from injuries or illness. *(Reply: "Possible. It depends when the counting stopped.")*
  - Newspapers then were controlled by the government. *(Reply: "True: Spain was a dictatorship under Francisco Franco, and the press was censored. That makes an official count worth checking. It doesn't by itself prove the count was wrong.")*
  - The later estimates are just guesses, so ignore them. *(Reply: "They may be estimates. That's a reason to ask how they were made, not a reason to ignore them.")*
- **2c.** "What can this table establish about the differing reports?" Choices: every pair is compatible; every pair is a contradiction; we need the underlying definitions and evidence before deciding ← fits. Reply: "Different numbers are a reason to investigate. Some might name different things, while some might conflict or be mistaken. This table has not established which. The death count especially calls for better evidence."

**Learn card:**

> Two numbers for the same thing aren't automatically a contradiction. Ask three questions. Are they measuring the same thing, in the same place? Is one a count and the other an estimate? Do they mean the same moment by "finished" or "opened"? Then ask what precision or uncertainty each source allows. Two exact incompatible claims about the same thing cannot both be true; rounded or estimated figures need their own evidence before we can decide.

This is spec 118's Case 1 distinction (disagreement against contradiction), now on real sources.

## 6. Step 3: How big is 3,700?

**Question panel:**

> Engineers measure a river's flow in **cubic metres per second**: how many one-metre cubes of water pass a point each second. At the peak of the second wave the Turia carried about **3,700 m³ every second**.

Tasks:

- **3a. Unit rate first.** One cubic metre is 1,000 litres. How many litres passed each second at the peak? *3,700 × 1,000 = **3,700,000** litres.* A double number line (m³ above, litres below) is the learn card's model.
- **3b.** A swimming pool 25 m long, 10 m wide and 2 m deep holds how many cubic metres? *25 × 10 × 2 = **500** m³.* How long would the peak flow take to fill it? *500 ÷ 3,700 ≈ **0.14** seconds, less than a fifth of a second.* The guided line: "Is the answer more or less than one second? 3,700 m³ arrive each second, and the pool needs only 500."
- **3c.** If the peak had lasted a whole hour (it didn't), how much water? *3,700 × 3,600 = **13,320,000** m³.* Reply to 3,700 × 60 = 222,000: "That's one minute's worth. An hour has 60 minutes of 60 seconds."
- **3d. Percent of what?** The new channel was designed to carry **5,000 m³/s**. What percentage of that was the 1957 peak? *3,700 ÷ 5,000 = 0.74 = **74%**.* With the other source's 3,600? ***72%***. Learn card builds it from benchmarks: 10% of 5,000 is 500, so 70% is 3,500; 1% is 50, so 4% is 200; 3,500 + 200 = 3,700.
- **3e.** "So the new channel could carry the 1957 flood with room to spare. Does that mean Valencia can never flood again?" Choices: Yes, the channel is bigger than the flood; No, a bigger flood is possible, and rain can also fall on streams that don't run into the new channel ← fits; We can't know anything about future floods. Reply to the first: "It could carry a 1957-sized flood. A design size is a choice about how big a flood to plan for."

**Tagged misconceptions (Marble research: Compound Units; Calculating Percentages):**

| Wrong entry | Misconception | Reply |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0.0037 for 3a | Dividing by 1,000 instead of multiplying | "A litre is smaller than a cubic metre, so there should be more litres, not fewer." |
| 7.4 or 7.4% for 3d | Misplacing the decimal | "0.74 is 74 out of 100. Check: is 3,700 most of 5,000, or a tiny part?" |
| 135% for 3d (5,000 ÷ 3,700) | Dividing the wrong way round | "Which is the part and which is the whole? The flood is the part of the channel's capacity it used." |

## 7. Step 4: What did the law assign, who decided, who was left out?

**Question panel:**

> The Plan Sur became law on 23 December 1961. A Spanish law is a public document, so the folder includes a few lines from it.

Primary-source note, 1961 preamble (the assigned hydraulic-works shares are confirmed in the BOE original; final Spanish transcription/context and teaching review remain required):

> The 1961 preamble assigns 75% of the hydraulic-works cost to the State, 20% to the city council and 5% to the provincial council. Use the verified original Spanish wording in the final reviewed source card; this paraphrase replaces the unverified quotation.
> Ley 81/1961, de 23 de diciembre, sobre el Plan Sur de Valencia.

Glossary: *el Estado* (the national government); *el Ayuntamiento* (the city council); *la Diputación Provincial* (the provincial council); *por ciento* (per cent).

Tasks:

- **4a.** Read the reviewed 1961 source card (Spanish, with English on request). "For every 100 pesetas of hydraulic-works cost, what share did the 1961 law assign to each body?" *State 75, city 20, province 5.* The guided line pairs *setenta y cinco* with 75, *veinte* with 20 and *cinco* with 5. This is the original assigned split, not evidence of what every body actually paid or the split of every Plan Sur cost. A 1971 law later capped the provincial contribution; the card must disclose that later change.
- **4b.** "Apply the original 1961 assigned split to an imaginary hydraulic-works cost of 2,000,000 pesetas. What share would be assigned to the city?" *20% of 2,000,000 = **400,000** pesetas.* Labelled as a made-up calculation under that original split, not an actual cost or payment.
- **4c. Who decided?** Information card: "In 1961 Spain was a dictatorship under Francisco Franco, and the Plan Sur law was passed by a parliament that people did not freely elect. In April 1979 Spain held its first free local elections in over forty years. Sources date Valencia's decision to make the old riverbed a park to 1978 or 1979." Question: "Can we tell from this card whether the elections led to the park decision?" Choices: Yes, they happened at about the same time; Not yet, because we'd need the decision's exact date and evidence of who made it and why ← fits; No, elections never change decisions like that. Reply to the first: "If the decision was in 1978, it came before the elections. Even if it came after, happening close together isn't the same as one causing the other." *(Spec 119 section 5's association against cause, here with a date that decides whether a cause is even possible.)*
- **4d. Whose voices are missing?** "The sources in the folder are a law, newspapers and a park's history. Who might tell this story differently?" Choose any, with replies: farmers whose huerta land the channel was dug through; people in neighbourhoods left on the far side of the channel; people who wanted the motorway; families of people who died, whose names aren't in these sources. None is marked wrong. The reply to all: "Every one of these would be worth hearing. A Keeper notes whose record is in the folder and whose isn't."

2. **One reason Willowbank is different.** Starters: "Willow Brook is a small brook, not a big river…"; "Valencia is a city of many thousands; Willowbank is a village…"; "Moving a river took Valencia years and a national government's money…".

4. **What you're not sure about.** "The sources disagree on how many people died"; "We don't know what the huerta farmers thought"; another.

**Wren's response (the same whatever is written):**

> "A real city, a real river, and the same question we've got. I'll read this before the plans. They'll argue better for it."

## 11. The 2024 flood

Spec 120 has decided this: the Valencia pack is about 1957 and does not use the floods of 29 October 2024. A grown-up note says that Valencia flooded again in 2024 and that this pack does not cover it. This case follows that rule. Step 3e's fitting answer stays general ("a bigger flood is possible, and rain can also fall on streams that don't run into the new channel") and names no recent event.
