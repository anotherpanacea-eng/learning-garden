# Bells and Calendars: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 207](../specs/207-bells-and-calendars.md). Use as written, after independent spec review and a math check. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion and is never gendered; `{courier}` is the explorer's courier. Pip is he/him, Dot she/her, Bramble she/her (with her magpie), Léon he/him, Wren Halloway she/her, the librarian they/them, the miller she/her. ## Labels used on every card

- **A story from Willowbank**: the town, its tower, the ferry, the coach and every character are made up.
- **A true thing about math**: checked math, explained simply.
- **A true thing from history**: something people really did or made.
- **A made-up model**: a simple game rule, shown on the card, that is not real measurement.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. The stopped clock (Act I, chapter 4: mend the village)

**Hook:** The storm stopped the tower clock at a quarter past three. Pip wants it ringing again.

**Short:**
> The clock stopped at a quarter past three. Pip climbs the stairs. "First we fix the hands. Next we wind it. Last, the bell!"

**Longer:**
> The storm stopped the tower clock at a quarter past three. It has said a quarter past three for days. "That's not true anymore," says Pip, who finds this upsetting.
>
> He climbs the tower stairs with {guide} and Dot. Dust, a rope, a big round face from the inside.
>
> "First we move the hands," says {guide}. "The long one shows minutes. The short one shows the hour."
>
> "Next we wind it," says Dot.
>
> "And last," says Pip, "I ring the bell."
>
> They set the hands to the right time. They wind the clock. Tick. Tock. Pip pulls the rope with all his weight, and lifts off the floor a little.
>
> BONG. Down in the square, the baker looks up and checks his own clock.
>
> "Assistant Bell Ringer," says Pip. Nobody gave him the job. Nobody takes it away.

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> A quarter past three is 3:15. The minute hand points at the 3, because 3 fives make 15. The hour hand sits just past the 3. Later in the hour it creeps toward the 4.

### Scene 2. The tower calendar (Act II, chapter 2: a garden across the seasons)

**Hook:** The librarian hangs a calendar in the clock room so the town can see the year. Pip wants his birthday every week.

**Short:**
> A calendar goes up in the clock room. Twelve months, four seasons, four festivals. "Can my birthday be every week?" asks Pip. "No," says everyone.

**Longer:**
> The librarian carries a long calendar up the stairs and pins it beside the clock. "The clock tells the hours," they say. "This tells the year."
>
> Twelve months go in a row. Each month has its days in weeks, seven at a time.
>
> Dot finds the season bands. "Flood Boards and Planting in spring. Market Fair and the Show in summer. Harvest Supper in autumn. Lantern Night in winter, on the longest night."
>
> "And then?" asks Pip.
>
> "Then spring again," says the librarian. "A year goes round, like the clock."
>
> Pip finds a Tuesday and draws a cake on it. Then another Tuesday. Then another.
>
> "A birthday comes once a year," says Dot.
>
> "Mine is very popular," says Pip, and draws a fourth cake.

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> A year has 12 months and 365 days, or 366 in a leap year. Months are 28 to 31 days long, so a week can start in one month and end in the next.

### Scene 3. The timetable board (Act III, chapter 3: the route by the high ford)

**Hook:** The post coach starts running to Vadoalto. Dot chalks its times on a board, the 24-hour way.

**Short:**
> Dot chalks the coach times: 06:50, 11:35, 15:15. "Fifteen o'clock?" says Bramble. "That's not a time." {guide} says it is.

**Longer:**
> Now the old path is open, a post coach runs up to Vadoalto by the high ford. Dot chalks the times on a board in the clock room, so anyone can climb up and read them.
>
> 06:50. 11:35. 15:15.
>
> Bramble reads it twice. "Fifteen fifteen? A clock only goes up to twelve."
>
> "This board counts the whole day," says {guide}. "Midnight is 00:00. After noon it keeps counting: 13, 14, 15."
>
> "So 15:15 is a quarter past three in the afternoon," says Dot.
>
> Bramble frowns. "Then why not just write that?"
>
> "Because 3:15 happens twice a day," says Dot. "The coach only goes once."
>
> The magpie taps the 06:50 with its beak. Bramble shrugs. "Fine. But the bell still only rings three times."
>
> She is right about that.

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> Each coach takes 1 hour 15 minutes to the high ford and 2 hours 30 minutes to Vadoalto. The times are made up. The tower bell strikes on the 12-hour count.

### Scene 4. A timetable from the ferry (Act III, chapter 4: letters up and down the river)

**Hook:** Léon writes from Les Saules with the ferry times, written the French way.

**Short:**
> Léon writes: "Bonjour ! (Hello!) The ferry leaves Willowbank at 13 h 05. In Les Saules we write times like that."

**Longer** (French lines for a fluent reader's check):
> *Bonjour des Saules !* (Hello from Les Saules!)
>
> Dear friends in Willowbank,
>
> The ferry is running again, down the brook to us. I am sending the times for your board. We write them the 24-hour way. The one after lunch is 13 h 05. That means 13:05, five past one in the afternoon.
>
> *Le bac part à 13 h 05.* (The ferry leaves at 13:05.)
>
> It takes 50 minutes, if the ducks move. Sometimes the ducks do not move.
>
> Please tell Pip that the bell is very loud from the river. We can hear it at Mill bend. We count the bongs to know the hour.
>
> Léon, slightly damp

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> French often writes 24-hour times with an "h" between the hours and minutes: 13 h 05. It means the same as 13:05.

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer. Worked steps appear one tap at a time.

**T1. First, next, last** (A true thing about math)
> First is the start. Next comes after. Last is the end. Before means earlier. After means later.
Worked example: 1. First: climb the stairs. 2. Next: pull the rope. 3. Last: the bell rings.

**T2. Yesterday, today, tomorrow** (A true thing about math)
> Today is now. Yesterday was the day before. Tomorrow is the day after. When a day passes, the words move along.
Worked example: 1. Today: the clock is mended. 2. A night passes. 3. Now that day is yesterday.

**T3. Quicker and slower** (A true thing about math)
> Start together. The one who finishes first was quicker. It took less time. Earlier and later tell when. Quicker and slower tell how long.
Worked example: 1. Dot and Pip start climbing together. 2. Dot reaches the top first. 3. Dot was quicker.

**T4. Measure how long** (A true thing about math)
> A sand-glass measures time. Turn it when the job starts. Look when the job stops. One minute is 60 seconds.
Worked example: 1. Pip starts ringing; turn the 1-minute glass. 2. He stops as it empties. 3. About 1 minute.

**T5. Line up how long** (A true thing about math)
> Draw each job as a bar from the same start. The longest bar took the most time. Order the bars, shortest first.
Worked example: 1. Climb: 1 minute. 2. Ring: 2 minutes. 3. Wind: 5 minutes. 4. Shortest to longest: climb, ring, wind.

**T6. The week goes round** (A true thing about math)
> A week has seven days. After Sunday comes Monday again. Start on any day and count either way.
Worked example: 1. Start at Thursday. 2. Step back one: Wednesday. 3. Step forward one: Friday.

**T7. Months have different lengths** (A true thing about math)
> Twelve months make a year. Seven months have 31 days, four have 30. February has 28, or 29 in a leap year.
Worked example (knuckles): 1. Make two fists. 2. Knuckle months have 31 days: January, March, May. 3. Dips have fewer: February, April.

**T8. Leap years** (A true thing about math; Keeper's Desk)
> A year divisible by 4 is a leap year. Century years are leap years only if divisible by 400.
Worked example: 1. 2028: divisible by 4, leap. 2. 1900: a century, not divisible by 400, not leap. 3. 2000: divisible by 400, leap.

**T9. Count round in fives** (A true thing about math)
> The long hand moves 5 minutes from one number to the next. Count in fives from the 12: 5, 10, 15, 20.
Worked example: 1. Long hand on the 4. 2. Count: 5, 10, 15, 20. 3. Twenty minutes past.

**T10. Past and to** (A true thing about math)
> On the right side, say minutes past this hour. On the left side, say minutes to the next hour.
Worked example: 1. Long hand on the 9. 2. Left side: 15 minutes to go. 3. Quarter to four: 3:45.

**T11. To the minute** (A true thing about math)
> Find the last five the long hand passed. Count on the little marks. The hour is the number the short hand last passed.
Worked example: 1. Long hand passed the 4: 20. 2. Three more marks: 23. 3. Short hand past 7: 7:23.

**T12. Morning and afternoon** (A true thing about math)
> Each clock time happens twice a day. a.m. is from midnight to noon. p.m. is from noon to midnight.
Worked example: 1. 7:45 at breakfast: 7:45 a.m. 2. 7:45 at bedtime: 7:45 p.m.

**T13. The 24-hour clock** (A true thing about math)
> The 24-hour clock counts the whole day. Midnight is 00:00. Noon is 12:00. After noon, add 12 to the hour.
Worked example: 1. 3:45 p.m. 2. It's after noon: 3 + 12 = 15. 3. 15:45. Morning: 8:05 a.m. is 08:05.

**T14. Changing units** (A true thing about math)
> 60 seconds make a minute. 60 minutes make an hour. 7 days make a week. 12 months make a year. Multiply, then add the rest.
Worked example: 1. 3 hours 25 minutes. 2. 3 × 60 = 180. 3. 180 + 25 = 205 minutes.

**T15. How long? Count on** (A true thing about math)
> Mark the start and end on a line. Jump to the next o'clock. Jump whole hours. Jump the minutes left. Add the jumps.
Worked example: 1. 2:50 to 3:00: 10. 2. 3:00 to 4:00: 60. 3. 4:00 to 4:10: 10. 4. 80 minutes: 1 hour 20.

**T16. When does it end?** (A true thing about math)
> Use the same line forward. Jump to the next o'clock, then add what's left.
Worked example: 1. Start 3:40, add 35. 2. Jump 20 to 4:00. 3. 15 left: 4:15.

**T17. Reading a timetable** (A true thing about math)
> Each column is one trip. Each row is one stop. Find your trip, then read down to your stop.
Worked example: 1. Ferry column: 10:20 from Willowbank. 2. Read down to Les Saules: 11:10. 3. 50 minutes.

## Real-world and history cards

**H1. A clock tower in China** (A true thing from history; Seedling and up)
> More than 900 years ago, Su Song had a great clock tower built in Kaifeng, China. A water wheel turned it. Little figures rang bells and beat drums.
Keeper's note:
> Su Song was a scholar and official. He wrote a book with drawings of the tower, finished in 1092 and printed in 1094. Invaders took the tower apart in 1127, and nobody could rebuild it then. People have built working copies from his drawings.

**H2. Ten days that never happened** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> In 1582 a new calendar began. In Rome, Thursday, October 4 was followed by Friday, October 15. The dates in between were skipped.
Keeper's note:
> The old calendar had a leap year every 4 years. That made its year a little too long, so it had drifted about 10 days. The new calendar, named for Pope Gregory XIII, skipped the days and changed the rule: century years are leap years only if divisible by 400. Not every country changed at once. Britain changed in 1752.

**H3. Railway time** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> In 1893 a law gave all of Italy's railways one shared time. It counted the hours from midnight, the way the 24-hour clock does.
Keeper's note:
> Before then, different parts of Italy kept slightly different times. With one shared time, a train time meant the same thing everywhere. Many timetables today still use the 24-hour clock.

### Set A. First, next, last; quicker and slower (Sprout and Seedling)

**A2 (Sprout, 3-choice)**

Feedback A: Yesterday was the day before. Which day comes after today?
Feedback B: Yes. Mended today, rung tomorrow.

**A3 (Sprout, 3-choice)**

Explanation: A day passed, so market day is now yesterday.
Feedback A: Tomorrow hasn't happened yet. The market already did.
Feedback B: Today moved on. Market day stayed behind.

**A5 (Seedling, 3-choice)**

Feedback B: "To" times name the next hour. Which hour is coming?
Feedback C: Count in fives: the 8 means 40.

**D4 (Sapling, number)**

**D5 (Sapling, number)**

Explanation: An hour has 60 minutes. 44 and 16 make 60.

Explanation: 3 + 12 = 15, so 15:15.
Feedback A: Add 12, not 10. Count on from noon: 13, 14, 15.
Feedback B: Yes. 15:15.
Feedback C: 03:15 is in the night. This coach leaves after lunch.

**E2 (Sapling, 3-choice)**

Feedback B: 7:45 p.m. is evening. It would be 19:45.

**E3 (Sapling, 3-choice)**

- B. 7:30 a.m. *(tests: forgetting it's after noon)*
- C. 7:30 p.m. *(right)*

Explanation: 19 − 12 = 7. After noon, so 7:30 p.m.
Feedback A: Take away 12, not 10.
Feedback B: 19 is after noon. Morning is before 12.
Feedback C: Yes. 7:30 in the evening.

**E4 (Sapling, 3-choice)**

- A. 12:20 p.m., just after noon. *(right)*
- B. 0:20 p.m. *(tests: taking 12 from the noon hour)*
- C. 12:20 a.m., just after midnight. *(tests: midnight hour confused with noon)*

Explanation: On the 24-hour clock, the noon hour stays 12. Midnight's hour is 00.
Feedback A: Yes. Twenty past noon.
Feedback B: The noon hour stays 12. Take 12 away only from 13 up.

**E5 (Sapling, 3-choice)**

## Key terms (glossary)

- **before / after**: earlier than / later than.
- **yesterday / today / tomorrow**: the day before now / now / the day after now.
- **morning, afternoon, evening**: parts of one day.
- **quicker / slower**: took less time / took more time.
- **earlier / later**: happened sooner / happened afterward.
- **duration**: how long something lasts, from start to end.
- **second**: a very short time. 60 seconds make a minute.
- **minute**: 60 seconds. 60 minutes make an hour.
- **week**: 7 days, going round and round.
- **month**: 28 to 31 days. 12 months make a year.
- **leap year**: a year with 366 days, with February 29.
- **century year**: a year ending in 00, like 1900 or 2000.
- **quarter past / quarter to**: 15 minutes after the hour / 15 minutes before the next hour.
- **a.m. / p.m.**: from midnight to noon / from noon to midnight.
- **noon / midnight**: 12:00 in the middle of the day / 00:00 at the start of the day.
- **24-hour clock**: a clock that counts the hours of the day from 00 to 23.
- **timetable**: a table of times for trips and stops.
- **sand-glass**: a glass that measures time with falling sand.

## Off-screen invitation (optional, grown-up-led)

**Time it for real** (Sprout to Sapling): with a sand timer, a kitchen timer or a clock with a second hand, time something real: brushing teeth, climbing the stairs, building a block tower. Guess first, then time it. Who started the timer? When did it stop? *Grown-up note: this is for fun. Nobody is racing. Small sand timers are glass; hold it together.*

**Read a real timetable** (Sapling and Keeper's Desk): look at a bus, train or ferry timetable with a grown-up. Find one trip. How long does it take? *Grown-up note: use any public timetable; no travel needed.*

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** ordering events and the words for time; comparing durations from a shared start; measuring with sand-glasses and seconds; days, weeks, months, years and leap years; telling the time to five minutes and to the minute, past and to; a.m., p.m. and the 24-hour clock; changing units of time; finding how long and when something ends on a number line, across the hour and noon; reading a timetable.

**What it does not claim:** the screen sand-glass is not real timing, so real timing happens off screen. The clock's hands move together, so the child does not draw both hands alone. Time zones, daylight saving, seconds on a clock face and Roman numerals are not taught. It builds on the Preparation Clock (o'clock and half past) and the Courier Clock Walk (elapsed minutes up to an hour) and does not repeat them.
