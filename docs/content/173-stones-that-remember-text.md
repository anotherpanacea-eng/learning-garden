## Story scenes (A story from Willowbank)

**Story label on every scene:** A story from Willowbank. The drawer, the quarry and the tray are made up.

### Scene 1. The librarian's stone drawer (Act I, chapter 3)

**Hook (30 max):** Storm water got into the library. The librarian has opened an old wooden drawer full of stones.

**Short (30 max):**
> The stones in the librarian's drawer are fine. Two paper labels are not. The ink has run. "We know what they are," says the librarian. "Not where they lay."

**Longer (150 max):**
> The library smells of wet paper. The librarian lifts out a long, shallow drawer and sets it on the table.
>
> Inside, each stone sits in its own little box. Each box has a label in tidy, round writing. *Shell, quarry, third layer up. Fern, quarry, gray band.*
>
> "I labeled every one," says the librarian. "Carefully."
>
> Two labels are just blue smudges now.
>
> Pip picks up one of the smudged stones. "It's still a fossil. Look, a shell."
>
> "It is," says the librarian. "We still know what it is. We no longer know where it lay, or what lay with it."
>
> {guide} looks at the other labels. "So the label was part of the fossil."
>
> "The most fragile part," says the librarian, and puts the drawer somewhere dry.

**Keeper's note (80 max):**
> This drawer is part of the story. Real paleontologists record where every fossil lies before they lift it: the place, the layer, what was nearby. Once a fossil is moved, that information cannot be found again. A fossil with no record still teaches something, but much less.

### Scene 2. Bramble's curiosity tray (Act II, chapter 1)

**Hook (30 max):** The market is back. Bramble has a tray of curiosities, and one stone has a very large label.

**Short (30 max):**
> Bramble's label says DRAGON EGG, VERY RARE. Pip asks if it is a stone potato. The librarian says it is fossil dung. Pip stops being hungry.

**Longer (150 max):**
> Bramble has a folding table at the market. On it: a feather, three ribbons, a bent spoon, and a lumpy brown stone. Its label says *DRAGON EGG, VERY RARE.*
>
> "Is it a stone potato?" asks Pip. "Can you cook it?"
>
> The magpie clacks in a rude way.
>
> The librarian looks closely and smiles. They take a polished slice of stone from their bag. Inside it are tiny pale chips.
>
> "Mine is like yours, cut open," they say. "These chips are bits of bone. This is fossil dung. A coprolite."
>
> Pip puts both paws behind his back.
>
> Bramble lifts her chin. "So it IS rare."
>
> "Rarer than a dragon egg," says the librarian. "There are no dragon eggs."
>
> "Then I'm keeping the label," says Bramble, and changes one word. It now says *DINNER, VERY RARE.*

**Keeper's note (80 max):**
> This tray is part of the story. Real coprolites are fossil dung, and scientists prize them: inside are bits of what an animal really ate. Mary Anning noticed some of the first ones in England in the 1820s. In real life, fossil collecting and selling follow local rules, and a fossil with no record of where it was found tells less.

## Teach cards

Each card holds one idea. Worked steps appear one per tap.

**T1. Record before you lift.**
> Where a fossil lies is part of its story. Write it down first.
> Step 1: Find the square. Step 2: Name the layer. Step 3: Note neighbors. Step 4: Lift.

**T2. Right tool, right place.**
> Heavy tools move rock far from the bone. Fine tools work close to it.
> Step 1: Pick for hard rock. Step 2: Trowel nearer. Step 3: Brush at the bone.

**T3. A jacket for the journey.**
> A traveling bone wears a plaster jacket, like a cast on an arm.
> Step 1: Paper on the bone. Step 2: Plaster strips over it. Step 3: Flip and cover.

**T4. Dung shows a meal.**
> A coprolite is fossil dung. Cut it open and you can see bits of food.
> Step 1: Slice. Step 2: Look with a lens. Step 3: Bone bits mean meat.

**T5. Could eat, did eat.**
> Teeth show what an animal could eat. Dung shows what it did eat, that time.
> Example: sharp teeth say "could eat meat." Bone in dung says "did eat meat."

**T6. Tiny to enormous.**
> Some dinosaurs were hen-sized. Some were longer than ten hay carts in a row.
> Step 1: Compsognathus, about one hen long. Step 2: Argentinosaurus, about ten carts long.

**T7. Everywhere, even under ice.**
> Dinosaur fossils are found on all seven continents. Antarctica has the fewest, because ice hides the rock.

**T8. Lower is older.**
> In undisturbed layers, the bottom formed first. Different layers hold different life.
> Step 1: Shells at the bottom. Step 2: Ferns above. Step 3: So the sea came first.

**T9. Change takes many generations.**
> Life changes slowly, as parents have young, over very many generations. One animal does not change itself.
> Example: early horses had four front toes. Today's horses have one.

**T10. The Life Rope (Sapling and up).**
> On our made-up rope, 1 meter means 100 million years.
> Step 1: Dinosaurs ended 66 million years ago. Step 2: 66 ÷ 100 = 0.66. Step 3: Pin 0.66 m.

**T11. Feathers before flight (Keeper's Desk).**
> Some feathered dinosaurs could not fly. Feathers came first, probably for warmth and showing off.

**T12. After the great extinctions (Keeper's Desk).**
> Five times, many kinds of living things died out over a short span of deep time. Each time, new kinds slowly spread afterward.

## Guided item: your first dig

> Step 1: Tap **Look** and find the bone. (It is in B2.)
> Step 2: Tap **Record**. Your notebook writes: *B2, gray layer, facing the brook.*
> Step 3: Choose the **brush** for the soil next to the bone.
> Step 4: Put the jacket cards in order: paper, plaster, flip.

**Feedback when done:** You recorded first, used gentle tools and jacketed it. That is how real teams work.

**If lifted before recording:** Where was it lying? Not written down. In a real dig you cannot put a fossil back.

**If the pick is used beside the bone:** A tiny crack. Heavy tools are for rock far from the bone.

## Slice Table lines

**C1 (bone bits):** Tiny pale chips of crushed bone.
**C2 (wood fibers):** Stringy brown fibers of old, rotted wood.
**C3 (fish scales):** Thin shiny flakes, like little fingernails.

**Question:** What did this animal eat, at least once?
**Second question:** Do we know exactly who made it?
- Feedback (no): Right. Often we can't be sure. Size, age and place give clues.
- Feedback (yes): A dropping rarely comes with a name. Scientists reason from clues.

**Life Rope** *(a made-up rope, 1 meter for every 100 million years)*:

- First land plants, roughly 470 million years ago: about 4.7 m.

**Feather Board** *(Keeper's Desk)*:

## True things from science (cards)

**Mary Anning's stones** *(A true thing from science history)*
> By 1824, Mary Anning noticed "bezoar stones" in the belly area of ichthyosaur skeletons. Broken open, they held fish bones and scales. In 1829 William Buckland named them coprolites and cited her work.

**A king-sized dropping** *(A true thing from science)*
> A coprolite about 44 cm long was found in Saskatchewan, Canada. It is packed with broken bone. Its size and age point to Tyrannosaurus.

**Plant-eaters' wood** *(A true thing from science)*
> In Montana, big coprolites are full of rotted conifer wood. Scientists think the duck-billed dinosaur Maiasaura left them.

**The forgotten giant** *(A true thing from science)*
> In 1954 a big skeleton was found near Orcau, in the Pyrenees. It was forgotten for over sixty years. In 2022 it was named Abditosaurus, "forgotten reptile."

**Antarctica's crested hunter** *(A true thing from science)*
> Cryolophosaurus was dug from Mount Kirkpatrick, Antarctica, in 1990 and 1991. Its rock is about 190 million years old.

**Horses through time** *(A true thing from science)*
> Hyracotherium lived about 55 million years ago. It was small, with four toes on each front foot. Today's horses stand on one toe. The family tree branches like a bush.

**Archaeopteryx** *(A true thing from science)*
> Found in Germany in 1861, about 150 million years old. It had teeth, clawed fingers, a long bony tail and feathered wings.

**Map pins:** Tyrannosaurus, North America. Argentinosaurus, South America. Abditosaurus, Europe. Microraptor, Asia. Cryolophosaurus, Antarctica. Spinosaurus, Africa. Australovenator, Australia.

**Joined land toggle:** *A simplified picture.* Long ago the continents were joined. They slowly moved apart.

## Problem sets

Each item: prompt, choices with misconception tags, ✓ for the answer, per-choice feedback, hint, explanation. "I'm stuck" ladder on every item (hint, easier worked example, Skip for now).

**Tag meanings:** `dig-fast` digging is quick; `lift-first` position does not matter; `big-tool` any tool will do; `lone-hunter` one person does it all or owns the find; `jacket-cleans` the jacket cleans the bone; `dung-rots` dung cannot fossilize; `dragon-real` dragons are real; `contents-guess` diet guessed without reading contents; `teeth-proof` teeth prove a meal; `dung-names-maker` a dropping names its maker; `all-huge` all dinosaurs were huge; `dinos-one-place` dinosaurs lived in one region; `few-finds-few-dinos` fewer finds means fewer dinosaurs; `continents-fixed` continents never move; `layers-same-age` layers formed together; `top-oldest` the top layer is oldest; `always-same` life and places never change; `one-animal-changed` one animal changes in its life; `ladder-progress` change means getting better; `feathers-for-flight` feathers arose for flying; `archaeopteryx-first` Archaeopteryx was the first feathered animal; `kpg-biggest` the dinosaur extinction was the worst; `cant-compare` extinctions cannot be compared; `extinction-only-end` an extinction is only an ending.

### Set 1. Dig in order

**1.1 (Sprout) Put the dig in order.** Cards: Clean in the lab · Find the bone · Put on a jacket · Write down where it lies · Dig gently.
- Answer: Find the bone, Write down where it lies, Dig gently, Put on a jacket, Clean in the lab.
- Tags: "Dig" before "Write down" `lift-first`; "Clean" before "jacket" `dig-fast`.
- Hint: What must happen before the bone moves?
- Explanation: Finding comes first, then recording. Digging, jacketing and lab cleaning follow, so nothing is lost on the way.

**1.2 (Sprout)** Which tool goes right next to the bone?
- a) A heavy pick. `big-tool` · Feedback: A pick can crack bone. Save it for rock far away.
- b) ✓ A soft brush. · Feedback: Yes. Gentle tools keep the bone whole.
- c) A big spade. `dig-fast` · Feedback: A spade moves lots of soil fast. Bones need slow work.
- Hint: Which tool would you use on a cracked teacup?
- Explanation: Paleontologists use heavy tools far from the fossil and gentle ones close to it.

**1.3 (Seedling)** Why write down where a bone lay before lifting it?
- a) ✓ Once it moves, its place is lost. · Feedback: Yes. The spot, the layer and the neighbors are evidence too.
- b) So nobody else can find the site. `lone-hunter` · Feedback: Records are shared, so others can check the work.
- c) It does not matter; only the bone counts. `lift-first` · Feedback: Think of the librarian's smudged labels. Something was lost.
- Hint: Remember the two smudged labels in the librarian's drawer.
- Explanation: Where a fossil lay tells its age and what lived with it. That information vanishes once it is lifted.

**1.4 (Seedling)** What is the plaster jacket for?
- a) To clean rock off the bone. `jacket-cleans` · Feedback: Cleaning happens later, in the lab. The jacket protects.
- b) To show the bone belongs to you. `lone-hunter` · Feedback: Fossils are studied by teams and kept for everyone.
- c) ✓ To keep it whole on the trip. · Feedback: Yes. Like a cast on an arm, it holds pieces together.
- Hint: Think about a bumpy ride to the lab.
- Explanation: Paper, then strips of burlap soaked in plaster, wrap the bone and its rock so it reaches the lab in one piece.

**1.5 (Seedling) Match each job to the person.**
- Prospector → walks the rocks looking for fossils.
- Preparator → cleans rock off fossils in the lab.
- Curator → looks after the museum's collection.
- Tag on any mismatch: `lone-hunter`.
- Hint: "Prepare" sounds like getting something ready.
- Explanation: Paleontology is teamwork. Many people handle one fossil between the hillside and the museum.

**1.6 (Seedling, number)** A skeleton was found in 1954. It was named in 2022. How many years later?
- Answer: **68 years.**
- Worked steps: 2022 − 1954. 2022 − 2000 = 22. 2000 − 1954 = 46. 22 + 46 = 68.
- Wrong-answer note: 78 or 1932 `place-value slip` (not a science tag). Feedback: Line up the digits, then subtract from the right.
- Hint: Count from 1954 up to 2000, then on to 2022.
- Explanation: Abditosaurus was found in 1954 and named in 2022, 68 years later. Good records help a find be picked up again.

### Set 2. What was for dinner?

**2.1 (Seedling)** Bramble's "dragon egg" is a coprolite. What is it?
- a) ✓ Dung that turned to stone. · Feedback: Yes. Fossil dung, with bits of food inside.
- b) An egg a dragon laid. `dragon-real` · Feedback: Dragons live in stories. Coprolites are real.
- c) A stone that water shaped like dung. `dung-rots` · Feedback: Some really are dung. Slice one and food bits show.
- Hint: What did the librarian's slice show inside?
- Explanation: A coprolite is fossil dung. It is a trace fossil, because it records eating.

**2.2 (Seedling)** A slice shows crushed bone. What did the animal eat?
- a) Plants. `contents-guess` · Feedback: Plants leave fibers or seeds. Bone means something else.
- b) ✓ Meat. · Feedback: Yes. Bone chips in dung mean it ate other animals.
- c) Fish. `contents-guess` · Feedback: Fish would leave thin shiny scales. These are bone chips.
- Hint: Read what is inside, not the shape outside.
- Explanation: Contents tell diet. Bone means a meat-eater, at least for that meal.

**2.3 (Sapling)** Which clue shows what an animal *did* eat?
- a) Its sharp teeth. `teeth-proof` · Feedback: Teeth show what it could eat. They don't record a meal.
- b) The size of its skull. `contents-guess` · Feedback: Skull size hints at body size, not dinner.
- c) ✓ Bits of bone in its dung. · Feedback: Yes. Dung holds the meal itself.
- Hint: Could eat or did eat?
- Explanation: Teeth are indirect evidence. Dung contents are direct evidence of a real meal.

**2.4 (Sapling)** Can scientists always say who left a coprolite?
- a) Yes, each dropping names its own maker. `dung-names-maker` · Feedback: Dung has no name tag. Scientists reason from clues.
- b) No, so coprolites are no use. `contents-guess` · Feedback: They still show a real meal, even with the maker unsure.
- c) ✓ Not always; clues help them guess. · Feedback: Yes. That is why papers say "probably."
- Hint: What clues pointed to Tyrannosaurus?
- Explanation: The Saskatchewan coprolite points to Tyrannosaurus by its size, age and bone. Many coprolites stay unassigned.

**2.5 (Seedling) True or not true from this card?** Card: *By 1824, Mary Anning noticed "bezoar stones" in the belly area of ichthyosaur skeletons. Broken open, they held fish bones and scales.*
- "The stones lay in the belly area." **True from this card.**
- "Inside were fish bones and scales." **True from this card.**
- "Mary Anning named them coprolites." **Not true from this card.** `lone-hunter` · Feedback: The card doesn't say. William Buckland named them in 1829, citing her.
- Hint: Read only what the card says.
- Explanation: Anning's careful noticing led Buckland to name coprolites. Good science often passes from hand to hand.

**2.6 (Sapling, number)** A 7 kg coprolite is 3 to 5 tenths bone. How much bone?
- Answer: **about 2.1 to 3.5 kg.**
- Worked steps: 7 ÷ 10 = 0.7. 0.7 × 3 = 2.1. 0.7 × 5 = 3.5.
- Hint: Find one tenth of 7 first.
- Explanation: Scientists estimated 30 to 50 percent bone, so the answer is a range, not one number.

### Set 3. Big, small, everywhere

**3.1 (Sprout) Order from smallest to biggest.** Cards: Argentinosaurus · bee hummingbird · Tyrannosaurus · Compsognathus.
- Answer: bee hummingbird, Compsognathus, Tyrannosaurus, Argentinosaurus.
- Tag if Compsognathus is placed among the giants: `all-huge`.
- Hint: One of these is a bird alive today.
- Explanation: The bee hummingbird is a tiny living dinosaur. Argentinosaurus was one of the longest land animals ever.

**3.2 (Sprout)** Were all dinosaurs huge?
- a) Yes, all bigger than a cart. `all-huge` · Feedback: Compsognathus was about one hen long.
- b) Yes, the small ones were babies. `all-huge` · Feedback: Some kinds were small even when fully grown.
- c) ✓ No, some were hen-sized. · Feedback: Yes. Dinosaurs came tiny, middling and enormous.
- Hint: Look at the Size Line.
- Explanation: Dinosaurs ranged from hen-sized to longer than ten hay carts.

**3.3 (Sapling, number)** Argentinosaurus was about 30 m long. A hay cart is 3 m. How many carts?
- Answer: **10 carts.** (Keeper's Desk extension: at 35 m, about 11 to 12 carts.)
- Worked steps: 30 ÷ 3 = 10.
- Hint: How many 3s make 30?
- Explanation: Estimates run about 30 to 35 m, because no complete skeleton has been found. (The cart is made up.)

**3.4 (Seedling)** Why have so few dinosaur fossils been found in Antarctica?
- a) Dinosaurs never lived there. `few-finds-few-dinos` · Feedback: Some did. Cryolophosaurus was found there.
- b) ✓ Ice covers most of the rock. · Feedback: Yes. Searchers can only dig the bare rocky places.
- c) It was always too cold for them. `continents-fixed` · Feedback: Antarctica was warmer and in a different place long ago.
- Hint: Where can you dig in Antarctica?
- Explanation: Few finds can mean hidden rock, not few animals. That is "absence of evidence."

**3.5 (Sapling)** Abditosaurus's closest relatives lived in South America and Africa. What does that suggest?
- a) ✓ Animals once crossed between faraway lands. · Feedback: Yes. Lower seas and moving land opened ways across.
- b) Each continent always kept its own dinosaurs. `dinos-one-place` · Feedback: Related kinds turn up on different continents.
- c) The continents have never moved. `continents-fixed` · Feedback: They move slowly, a few centimeters a year.
- Hint: Relatives far apart had to get apart somehow.
- Explanation: Its ancestors probably reached Europe from Africa when seas were lower, about 70 million years ago.

### Set 4. Layers and long time

**4.1 (Sapling)** Shells lie at the bottom, leaves at the top. Which lived first?
- a) The leaves, at the top. `top-oldest` · Feedback: Layers pile up. The top one came last.
- b) Both at the same time. `layers-same-age` · Feedback: Each layer was laid down after the one beneath.
- c) ✓ The shells, at the bottom. · Feedback: Yes. In undisturbed layers, lower is older.
- Hint: Which layer had to be there first?
- Explanation: Layers build up over time, so fossils low down are older (unless the rock was folded, as in spec 139).

**4.2 (Sapling)** What do shells under leaves tell you about this place?
- a) ✓ It was sea, and later land. · Feedback: Yes. The layers record a changing landscape.
- b) It has always looked as it does now. `always-same` · Feedback: The layers show it changed.
- c) It was land, and later sea. `top-oldest` · Feedback: Check the order. Bottom is older.
- Hint: Sea creatures in the older layer, land plants above.
- Explanation: Fossils show what lived where. Their order in the layers shows how a place changed.

**4.3 (Sapling)** Early horses had four front toes. Today's horses have one. What happened?
- a) One horse lost toes as it grew. `one-animal-changed` · Feedback: A single animal can't do that. The change took generations.
- b) ✓ Horses changed over many generations. · Feedback: Yes. Millions of years, many branches of the family.
- c) Horses got better and more advanced. `ladder-progress` · Feedback: Change isn't climbing a ladder. One toe suits running on open ground.
- Hint: Think about parents, young, and their young.
- Explanation: Fossils show horse feet changing across very many generations, on a bushy family tree.

**4.4 (Keeper's Desk, number)** Anchiornis: 160 to 155 million years ago. Archaeopteryx: 150. How much older?
- Answer: **about 5 to 10 million years older.**
- Worked steps: 155 − 150 = 5. 160 − 150 = 10.
- Tag for "Archaeopteryx is older": `archaeopteryx-first`.
- Hint: A bigger "years ago" number means older.
- Explanation: Archaeopteryx was found first, in 1861, but feathered dinosaurs older than it are now known.

**4.5 (Keeper's Desk)** Sinosauropteryx had feathery fuzz but could not fly. What does that show?
- a) Feathers first grew for flying. `feathers-for-flight` · Feedback: This animal had them and could not fly.
- b) ✓ Feathers came before flight. · Feedback: Yes. They likely helped first with warmth or display.
- c) The fuzz must be a mistake. `feathers-for-flight` · Feedback: Many fossils from China show the same fuzz.
- Hint: Feathers, but no flying. Which came first?
- Explanation: Feathers appear in dinosaurs that could not fly, so flight came later. How flight began is still argued.

**4.6 (Keeper's Desk)** Which great extinction was the most severe?
- a) ✓ The end-Permian. · Feedback: Yes, about 252 million years ago. About 8 in 10 kinds of sea life, maybe more, were lost.
- b) The K-Pg, when dinosaurs ended. `kpg-biggest` · Feedback: It is the most famous. The end-Permian was larger.
- c) We can't compare them at all. `cant-compare` · Feedback: Fossil counts give rough sizes. The ranges still differ clearly.
- Hint: The most famous one is not the biggest.
- Explanation: Estimates are rough, but the end-Permian is the largest loss in the fossil record. Recovery took millions of years.

**4.7 (Keeper's Desk, say why, read by a grown-up)** After a great extinction, what happens next? Tell a grown-up.
- What a good answer includes: over millions of years, new kinds spread into the empty space; after the dinosaurs ended, mammals and birds spread. Tag on "nothing, it just ends": `extinction-only-end`.
- Hint: Look at the layer above the line on the Quarry Wall.
- Explanation: Each extinction was also a beginning. Recovery is slow, in deep time, and new life fills the gaps. Not stored, not graded.

## Key terms

- **Paleontologist:** a scientist who studies life from long ago, using fossils.
- **Fossil:** remains or traces of a living thing from long ago, usually in rock.
- **Trace fossil:** a mark an animal left, like a footprint or dung.
- **Coprolite:** fossil dung.
- **Plaster jacket:** a hard wrap that protects a fossil on its trip to the lab.
- **Layer:** one sheet of rock laid down on top of others.
- **Generation:** parents, then their young, then their young's young.
- **Continent:** one of Earth's seven big areas of land.
- **Extinct:** gone; none left alive anywhere.
- **Mass extinction:** a time when many kinds died out over a short span of deep time.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** how paleontologists find, record, dig, protect and study fossils; fossil dung as direct evidence of diet; the size range of dinosaurs; that dinosaur fossils are found on every continent; reading rock layers as a record of changing life; change across generations; and, for older explorers, feathers before flight and the five great extinctions with their recoveries.

**What it does not claim:** it does not teach how fossils form in detail (spec 142), rock types (spec 139) or how natural selection works (spec 161). The benches, Quarry Wall and Life Rope are simplified models. Dates are approximate and some are still debated; the pack says "about." Extinctions are taught as deep-time patterns, never as scenes, and nothing speaks of the future.
