# Ribbon Patterns: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 194](../specs/194-ribbon-patterns.md). Use as written, after independent spec review and a math check. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion and is never gendered; `{courier}` is the explorer's courier. Bramble is she/her (with her magpie), Pip he/him, Dot she/her, Ember the pocket-dragon. Real-world facts are in the [source audit](../source-audits/194-ribbon-patterns.md); items still marked `[check]` stay off screen until cleared. Nothing here is scored or timed.

## Labels used on every card

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. The tangled gate (Act I, chapter 2: deliver and explain)

**Hook:** The storm untied half of Bramble's ribbons. The magpie tied them back. Something looks odd.

**Short:**
> The storm tangled Bramble's gate. The magpie tied the ribbons back. "Red, blue, red, blue, blue," says Bramble. "Who did that?"

**Longer:**
> After the storm, Bramble's gate is a mess. Half the ribbons lie in the mud. The magpie has been busy. It has tied them all back on, very proudly.
>
> Bramble reads the bars aloud. "Red, blue, red, blue, blue, blue, red, blue."
>
> The magpie puffs up its chest.
>
> "It's lovely," says Pip, who is being kind.
>
> "It's muddled," says Bramble, who is being honest. "My grandmother's gate goes red, blue, red, blue, all the way along."
>
> {guide} looks along the bars. "So one ribbon broke the pattern. Which one?"
>
> The magpie looks at the fifth bar, then at the sky.

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> A repeating pattern has a part that comes back again and again. Here the part is red, blue. If you know the part, you can find a mistake without counting every ribbon.

### Scene 2. Ribbons for Ember (Act II, chapter 3: Ember's birthday)

**Hook:** Bramble ties ribbons on the café chairs in a pattern that grows. Pip wants to try every outfit.

**Short:**
> Bramble ties ribbon squares on the chairs: one square, then two, then three. "Each new square needs three more ribbons," she says.

**Longer:**
> The café is full of trays. Bramble arrives with a basket of ribbons and starts making ribbon squares along the bench. One square takes four ribbons. Two squares, side by side, take seven.
>
> "Why not eight?" asks Pip. "Two squares, four each."
>
> "They share a side," says Bramble. "Each new square needs only three more."
>
> Ember counts on her claws and runs out of claws.
>
> Then Pip finds the hat box. Three hats, two ribbons for the band. He tries the patchwork hat with red. Then green with gold. Then patchwork with red again.
>
> "You did that one," says Dot.
>
> "How do I know when I've done them all?" asks Pip.
>
> "Hold one hat still," says Bramble. "Try every ribbon with it. Then the next hat." She goes a bit pink. "My grandmother showed me that."

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> One square takes 4 ribbons. Each new square adds 3. Ten squares take 1 + 3 × 10 = 31. Three hats and two ribbons make 3 × 2 = 6 outfits.

### Scene 3. A ribbon on each Way (Act VI, chapter 2: compare routes and keep a plan; the ending)

**Hook:** Bramble ties ribbons on the posts along the river path, in a rule. Will the hundredth post get one?

**Short:**
> Bramble ties a ribbon on post 1, then post 4, then post 7. "Every third post," she says. "Will post 100 get one?"

**Longer:**
> Three Ways go to Vadoalto. Bramble walks the river path with a basket of blue ribbons. She ties one on the first post. She skips two. She ties one on post 4. Then post 7. Then post 10.
>
> "Why not every post?" asks Pip.
>
> "Not enough ribbon," says Bramble. "And the magpie steals one in three."
>
> Dot has her notebook out. "The path has a hundred posts. Will the last one get a ribbon?"
>
> "Count them," says Pip.
>
> "Or," says {guide}, "find the rule, and ask the rule."
>
> Bramble ties a ribbon on post 13 and says nothing, which means she knows.

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> The ribbon posts are 1, 4, 7, 10: the nth ribbon is on post 3n − 2. Post 100 means 3n − 2 = 100, so n = 34. Yes: the 34th ribbon.

## Teach cards

Each card is under 30 words. Worked steps appear one tap at a time.

**T1. A part that repeats** (A true thing about math)
> A repeating pattern has a part that comes again and again. Find that part. Then you can say what comes next.
Worked example: 1. Red, blue, red, blue, red. 2. The part is red, blue. 3. After red comes blue.

**T2. Swap the groups** (A true thing about math)
> Swap two groups around. Nothing was added. Nothing was taken away. It is the same amount as before.
Worked example: 1. Red bows here, blue bows there. 2. Swap their places. 3. Same bows, same amount.

**T3. Count in whole parts** (A true thing about math)
> To find a far ribbon, count in whole parts. Each part is the same, so you can skip ahead.
Worked example: 1. The part is red, red, blue: 3 ribbons. 2. 12 ribbons is 4 parts. 3. The 12th ribbon ends a part: blue.

**T4. A pattern that grows** (A true thing about math)
> A growing pattern gets bigger each step. Look at what each new step adds.
Worked example: 1. Ribbon squares: 4, 7, 10 ribbons. 2. 7 − 4 = 3. 10 − 7 = 3. 3. Each new square adds 3.

**T5. Say the rule in words** (A true thing about math)
> A good rule says where the pattern starts and what each step adds. Then anyone can build it again.
Worked example: 1. Not "it goes up". 2. "Start with 4 ribbons. Each new square adds 3."

**T6. Test your rule** (A true thing about math)
> A rule that worked twice might still fail. Try it on a new case before you trust it.
Worked example: 1. 47 − 10 = 37. 57 − 10 = 47. 2. Rule: the tens digit drops by 1. 3. Test: 82 − 10 = 72. It holds.

**T7. Tables have patterns** (A true thing about math)
> A table pairs each input with an output. A pattern down a column can check an answer.
Worked example: 1. Posts and ribbons, 4 each: 4, 8, 12, 16. 2. All even. 3. An odd answer can't be right.

**T8. Build from facts you know** (A true thing about math)
> A fact you don't know is often two facts you do know, put together.
Worked example: 1. 8 × 7 is 8 × 5 and 8 × 2. 2. 40 + 16. 3. 56.

**T9. Two rules side by side** (A true thing about math)
> Line up two patterns: first with first, second with second. Then compare each pair.
Worked example: 1. Bramble: 0, 3, 6, 9. Magpie: 0, 6, 12, 18. 2. 6 is twice 3; 12 is twice 6. 3. Always twice.

**T10. Pairs on a grid** (A true thing about math)
> Make a pair from each column: (Bramble's number, magpie's number). Go across first, then up.
Worked example: 1. Pair (3, 6). 2. Across 3, up 6. 3. The points sit on one straight line from (0, 0).

**T11. Start and step** (A true thing about math)
> A term-to-term rule gives a start and a step. A step can be a decimal. It can take you below zero.
Worked example: 1. 7, 4, 1. 2. Start at 7, subtract 3. 3. Next: 1 − 3 = −2.

**T12. A rule for any position** (A true thing about math)
> A position-to-term rule turns the step number n into the term. Put in n = 1, then 2, then 3.
Worked example: 1. Rule: 3n − 2. 2. n = 1: 3 − 2 = 1. 3. n = 2: 4. n = 3: 7.

**T13. Why the number before n is the step** (A true thing about math)
> Each square adds 3 ribbons, so n squares add n lots of 3. Add the one ribbon you start with.
Worked example: 1. 1 + 3 = 4. 2. 1 + 3 + 3 = 7. 3. So 3n + 1. The 1 is the zeroth term.

**T14. Is that number in the sequence?** (A true thing about math)
> Set the rule equal to the number. If the position comes out whole, it is in. If not, it isn't.
Worked example: 1. 4n − 1 = 99. 2. 4n = 100, n = 25. In. 3. 4n − 1 = 100 gives 25.25. Not in.

**T15. Hold one choice still** (A true thing about math)
> To list every way, hold one choice still. Run through every other choice. Then move on.
Worked example: 1. Patchwork hat: red, gold. 2. Black hat: red, gold. 3. Green hat: red, gold.

**T16. Count the grid** (A true thing about math)
> Rows times columns counts every outfit. Adding the two side counts misses most of them.
Worked example: 1. 3 hats down, 4 ribbons across. 2. 3 × 4 = 12 outfits. 3. 3 + 4 = 7 is too few.

**T17. Not every sequence adds the same** (A true thing about math; Keeper's Desk)
> Write the differences. Same difference each time: arithmetic. Same multiplier each time: geometric. Neither: something else.
Worked example: 1. 3, 6, 12, 24: × 2 each time, geometric. 2. 1, 4, 9, 16: differences 3, 5, 7. 3. Neither.

## Real-world and history cards

**H1. Counting rhythms** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> Poets in India counted the rhythms they could make from short and long beats. The counts go 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21. About 1150, Hemachandra wrote the rule.
Keeper's note:
> A long beat lasts as long as two short ones. Each count is the two before it added together. Gopala wrote about it a little earlier, and older poets had studied the puzzle too.

**H2. Fibonacci's rabbits** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> In 1202, Leonardo of Pisa, now called Fibonacci, wrote a puzzle about pairs of rabbits. The counts went 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, and on.
Keeper's note:
> His book says one pair becomes 377 pairs in a year. The rabbits follow a pretend rule: none die, and each grown pair has a new pair every month. Real rabbits do not work like that. The numbers are the ones India's poets listed.

**H3. The number triangle** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> In China, Yang Hui wrote about a triangle of numbers in the 1200s. He said it came from an older mathematician, Jia Xian.
Keeper's note:
> Each number is the two above it added. One row reads 1, 4, 6, 4, 1. That 6 is the number of ways to pick two ribbon colors from four. Later, in Europe, the triangle was named after Blaise Pascal.

## Bench (a): The Gate Row

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> The gate is one long row. A part of 2, 3 or 4 ribbons repeats. Bramble tied every row.

**Opening:** What comes next on the gate? Find the part that repeats.
**Buttons:** Pick a ribbon · Tie it on · Show the part · Swap the groups
**After a pick:** Look along the gate. Does your ribbon keep the pattern?
**Mend mode opening:** One ribbon breaks the pattern. Tap it.

**Guided item:** Red, blue, red, blue, red. What comes next?
- Step 1: Say the colors aloud. *(Red, blue, red, blue, red.)*
- Step 2: Which part comes again? *(Red, blue.)*
- Step 3: After red comes blue. Tie a blue ribbon.

**Swap the groups card:** Three red bows sit on the left. Two blue bows sit on the right. Tap **Swap**. Same bows? Same amount?

## Bench (b): Growing Ribbons

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> Ribbon squares in a row. Squares side by side share a ribbon. Bramble's ribbons never run out.

**Opening:** Build the next step. What does each new step add?
**Buttons:** Next step · Show what's new · Make a table · Say the rule
**Rule builder:** Start with ___ ribbons. Each new step adds ___.
**Second pattern (for the ladder):** ribbon triangles in a row: 3, 5, 7 ribbons.

**Guided item:** One square takes 4 ribbons. Two squares take 7. Why not 8?
- Step 1: Look where the squares touch. *(They share a side.)*
- Step 2: The second square needs only 3 new ribbons.
- Step 3: 4 + 3 = 7.

## Bench (c): The Magpie's Table

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> Each post gets the same number of ribbons. The magpie keeps count in a table.

**Opening:** Fill the table. What pattern do you see down the column?
**Table headers:** Posts · Ribbons
**Buttons:** Add a row · Split the posts · Two rules · Plot the pairs
**Two rules opening:** Bramble ties 3 a post. The magpie ties 6. Compare each pair.
**Grid labels:** Across: Bramble's ribbons · Up: the magpie's ribbons

**Guided item:** 4 ribbons on each post. How many on 5 posts?
- Step 1: Read down the column: 4, 8, 12, 16.
- Step 2: Each post adds 4. *(16 + 4 = 20.)*
- Step 3: Check: 20 is even, like every 4s number.

## Bench (d): The Number Ribbon

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> A long ribbon marked with numbers, zero in the middle. Below zero runs to the left.

**Opening:** Lay equal steps along the ribbon. Say the start and the step.
**Buttons:** Add a step · Show differences · Position table · Test a number
**Position table headers:** Position n · 0 · 1 · 2 · 3 · 4
**Test a number prompt:** Type a number. Is it on this sequence?

**Guided item:** The sequence goes 7, 4, 1. What comes next?
- Step 1: Find the step. *(7 to 4: subtract 3.)*
- Step 2: Keep the same step past zero.
- Step 3: 1 − 3 = −2.

## Bench (e): Every Way to Choose

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> Bramble's hat box: three hats and some ribbons. An outfit is one hat with one ribbon.

**Opening:** List every outfit. How will you know you have them all?
**Buttons:** Hold this one still · Next choice · Show the grid · Show the tree
**Rhythm strip (Keeper's Desk):** Tap short (one beat) or long (two beats) to fill the strip.

**Guided item:** Three hats, two ribbons. List every outfit.
- Step 1: Hold the patchwork hat still. *(Red, then gold.)*
- Step 2: Now the black hat. *(Red, then gold.)*
- Step 3: Now the green hat. Six outfits, none twice.

- B. Step 3 takes 10 ribbons, and 3 × 3 + 1 = 10. *(tests: one check as a reason)*
- C. The pattern starts with 3 ribbons. *(tests: the number before n as the start)*
Hint: What does each new square add?
Explanation: The number before n is the step. The + 1 is the zeroth term.
Feedback A: Yes. And the 1 is the ribbon you start with.
Feedback B: That checks one step. Why does the 3 work for every step?
Feedback C: The first square takes 4 ribbons. What does each new one add?

**D6 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: Find the nth term of 3, 7, 11, 15.
Answer: 4n − 1.
Worked steps: 1. The step is 4, so 4n. 2. 4n gives 4, 8, 12, 16. 3. Each term is 1 less: 4n − 1. 4. Check n = 1: 4 − 1 = 3.
Tests: n + 4 (using the step as the whole rule); 4n + 3 (using the first term as the constant).
Hint: Step first. Then compare 4n with the real terms.
Explanation: The zeroth term is 3 − 4 = −1. So 4n − 1.

**D7 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Is 100 in the sequence 3, 7, 11, 15?
- A. No. 4n − 1 = 100 gives n = 25.25. *(right)*
- B. Yes. 100 is in the 4 times table. *(tests: "multiples of 4" read as "goes up by 4")*
- C. Yes. It's about the 25th term. *(tests: rounding the position)*
Hint: Set 4n − 1 equal to 100.
Explanation: A position must be a whole number. 99 is in, as the 25th term.
Feedback A: Yes. No whole position, so 100 isn't in.
Feedback B: These numbers are 1 less than the 4s. Is 100?
Feedback C: There's no 25.25th ribbon. Positions are whole numbers.

**D8 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25. What kind of sequence is it?
- A. Arithmetic, because it goes up. *(tests: classifying by "goes up")*
- B. Neither. Its steps keep changing. *(right)*
- C. Geometric, because the numbers grow fast. *(tests: fast means multiplying)*
Hint: Write the differences. Then the multipliers.
Explanation: Differences 3, 5, 7, 9 change. So do multipliers. These are the square numbers.
Feedback A: Write the differences: 3, 5, 7. Are they the same?
Feedback B: Yes. A square number pattern, neither kind.
Feedback C: 4 is 1 × 4, but 9 isn't 4 × 4.

### Set E. Every Way to Choose (Seedling, Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**E1 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Three hats and two ribbons. How many outfits?
- A. 5 *(tests: adding the choices)*
- B. 6 *(right)*
- C. 3 *(tests: one ribbon for each hat)*
Hint: Hold one hat still. How many ribbons go with it?
Explanation: Each hat goes with 2 ribbons. 2 + 2 + 2 = 6.
Feedback A: 3 + 2 counts hats and ribbons, not outfits.
Feedback B: Yes. Each hat, every ribbon.
Feedback C: Each hat can wear either ribbon.

**E2 (Seedling, order)**
Prompt: List every outfit. Start with the patchwork hat.
Answer: patchwork and red, patchwork and gold, black and red, black and gold, green and red, green and gold.
Tests: repeating an outfit; changing hat and ribbon at once.
Hint: Hold the patchwork hat still. Try each ribbon.
Explanation: Hold, run through, move on. Six outfits, none twice.

**E3 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: 3 knots and 4 ribbon colors. How many knot-and-color bows?
Answer: 12.
Worked steps: 1. Grid: 3 rows (knots), 4 columns (colors). 2. 3 × 4 = 12.
Tests: 7 (adding 3 and 4).
Hint: Draw a grid. Count rows of 4.
Explanation: Each knot makes 4 bows. 4 + 4 + 4 = 3 × 4.

**E4 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Pip lists 10 outfits from 3 hats and 4 ribbons. Is he done?
- A. Yes. Ten outfits is plenty. *(tests: stopping before the list is complete)*
- B. No. A 3 by 4 grid has 12, so 2 are missing. *(right)*
- C. Nobody can tell. Keep guessing until he gets bored. *(tests: no way to check completeness)*
Hint: How many squares in a 3 by 4 grid?
Explanation: The grid shows exactly which two are missing.
Feedback A: Plenty isn't all. How many does the grid hold?
Feedback B: Yes. The grid's empty squares show which two.
Feedback C: You can tell. Rows times columns.

**E5 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: A bow uses two different colors: red, blue, gold, green. How many bows?
Answer: 6.
Worked steps: 1. Hold red: red-blue, red-gold, red-green. 2. Hold blue: blue-gold, blue-green. Red-blue is done. 3. Hold gold: gold-green. 4. 3 + 2 + 1 = 6.
Tests: 12 (counting red-blue and blue-red as two); 16 (the full grid, with same-color pairs).
Hint: Red-blue and blue-red make the same bow.
Explanation: Order doesn't matter here. Each pair once: 6.

**E6 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: Short beats take 1, long beats take 2. How many 5-beat rhythms?
Answer: 8.
Worked steps: 1. No longs: 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 (1 way). 2. One long: it can sit in 4 places (4 ways). 3. Two longs: long, long, short in 3 orders (3 ways). 4. 1 + 4 + 3 = 8.
Tests: 5 (the 4-beat count); 6 (missing some orders).
Hint: Sort them by how many long beats.
Explanation: Sorted by long beats, nothing is missed or counted twice.

**E7 (Keeper's Desk, true / not true from this source)**
Source: cards H1 and H2.
Prompt: Tap each sentence: true from these cards, or not from these cards.
- "Poets in India counted rhythms: 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21." *(true from these cards)*
- "Fibonacci was the first person to find these numbers." *(not from these cards: India's poets listed them earlier)*
- "Fibonacci's rabbits are a pretend rule." *(true from these cards)*
- "Real rabbit families grow exactly like this." *(not from these cards: the cards say they don't)*
Hint: Read both cards. Who knew the numbers first?
Explanation: The numbers come from more than one place and time.

**E8 (Keeper's Desk, say why; spoken to a grown-up, not stored)**
Prompt: 5 beats make 8 rhythms; 4 beats make 5. Why do 6 make 13?
Model answer for the grown-up: A 6-beat rhythm ends with a short beat or a long beat. If it ends short, the rest is any 5-beat rhythm: 8. If it ends long, the rest is any 4-beat rhythm: 5. 8 + 5 = 13.
Tests: guessing the next number without a reason.
Hint: How can a 6-beat rhythm end?
Explanation: Each count is the two before it added, and now you can say why.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **pattern**: something that repeats or grows by a rule.
- **repeating part (unit of repeat)**: the piece of a pattern that comes again and again.
- **growing pattern**: a pattern that gets bigger each step.
- **rule**: words or numbers that tell you how to build a pattern.
- **sequence**: numbers in order that follow a rule.
- **term**: one number in a sequence.
- **term-to-term rule**: a start and a step, to get the next term.
- **position-to-term rule**: a rule that turns a position n into its term, like 3n + 1.
- **nth term**: the term in position n.
- **common difference**: the same step between every pair of terms.
- **zeroth term**: the term one step before the first.
- **arithmetic sequence**: a sequence that adds the same amount each time.
- **geometric sequence**: a sequence that multiplies by the same amount each time.
- **ordered pair**: two numbers in brackets, across first, then up.
- **systematic list**: a list made in an order, so nothing is missed or repeated.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** spotting and extending repeating patterns by their repeating part; noticing what stays the same when groups swap; growing patterns and rules in words; testing a rule on a new case; table patterns used as checks; deriving a fact from known facts; comparing two rules and plotting pairs; linear sequences with decimal and negative steps; position-to-term rules and the nth term, with why the number before n is the common difference; deciding whether a number is in a sequence; systematic listing and knowing when a list is complete; a first look at square numbers, the triangle of numbers and the rhythm counts.

**What it does not claim:** the gate, ribbons and models are made up. It does not teach shape symmetry or tiling (the Sign Painter does), times-table fluency, or solving equations in general. Square and triangular numbers and the rhythm counts are previews only. Plotting pairs assumes the explorer can already read a coordinate grid.
