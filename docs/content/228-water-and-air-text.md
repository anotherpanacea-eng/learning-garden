# Water and Air: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 228](../specs/228-water-and-air.md). Use as written. `{guide}` is the chosen companion; the explorer is "you". US spelling, metric first with US units where a US reader needs them, no em-dashes. Facts marked [check] stay out of the build until the source audit clears them. Before the end of Act III the old Keeper is never named.

## Arriving

Hook (A story from Willowbank): The librarian has dusted off the old globe. "Most of this is water," they say. "And all of it wears a coat of air."

Doors:
- **Where the Water Is**: Find the water, seen and hidden.
- **The Hundred Cups**: How much is salty? How much is fresh?

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. Water everywhere (Act I, chapter 3: investigate and plant)

**Short:** After the storm, water is everywhere. "Where did it all come from?" asks Pip. "And where is it going?" asks Dot.

**Longer:** The morning after the storm, every rut in the lane held a puddle. Willow Brook ran brown and fast under the footbridge. The millpond was full to the brim.

"Water everywhere," said Pip, shaking one paw, then the other.

"Not just where you can see it," said {guide}. "Some is soaking down into the ground right now. Some is frozen up on the peaks."

Dot peered over the edge of the old well. Far down, something glinted. "There's water down there too. But nobody poured it in."

"It seeps through the ground and gathers," said {guide}. "Let's make a list of every place water hides."

Pip found the biggest puddle and sat in it. "Here's one."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** Snow and ice are water too, just frozen solid. Some of the brook's water starts as snow melting on the mountains.

### Scene 2. A balloon that weighs something (Act II, chapter 3: Ember's birthday)

**Short:** Ember's balloons bob over the café. "Air weighs nothing," says Pip. The baker makes a balance from a stick.

**Longer:** The café was strung with balloons for Ember's birthday. Pip batted one into the air.

"Air weighs nothing," he said. "That's why balloons float."

"These balloons don't float," said Ember. "They sink. Slowly. Watch."

The baker hung a long stick from a string, level as a seesaw. He tied a flat balloon to one end and a blown-up balloon to the other.

The stick tipped, just a little, toward the full balloon.

"The air inside is squeezed in tight," said {guide}. "So there's a tiny bit more air in there. And air has weight."

Pip stared at the balloon as if it had tricked him. Then he asked whether there was cake.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** Air is made of real stuff. The air in a big room weighs about as much as a grown-up person. We don't feel it because it presses on us from every side.

### Scene 3. Rain from the sea (Act II, chapter 2: a garden across the seasons)

**Short:** A soft rain blows in from the west. "That's sea rain," says the orchard keeper. "The sea is far away," says Dot.

**Longer:** The orchard keeper looked west and sniffed. "Rain by noon. Sea rain."

"The sea is days away," said Dot. "How can its rain get here?"

"Not the sea itself," said {guide}. "The Sun warms the ocean. Some water rises into the air as invisible vapor. The wind carries it a long, long way."

"Then it cools and makes clouds," said the orchard keeper, "and drops on my trees."

"So the brook," said Pip slowly, "used to be ocean?"

"Some of it, once," said {guide}. "Most of the water that rises into the air rises from the oceans."

The rain arrived at noon, exactly as promised. The orchard keeper did not say she told them so. She just smiled at the trees.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** The ocean is the biggest water store on Earth. Its warm surface feeds most of the water vapor that becomes clouds and rain.

### Scene 4. Cold on the way up (Act III, chapter 3: the route by the high ford)

**Short:** The path to the high ford climbs and climbs. The higher it goes, the colder the air gets.

**Longer:** The courier's map showed the old path to Vadoalto, marked here and there by faded ribbons. It climbed past the last orchard, through beech woods, and up onto open grass.

"It was warmer down in Willowbank," said Pip, "so why is my nose cold up here?"

"We're high up," said {guide}. "Air gets colder the higher you climb, in the low part of the sky where weather happens."

Above them, snow lay in the shadows of the peaks.

"Whatever the time of year," said Dot, "up here is colder than down in the valley."

"The mountains have their own climate," said {guide}. "Every few hundred meters you climb, the trees change."

The beech woods gave way to pines, and the pines gave way to grass and rock. Pip said he would like to go back to the beech woods, where it was warmer and there might be lunch.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** In mountains, the climate changes with height, a little like traveling toward the poles. There are snowy peaks near the Equator.

### Scene 5. Snow up high, river down low (Act III, chapter 4: letters up and down the river)

**Short:** Nuria writes from Vadoalto. "Our spring flood starts as snow. Where does your water start?"

**Longer:** The courier brought Nuria's letter, with an otter's paw print by her name.

*La nieve de arriba es el río de abajo.* (The snow up high is the river down low.)

"In Vadoalto," she wrote, "the brook rises every spring when the snow on the peaks melts. My job is to watch the snow as well as the river."

"So snow is a kind of water bank," said Dot. "Water saved up for later."

"Fresh water," said {guide}. "Not salty like the sea. And most of Earth's fresh water is frozen, or hidden underground."

Pip suggested they write back and ask whether Vadoalto had snow to spare. Dot wrote back and asked a better question: how much fresh water is there, anyway?

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** Only a small share of Earth's water is fresh. Most of that fresh water is in ice and snow, or underground. Rivers and lakes hold only a tiny part.

### Scene 6. The wet corner keeps its carbon (Act IV, chapter 2: a puzzling change at the bottom of the orchard)

**Short:** Reeds grow in the orchard's wet corner. The soil there is dark and soft. "It's full of carbon," says the librarian. Bramble has a fair answer.

**Longer:** At the bottom of the orchard, the low corner stayed wet even in dry weeks. Reeds nobody planted grew there now, thick and green.

The librarian knelt and pressed a finger into the soil. It was dark, nearly black, and soft as cake.

"Old reeds," they said. "When wet plants die, they rot very slowly, so they pile up. That dark soil is full of carbon the reeds took from the air."

"Then the old Sponge marsh held carbon too," said {guide}.

Bramble folded her arms. "So do apple trees. And that black soil took ages to pile up. A new marsh wouldn't make much of it for a long, long time."

"That's true," said the librarian. "Peat grows very slowly. You're right on both."

"So carbon doesn't settle it?" asked Dot.

"It's one fact," said {guide}. "The town will weigh a lot of facts."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** Wet soils called peat store huge amounts of carbon. When peat is drained, it dries, rots faster and gives carbon back to the air.

## Teach cards

Each worked example reveals one step per tap.

**T1. Water is in many places** (A true thing about science). Water is in oceans, rivers, lakes, ice and snow, and under the ground. Some you can see. Some is hidden.
Worked example: (1) The brook: water you can see. (2) The well: water under the ground. (3) Snow on the peaks: water frozen solid.

**T2. Most water is in the oceans.** Look at the globe. Blue covers most of it. The oceans hold far more water than all the rivers and lakes.
Worked example: (1) Find the land. (2) Find the blue. (3) Which is bigger? The blue.

**T3. Ice is water too.** Ice and snow are water frozen solid. Warm them and they melt into liquid water. Same water, two forms.
Worked example: (1) Ice on the millpond. (2) The Sun comes out. (3) It melts into water.

**T4. Land and water shapes** (A true thing about science). An ocean is huge and salty. A lake has land all around it. A river flows downhill to the sea. A glacier is a river of ice.
Worked example: (1) Willow Brook flows downhill. (2) So it is a river. (3) The millpond has land all around. (4) So it is like a small lake.

**T5. Salty or fresh.** Ocean water is salty. Rivers, most lakes, ice and the water in most wells are fresh: not salty. People drink fresh water.
Worked example: (1) Melt glacier ice. (2) Taste? No need: it was never sea water. (3) Glacier ice is fresh.

**T6. The hundred cups.** Pretend all Earth's water fills 100 cups. About 97 cups are salty. About 3 cups are fresh.
Worked example: (1) 100 cups in all. (2) 97 salty. (3) 100 − 97 = 3 fresh.

**T7. Where the fresh water is.** Of the 3 fresh cups, about 2 are frozen ice and snow. About 1 is underground. Rivers and lakes: less than a spoonful.
Worked example: (1) 3 fresh cups. (2) 2 are ice. (3) 3 − 2 = about 1 underground. (4) Rivers and lakes are a tiny sliver.

**T8. A graph shows a share.** A bar or slice shows a share of the whole, not a size you could hold. Read what the whole is first.
Worked example: (1) The whole is all Earth's water. (2) The ocean bar reaches 97. (3) So 97 of every 100 parts.

**T9. Climate bands.** Near the Equator, sunlight hits most directly: hot all year. Near the poles, it hits at a low slant: very cold. In between: temperate, with four seasons.
Worked example: (1) Shine a flashlight straight down: a small bright spot. (2) Tilt it: the same light spreads wide. (3) Spread light warms less.

**T10. Deserts are dry.** A desert is a place with very little rain or snow. Some deserts are hot. Some are freezing cold.
Worked example: (1) The Sahara: hot and dry. (2) Antarctica: cold and dry. (3) Both are deserts.

**T11. Height matters too.** Air gets colder as you climb a mountain. A high peak can be snowy even near the Equator.
Worked example: (1) Lowland near the Equator: hot. (2) Climb high. (3) Colder and colder. (4) Snow at the top.

**T12. Climate is the long pattern.** A climate zone is the usual weather over many years, not today's weather. Zones blend into each other gradually.
Worked example: (1) One snowy week in Willowbank. (2) Most years: mild, four seasons. (3) Willowbank stays temperate.

**T13. Air is real stuff.** Air has weight. It presses on everything, from every side: down, up and sideways.
Worked example: (1) A flat balloon. (2) A full one. (3) The full one tips the scale, just a little.

**T14. A thin coat.** The atmosphere is the air around Earth. It is very thin compared with Earth. Almost all weather happens in its lowest layer.
Worked example: (1) A globe 30 cm (12 in) across. (2) The weather layer: about two sheets of paper thick. (3) That's very thin.

**T15. Two jobs of the air.** High up, the ozone layer soaks up much of the Sun's harmful ultraviolet light. Some gases also hold in warmth, and ozone is one of them. Two jobs.
Worked example: (1) Ultraviolet comes in. (2) Ozone high up soaks up much of it. (3) A second job: some gases, ozone too, hold warmth near the ground.

**T16. The ocean stores warmth.** Water warms up slowly and cools down slowly. So the sea keeps coasts milder: cooler summers, warmer winters.
Worked example: (1) Summer: the sea stays cooler than the land. (2) Winter: the sea stays warmer. (3) The coast swings less.

**T17. The ocean makes rain.** The Sun warms the ocean. Water evaporates into vapor. Wind carries it. It cools into clouds and rain.
Worked example: (1) Sun warms the sea. (2) Vapor rises. (3) Wind blows it inland. (4) Rain falls on the valley.

**T18. Currents carry warmth.** Big ocean currents move warm water from near the Equator toward the poles. Some coasts are milder than their place on the globe suggests.
Worked example: (1) Warm water near the Equator. (2) A current carries it north. (3) Winds blow over it. (4) Nearby coasts are milder.

**T19. Fuels come from nature.** Wood, coal, oil and gas are fuels. Wind, sunlight and flowing water are sources too. All come from natural resources.
Worked example: (1) The baker burns wood. (2) Wood comes from trees. (3) Trees are a natural resource.

**T20. Using them changes things.** Every source does something to the world around it. Burning fuel gives off smoke and carbon dioxide. A dam floods land.
Worked example: (1) Coal is dug from mines. (2) Burned, it gives off carbon dioxide. (3) Two effects from one fuel.

**T21. Forests store carbon.** A growing tree takes carbon dioxide from the air and builds wood from its carbon. A standing forest keeps that carbon out of the air.
Worked example: (1) Air has carbon dioxide. (2) Leaves take it in. (3) The carbon becomes wood.

**T22. The flip.** The same forest can store carbon or release it. Burned or cleared, its carbon goes back into the air.
Worked example: (1) Standing forest: carbon stored. (2) Forest burned. (3) Carbon dioxide into the air. (4) No more taking it in.

**T23. Carbon goes round** (Keeper's Desk). Carbon moves between air, plants, animals, soil and fuel. It is never used up. Plants take it in; living things and fires give it back.
Worked example: (1) A reed takes in carbon dioxide. (2) A vole eats the reed. (3) The vole breathes out carbon dioxide. (4) Back in the air.

**T24. Balance and tipping** (Keeper's Desk). In nature, taking in and giving back roughly balance. Burning old fuel adds carbon faster than plants take it back.
Worked example: (1) Plants take in 3. (2) Living things give back 3. (3) Balanced. (4) Burn fuel: add 1 more each turn.

## History cards

**Water from under the desert (A true thing from history).** In Persia, now Iran, people dug qanats: long tunnels that carry underground water downhill to towns and fields. Some are very old. Some still flow today.

**Keeper's note on the qanats (A true thing from history).** Diggers sank a line of wells and joined them with a gently sloping tunnel. Villages shared the water by long-agreed rules. Eleven qanats in Iran are now World Heritage sites.

**The mercury on the mountain (A true thing from history).** In 1648, in France, Florin Périer carried a glass tube of mercury up the Puy de Dôme, for Blaise Pascal. At the top, the mercury stood lower. Less air pressed down up there.

**Keeper's note on Périer (A true thing from history).** One tube stayed in the town below as a check. The summit tube read about 8 cm lower (computed from the recorded readings). Pascal said the weight of the air explained it. Not every scholar agreed at first.

**Plants in bands (A true thing from history).** In 1802, Alexander von Humboldt and Aimé Bonpland climbed high on Chimborazo, a snowy volcano in Ecuador, near the Equator. Humboldt later drew the mountain with its plants in bands by height.

**The warm river in the sea (A true thing from history).** In the late 1760s, Benjamin Franklin asked his cousin Timothy Folger, a Nantucket whaling captain, why some ships crossed the Atlantic slowly. Folger sketched the Gulf Stream. Franklin later measured how warm its water was.

**Two jars in the sun (A true thing from history).** In 1856, Eunice Foote put air in one glass jar and carbon dioxide in another. In sunlight, the carbon dioxide jar got hotter. She said more of that gas in the air would make Earth warmer.

**The curve on the mountain (A true thing from history).** In 1958, Charles David Keeling began measuring carbon dioxide on Mauna Loa, Hawaii. The record goes on today. The amount has risen year after year.

**Keeper's note on Keeling (A true thing from history).** Keeling's first reading was about 313 parts in a million. Before the 1800s it was about 280. Now it is more than 420: half as much again as before the 1800s.

### Set 1. Where the water is (Sprout)

**1.1 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Where is most of Earth's water?
- A. In rivers and lakes *(misconception: most-water-in-rivers)*
- B. In the oceans *(right)*
- C. In the clouds *(misconception: clouds-hold-most-water)*
Hint: Spin the globe. What color covers most of it?
Feedback: A: Rivers and lakes are small next to the sea. B: Yes. The oceans hold most water by far. C: Clouds hold only a little water.
Explanation: The oceans hold far more water than everything else.

**1.2 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Is the ice on the mountain water?
- A. No. Ice is not water. *(misconception: ice-not-water)*
- B. Only after it melts. *(misconception: ice-not-water, partial)*
- C. Yes. It is water frozen solid. *(right)*
Hint: What happens to ice when the Sun warms it?
Feedback: A: Ice is water, just frozen. B: It was water all along. C: Yes. Same water, frozen.
Explanation: Ice and snow are water in solid form.

**1.3 (Sprout, sort)**
Prompt: Sort each card. Liquid or solid?
Cards: the sea · Willow Brook · snow on the peaks · ice on the millpond · water in the well · a glacier
Answer: liquid: the sea, Willow Brook, water in the well; solid: snow on the peaks, ice on the millpond, a glacier.
Misconception tested: ice-not-water (leaving frozen cards out).
Hint: Can you pour it? Then it is liquid.
Explanation: All six are water. Three are liquid and three are frozen solid.

**1.4 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: No rain fell today. Where does the well's water come from?
- A. From under the ground *(right)*
- B. The well makes water *(misconception: water-made-new)*
- C. Somebody pours it in *(misconception: no-water-underground)*
Hint: What is all around the bottom of the well?
Feedback: A: Yes. Water seeps through soil and rock into the well. B: Wells can't make water. It gathers there. C: Nobody pours it. It comes from the ground.
Explanation: There is water under the ground, held in soil and rock.

**1.5 (Sprout, match)**
Prompt: Match each word to its picture.
Words: ocean · river · lake · glacier
Pictures: wide salty water to the sky's edge · water flowing downhill · water with land all around · a slow river of ice
Answer: ocean → wide salty water; river → flowing downhill; lake → land all around; glacier → river of ice.
Misconception tested: shapes of land and water mixed up (lake and river).
Hint: Which one moves downhill?
Explanation: Each word names a shape of water on the land.

**1.6 (Sprout, say why, to a grown-up)**
Prompt: Tell a grown-up two places water can hide.
Answer to listen for: underground (a well, a spring), frozen (snow, ice), or in the air as vapor.
Misconception tested: water-only-where-seen.
Hint: Think of the well, and the peaks.
Explanation: Water hides under the ground, in snow and ice, and in the air.

**2.3 (Sapling, number with steps)**
Given: 3 fresh cups. About 2 are ice. Rivers and lakes are less than a spoonful.

## "I'm stuck" worked examples (step 2 of the ladder)

- **Where water is:** A puddle freezes at night. Is it still water? Start: what happens when it warms?
- **Underground water:** A spring bubbles out of a hillside. Where was the water? Start: look below the grass.
- **Salty or fresh:** Rain falls on a lake. Salty or fresh? Start: was it ever sea water?
- **Hundred cups:** 10 cups, 7 salty. How many fresh? Start: 10 − 7 = 3.
- **Graph share:** A bar reaches 50 out of 100. Start: that's half of the whole.
- **Climate bands:** A flashlight straight down, then tilted. Start: which spot is brighter?
- **Desert:** A cold place with almost no snowfall. Desert or not? Start: is it dry?
- **Air:** A ball pumped full, then let down. Start: which holds more air?
- **Layers:** A cloud floats low. Which layer? Start: the lowest.
- **Ocean engine:** A pond and a stone in the Sun. Which warms faster? Start: touch the stone.
- **Room of air:** A box 2 m by 1 m by 1 m. Start: 2 × 1 × 1 = 2 cubic meters, so 2 × 1.2 = 2.4 kg.
- **Fuel cards:** Wood. Where from, and what does burning do? Start: trees; smoke.
- **Carbon tokens:** Air 5, add 1 each turn for 2 turns. Start: 5 + 2 = 7.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Ocean:** the huge body of salty water that covers most of Earth.
- **River:** water flowing downhill in a channel, toward a lake or the sea.
- **Lake:** water with land all around it.
- **Glacier:** a huge, slow-moving river of ice.
- **Groundwater:** water under the ground, filling gaps in soil and rock.
- **Spring:** a place where groundwater comes out of the ground by itself.
- **Solid:** keeps its own shape, like ice.
- **Liquid:** flows and takes the shape of its container, like water.
- **Water vapor:** water as an invisible gas in the air.
- **Fresh water:** water that is not salty.
- **Salt water:** water with salt in it, like the sea.
- **Share:** a part of a whole.
- **Bar graph:** a picture that shows amounts as bars of different heights.
- **Equator:** the imaginary line around the middle of Earth.
- **Latitude:** how far a place is from the Equator, toward a pole.
- **Climate:** the usual weather of a place over many years.
- **Climate zone:** a large area with the same kind of climate.
- **Tropical:** hot all year, near the Equator.
- **Temperate:** mild, with four seasons.
- **Polar:** freezing cold, near the North or South Pole.
- **Arid:** very dry.
- **Desert:** a place with very little rain or snow. Hot or cold.
- **Atmosphere:** the layer of air around Earth.
- **Troposphere:** the lowest layer of air, where almost all weather happens.
- **Stratosphere:** the layer above, where the ozone layer is.
- **Ozone layer:** a layer of gas high up that soaks up much of the Sun's harmful ultraviolet light.
- **Ultraviolet (UV):** invisible sunlight that can harm living things.
- **Air pressure:** the push of air on everything around it.
- **Evaporate:** to change from liquid water into water vapor.
- **Current:** a big, steady flow of water in the ocean.
- **Mercury:** a heavy metal that is liquid, used in old air-pressure tubes.
- **Dam:** a wall built across a river to hold back water.
- **Wind turbine:** a tall machine with blades that the wind turns to make electricity.
- **Solar panel:** a flat panel that turns sunlight into electricity.
- **Natural resource:** something from nature that people use.
- **Fuel:** something burned to release energy, like wood or coal.
- **Fossil fuel:** coal, oil or gas, made from living things buried millions of years ago.
- **Carbon:** a building block of every living thing, and of fuels.
- **Carbon dioxide:** a gas in the air, made of carbon and oxygen.
- **Carbon store:** a place that holds carbon, like a forest or peat.
- **Peat:** dark, wet soil made of old plants that rotted slowly.
- **Photosynthesis:** how plants use sunlight to make food from carbon dioxide and water.
- **Respiration:** how living things release energy from food, giving out carbon dioxide.
- **Decompose:** to rot and break down.
- **Deforestation:** clearing forests for good.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** where water is on Earth, seen and hidden, and that ice and snow are water; how much is salty and fresh, shown as 100 cups and a bar graph; Earth's climate zones and why they differ (sunlight, height, rain); that air weighs something and pushes in every direction, and the atmosphere's thin, layered shell; the ocean's three climate jobs; where fuels come from and what using them does; forests and wet soils as carbon stores that can flip to sources; and, at the Keeper's Desk, the carbon cycle as a balance that burning old fuel tips.

**What it does not claim:** the cups, coast towns, zone bench and carbon tokens are made-up models with stated rules, not measurements. The zone bench leaves out winds, seas and rain shadows. Warming from added carbon dioxide is introduced gently, without the term "climate change" and without any forecast. The Sponge marsh is Willowbank fiction, and its carbon is one fact the town weighs, never the answer to its choice.

**Safety:** Everything in this pack happens on the screen. Off screen, a grown-up leads, picks the place and keeps everyone away from deep or fast water.
