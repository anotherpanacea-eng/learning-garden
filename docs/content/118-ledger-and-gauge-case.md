Companion to [spec 118](../specs/118-keepers-desk-entry.md) (conventions, records and acceptance) and [spec 120](https://github.com/anotherpanacea-eng/learning-garden/blob/claude/project-thread-zpfie6/docs/specs/120-keeper-of-the-ways.md) (Act IV, Hollis's pages 2 and 4, Nuria's rain gauge letter, the three plans). Case ID `ledger-and-gauge`. Appears in Act IV once the explorer has page 4 (the Sponge drained in year 22) and Nuria's rain gauge letter. This is the "full dossier" spec 120 names for Act IV's older learner.

## 1. Why this case

Act IV's question is the one the whole story has been building to: did draining the Sponge make the floods worse, or was it just wetter years? The records do not decide the cause. The desk asks the explorer to distinguish recorded counts and heights from possible explanations, then write for a meeting where people want a simpler answer. Rain, drainage, channel changes, recording practices and other possibilities remain open; no reply steers the explorer to "probably both".

The reasoning is the hard kind: two small records, made by different people for different purposes, two changes made in the same year, and data that is missing on purpose ("Years not written had no flood worth the ink").

## 2. Hook

> Wren spreads three things across the desk: Hollis's flood ledger, Hollis's page about the year of the fine harvest, and Nuria's copied rain gauge.
>
> "The meeting is on Thursday. Half the town says it's the weather. Half says it's the Sponge. Bramble says it's both, loudly, which isn't helping. I'd like one page that says what these records actually show, and what they don't."

## 4. Steps

1. **What does the ledger show?** (reading data, the mean, before and after)
2. **Put the gauge beside it** (comparing groups, matching like with like)
3. **What do the records support?** (correlation, cause and confounding)
4. **Your page for the meeting** (argument with an objection, an uncertainty and a next record)

Steps open in any order.

## 5. Step 1: What does the ledger show?

**Learn card** (Marble research for Calculating the Mean and Comparing measurements):

> The **mean** shares a total out evenly: add the values, divide by how many there are.
>
> To compare two groups, say **how many** are in each and give a typical value **with** the spread. A mean from two values tells you much less than a mean from fifty.
>
> Worked example: heights 30, 50 and 70. Total 150. Three values. Mean 150 ÷ 3 = 50.

**Chart:** the seven floods as bars along a 40-year line, with a dashed line at year 22 labelled *the Sponge drained*. The explorer can turn the storm's 160 cm on or off; it is drawn in a different style and labelled *after Hollis's keeping*.

**Guided first task (1a):** the bars left of the dashed line are highlighted and counted.

Tasks (Check my working):

- **1a.** How many recorded floods before year 22, and how many after? *Answer: **2** before (years 4 and 15); **5** after (27, 31, 34, 36, 38).*
- **1b.** Mean height before year 22. *Answer: (40 + 60) ÷ 2 = **50 cm**.*
- **1c.** Mean height after year 22, in Hollis's keeping. *Answer: (120 + 80 + 120 + 140 + 100) ÷ 5 = 560 ÷ 5 = **112 cm**.*
- **1d.** The years before the drain run from 1 to 21, which is 21 years. After, 22 to 40, which is 19 years. Choose the fairest sentence:
  - "There were 2 recorded floods in 21 years before, and 5 in 19 years after." ← fits the ledger
  - "Floods were more than twice as common after, because 5 is more than twice 2."
  - "Floods were exactly as common before and after, once you count the years."

  Reply to the second: "5 is more than twice 2, and the after-period is a little shorter too, so that's roughly right. Saying it with both counts and both lengths lets the reader check." Reply to the third: "21 years and 19 years are close, but 2 floods and 5 floods aren't."
- **1e.** "How sure can we be of the before mean, 50 cm?" Choices: "Very sure: it's a proper mean."; "Not very: it comes from just two floods."; "Not at all: means don't work on old records." The second gets: "Yes. Two values make a mean, but one more flood could move it a lot." The others get a reply pointing to how many values it uses.

**I'm stuck (1c):**

1. *Hint:* "Add the five heights after year 22. Then share the total between five."
2. *Similar example:* "Heights 20, 40 and 60: total 120, three floods, mean 120 ÷ 3 = 40. Now add 120, 80, 120, 140 and 100."
3. **Skip for now.**

**Replies to likely errors:**

| Entered | Misconception | Reply |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 120 for 1c | Taking the most common or middle value as the mean, or including the storm (720 ÷ 6) | "120 is the most common height and the middle one, and it's also what you get if the storm sneaks in. The storm came after Hollis's keeping. Share the five heights from Hollis's years." |
| 140 for 1c | Choosing the largest | "That's the highest flood. The mean is the level they'd all be if shared out evenly." |
| 560 for 1c | Stopping at the total | "That's all five heights added. Now share the total between the five floods." |
| 50 cm read as "floods were 50 cm higher after" | Comparing a mean with a difference | "50 is the before mean. How much higher is 112 than 50?" (62 cm) |

## 6. Step 2: Put the gauge beside it

**Learn card:**

> Two records are easier to compare when they're on the same grid. Nuria counted rain in tens of years, so count the floods the same way.
>
> The **same count** of big-rain events is not the same amount of rain. Six short showers and six much longer storms could make different totals. This table gives no intensity, duration, local ground conditions or stated threshold for big rain. It cannot hold rainfall constant while we test another cause.

**Guided first task (2a):** the first column of the table below, filled together.

Tasks:

- **2a.** Fill the table: floods and the highest flood in each ten years. *Answers:*

  | Years | 1 to 10 | 11 to 20 | 21 to 30 | 31 to 40 |
  | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
  | Big rains | 6 | 5 | 6 | 8 |
  | Recorded floods | **1** | **1** | **1** | **4** |
  | Highest flood (cm) | **40** | **60** | **120** | **140** |

- **2b.** "Find two stretches of years with the same number of big rains." *Answer: **years 1 to 10 and 21 to 30**, both 6.*
- **2c.** "What was different about the floods in those two stretches?" *Answer: the same number of floods (1 each), but **40 cm** against **120 cm**: three times as high.*
- **2d.** "Which change does Hollis's page explicitly record?" Choices:
  - "The Sponge was drained, and the brook was straightened." ← fits Hollis's page
  - "The rain: the later years were much wetter."
  - "Nothing changed, so the difference is a mistake."

  Reply to the second: "The event counts are equal, but we do not know their rainfall totals. This answer needs another record; it is not ruled out by these counts." Reply to the third: "Hollis's page 4 says two things changed in year 22. What were they?"
- **2e.** "And years 31 to 40?" *Answer: the most recorded big-rain events (**8**) and recorded floods (**4**).* Interpretation choices are ungraded: "More events and more recorded floods occurred together."; "Rain caused the difference."; "We need rainfall and recording details before choosing a cause." The replies distinguish the directly counted association from an untested causal explanation. Neither this decade nor the equal-count pair isolates rainfall from drainage or channel changes.

**I'm stuck (2a):**

1. *Hint:* "Which flood years fall between 1 and 10? Between 11 and 20?"
2. *Similar example:* "Floods in years 3, 12 and 18: years 1 to 10 has one (year 3); years 11 to 20 has two (12 and 18). Now sort 4, 15, 27, 31, 34, 36 and 38."
3. **Skip for now.**

## 7. Step 3: What do the records support?

**Learn card** (Marble research for Correlation vs Causation: check for a hidden common cause, coincidence and the reversed arrow before claiming a cause):

> When two things change together, there are several ways it could happen:
>
> - One causes the other.
> - Something else causes both.
> - Two things changed at the same time, and you can't tell which did it.
> - Chance: with only a few events, patterns can appear by luck.
>
> A good reader says which of these the records can rule out, and which they can't.

**Statement sort.** Each card goes in one of three trays: **The records support this**, **The records count against this**, **The records can't tell us**. Every card has an authored reply for each tray. The replies explain; they never mark a tray wrong. The reply that matches the case's reasoning is noted in brackets for the reviewer.

| Card | Fitting tray (reviewer's note) | Reply when placed in "support" |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recorded floods were higher after year 22. | Support | "Yes: means of 50 and 112, highest 60 and 140." |
| The last ten years had more recorded big-rain events at Vadoalto. | Support | "Yes: 8 recorded events against 5 or 6; that is not a rainfall total." |
| Rain alone explains the higher floods. | Can't tell | "Equal event counts can hide different intensity or duration. These records do not rule that explanation in or out." |
| Draining the Sponge was the only cause. | Can't tell | "The brook was straightened too, and rainfall details are missing. A cause is not isolated here." |
| Draining the Sponge made floods worse. | Can't tell | "This is a plausible question to investigate, not a conclusion established by equal event counts. The records do not separate the changes." |
| Straightening the brook made floods worse. | Can't tell | "It happened the same year as the drain. These records can't pull the two apart." |
| There were no small floods before year 22. | Can't tell | "Hollis only wrote floods 'worth the ink'. Small ones may have happened and gone unwritten." |
| The storm will happen again next year. | Can't tell | "Nothing in a list of past floods says what next year will bring." |

**3b.** "Hollis wrote *Years not written had no flood worth the ink.* Why does that matter?" Choices:
- "We don't know what counted as worth the ink, or if it changed over 40 years." ← fits the record
- "It doesn't matter: Hollis wrote down every flood."
- "It means the ledger is useless."

Reply to the third: "It's a limit, not a reason to throw it away. What would it make you careful about?"

**3c.** "Bramble says: *My hat and my boots both got wet that year too. Doesn't mean my hat did it.* Which idea is she using?" Choices: "Things happening together doesn't prove one caused the other." · "Hats cause floods." · "You can never learn anything from records." The first gets: "That's it. Now, do the records give any more than 'together'?" (pointing to the equal-event-count pair and its missing rainfall information).

**I'm stuck (sort):**

1. *Hint:* "For each card, ask: which numbers would I point to? If you can't point to any, it may be 'can't tell'."
2. *Similar example:* "Card: 'The café sold more ice cream in the hot weeks.' If the sales sheet shows it, that's 'support'. Card: 'Hot weather made people buy ice cream.' The sheet shows they went together, but a festival in the same weeks could explain it too: 'can't tell'."
3. **Skip for now.**

   The explorer answers it, concedes part of it, or changes plan.
