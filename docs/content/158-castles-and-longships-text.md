## The story scenes

### Scene 1. Horns for the show (Act II, chapter 4, "The first show")

**Label:** A story from Willowbank.

**Hook:**
> The Story Loft is putting on a Viking show. Pip has made helmets. They have horns. Very big horns.

**Short:**
> Pip makes horned helmets for the Viking show. The librarian brings a book. "Opera costumes in 1876 made those horns famous," they say.

**Longer:**
> The Story Loft is putting on its first show, and it has Vikings in it. Pip has made six helmets from flour sacks and bent willow. Each one has two tall horns.
>
> "Fierce," says Pip. One horn droops.
>
> The librarian comes up the ladder with a heavy book. "I love them," they say. "I should tell you something about the horns, though."
>
> They open the book to a drawing of singers in long cloaks and horned helmets. "This is from an opera in 1876. Painters had drawn horns before, but these costumes made them famous."
>
> "So real Vikings didn't have horns?" asks {guide}.
>
> "The helmets we've found don't," says the librarian.
>
> Pip takes the horns off. He keeps one for the poster.

**Keeper's note:**
> Earlier painters drew horned Vikings, but Carl Emil Doepler's 1876 costumes for Wagner's *Ring* operas made them famous. The two nearly complete Viking-age helmets, from Gjermundbu in Norway and Yarm in England, are rounded iron caps. Much older Bronze Age helmets from Denmark do have horns.

### Scene 2. Léon's postcard (Act III, chapter 4, "Letters up and down the river")

**Label:** A story from Willowbank.

**Short:**
> Léon sends a drawing of the old castle above Les Saules. "Le vieux château." (The old castle.) "Which part is oldest?" he asks.

**Longer:**
> Léon's letter from Les Saules has a drawing in it: a square stone tower on a hill, a ring of walls with round towers around it, and a second ring around that.
>
> Underneath, in Léon's neat writing: *Le vieux château. Il est très vieux.* (The old castle. It is very old.) *My aunt says it was built all at once. My uncle says it grew, bit by bit. Which part is oldest? You have a library. I have a hill.*
>
> "The walls go round it like rings in a tree," says {courier}.
>
> "Then let's count from the middle out," says the librarian, and takes down the castle book.

**Keeper's note:**
> Léon's castle and Les Saules are fictional. Real castles often grew in stages over centuries. Loarre, in the Spanish Pyrenees, got its outer wall more than two hundred years after its first towers.

### Scene 3. Three ribbons (Act II, chapter 3, "Ember's birthday")

**Label:** A story from Willowbank.

**Short:**
> Bramble brings three new ribbons to Ember's party: blue, red and yellow. "Leaves, roots and a weed," she says. "Grandmother's way."

**Longer:**
> Bramble arrives at the café with a parcel for Ember. Inside are three ribbons: a deep blue, a rusty red and a bright yellow.
>
> "Shop ribbons?" asks Puff. Bramble looks offended. "Plants. The blue is from leaves, the red from roots and the yellow from a weed by the mill race. Grandmother taught me."
>
> The magpie tries to steal the yellow. "Which plant was hardest?" asks {guide}.
>
> "The red," says Bramble at once. "The roots take years to grow. And the best red of all, in the old days, didn't come from a plant."
>
> "Then from what?" asks Pip.
>
> "Ask the library," says Bramble, who doesn't know, and won't say so.

**Keeper's note:**
> Bramble is right about the plants. Medieval dyers used woad leaves for blue, madder roots for red and weld for yellow. The costliest red, kermes, came from insects. Real dyeing uses heat and chemicals, so it is a job for a grown-up.

## Reading 1. Longships

All cards: **A true thing from history.** Each teach card is 30 words or fewer.

**1.1 Who were the Vikings?**
> Vikings were people from Scandinavia, today's Norway, Denmark and Sweden. Some went raiding. Others farmed, fished, traded and settled in new lands.

**1.2 When?**
> Historians often call about 790 to 1066 the Viking Age. Some books choose slightly different dates.

**1.3 The longship**
> A longship was long, narrow and light, with oars and a sail. It needed only shallow water, so it could go up rivers and onto beaches.

**1.4 A great longship**
> One longship found in Denmark was about 30 meters long, with 60 oars. Tree rings show it was built near Dublin, about 1042.

*Details:* It is called Skuldelev 2. Its crew was about 65 to 70 people, and it sat only about 1 meter deep.

*Worked example (one step per tap):*
1. A small boat has 8 oars.
2. There are the same number on each side.
3. 8 ÷ 2 = 4.
4. So there are 4 oars on each side.

**1.5 Viking towns**
> Vikings built and ran trading towns. Dublin grew from a Viking fort of 841. York, which they called Jorvik, was taken in 866.

**1.6 Silver from far away**
> Viking traders sailed rivers far to the east. Silver coins made as far away as Iraq and Afghanistan reached Viking hands, passed on through trade.

**1.7 A buried treasure**
> In 2007, people found a buried Viking treasure in Yorkshire, England. A silver cup held 617 silver coins and 65 other objects.

*Details:* It is called the Vale of York hoard, buried around 927. Its coins came from as far apart as Afghanistan and North Africa.

**1.8 Across the ocean**
> Norse sailors reached Iceland, Greenland and then North America. At L'Anse aux Meadows, in Newfoundland, Canada, the remains of their camp survive.

*Details:* Two Icelandic sagas, written down more than 200 years later, tell of Leif Erikson and others sailing to "Vinland." Nobody has proved this camp was his. People had lived in North America for thousands of years already.

**1.9 A date from tree rings**
> In 993, a burst from space left a mark in tree rings. Count the rings from that mark out to the bark. That gives the cutting year.

*Worked example:*
1. A made-up log has the 993 mark.
2. You count 7 rings after it, to the bark.
3. 993 + 7 = 1000.
4. This tree was cut in 1000.

**1.10 What the rings showed**
> Scientists counted rings in wood cut at L'Anse aux Meadows. The trees were cut in 1021. So Norse people were there in 1021.

*Details:* The date does not tell us when the Norse first arrived. The camp seems to have been used for only a few years.

**1.11 Horned helmets?**
> The nearly whole Viking helmets that have been found have no horns. Opera costumes from 1876 made the horns famous.

**Guided item (steps shown before answering):**
- Prompt: A made-up log's bark is 10 rings after the 993 mark. What year?
- Steps shown: "Start at 993" · "Add the rings: 993 + 10"
- Answer: 1003.
- Feedback (right): Yes. 993 + 10 = 1003. Count forward from the mark.
- Feedback (wrong): Rings grow one each year, going forward in time. Add, starting from 993.

## Reading 2. Castles through time

All cards: **A true thing from history.**

**2.1 Remember what a castle was for**
> A castle was a strong home for a lord. It was also a center for running the lord's lands, holding courts, and defense.

**2.2 Motte and bailey**
> Early castles had a high earth mound, the motte, with a wooden tower. Below was a fenced yard, the bailey. A ditch went round both.

**2.3 Fast to build**
> After 1066 the Normans built many motte-and-bailey castles in England. Earth and timber were quick to raise. Some were rebuilt in stone later.

**2.4 Parts of a castle**
> A keep was the main tower. A curtain wall ran round the castle. A gatehouse guarded the entrance. Some had a moat, a deep ditch round the walls.

**2.5 Rooms inside**
> The great hall was for meals and meetings. Food was cooked in the kitchen. People prayed in the chapel. A garderobe was a toilet.

**2.6 Stone keeps**
> Stone does not burn or rot like wood. The White Tower in London, a stone keep, was begun in the 1070s. Stone was slower and costlier.

**2.7 Weak spots**
> Builders studied weak spots. Wood could burn. Square corners could be dug under. A gate was the easiest way in. Each new design fixed one.

**2.8 The corner at Rochester**
> In 1215, attackers dug a tunnel under one corner of Rochester's stone keep. The corner fell. It was later rebuilt round, with no corner.

*Details (Keeper's Desk):* The tunnel was held up by wooden props. King John sent for the fat of forty pigs to burn them. When the props burned, the corner above fell in.

**2.9 Gatehouses**
> The gate was the easiest way in. So gates became strong buildings of their own, called gatehouses, with towers on each side.

**2.10 Walls inside walls**
> A concentric castle has a ring of walls inside another ring. The inner walls stand higher. Beaumaris, in Wales, was begun in 1295.

*Details:* King Edward I ordered Beaumaris. It was never finished, because the money went to his wars in Scotland.

**2.11 Use the land**
> Loarre Castle stands in the foothills of the Pyrenees, in Aragon, Spain. Much of it was built in the 1000s, on a high rocky outcrop.

*Details:* A high rock did the job of a motte. Loarre is one of the oldest castles in Spain, and is often called its most important Romanesque fortress.

**2.12 A siege**
> In a siege, an army surrounded a castle so nothing could get in or out. It could last weeks or months, until food ran low.

*Note:* Builders dug wells inside the walls, and castles were stocked with food before a siege.

**Guided item:**
- Prompt: Rochester's corner fell in 1215. How many years after 1066?
- Steps shown: "1215 − 1066 = ?"
- Answer: 149.
- Feedback (right): Yes. 149 years. Castles kept changing all that time.
- Feedback (wrong): Take the earlier year from the later one: 1215 − 1066.

## Reading 3. What people wore

All cards: **A true thing from history.**

**3.1 Wool and linen**
> Most people wore clothes of wool and linen. Linen, made from the flax plant, was often worn next to the skin. Wool kept you warm.

**3.2 Colors from plants**
> Dyers made blue from woad leaves, red from madder roots and yellow from weld. Mixing dyes made other colors, like green.

*Worked example:*
1. A ribbon is dipped in yellow from weld.
2. Then it is dipped in blue from woad.
3. Yellow and blue make green.
4. So it comes out green.

**3.3 The costliest red**
> The richest red dye, kermes, was made from tiny insects. It cost far more than madder. Scarlet was a fine, expensive wool cloth.

**3.4 Rich and poor**
> Rich people wore silk, fur and bright colors that cost a lot. Most working people wore plainer cloth, often undyed or in cheaper colors.

**3.5 How clothes fastened**
> Clothes were closed with pins, brooches, laces and belts. Buttons with buttonholes spread in Europe from about the 1200s.

**3.6 A law about clothes**
> In 1363, an English law said what cloth each rank of person could wear. Lawmakers worried that ordinary people were dressing like the rich.

*Details (Keeper's Desk):* A law like this shows what rulers worried about. It doesn't show what everyone actually wore.

**3.7 Clothes as signals**
> In a medieval town, clothes told people who you were: rich or poor, what work you did. Everyone could read the signal.

## Reading 4. 1066 and the long embroidery

All cards: **A true thing from history.**

**4.1 Vikings and Anglo-Saxons**
> Before 1066, England had Anglo-Saxon kingdoms. Viking armies attacked and settled. In 878, Alfred, king of Wessex, defeated the Viking leader Guthrum.

*Details:* England was then divided. The Danelaw, in the north and east, was under Viking rule.

**4.2 One kingdom**
> In 927, King Athelstan took York and ruled all the English. Historians often call him the first king of England.

**4.3 A king with no heir**
> King Edward the Confessor died in January 1066, with no children. Three men said they should be king.

**4.4 Three claims**
> Harold Godwinson, an English earl, was crowned. Harald Hardrada, king of Norway, invaded. William, duke of Normandy, said the crown was promised to him.

**4.5 Two battles**
> Harold defeated Harald at Stamford Bridge on 25 September. On 28 September, William landed at Pevensey, in the south.

**4.6 Hastings**
> William's army defeated Harold's near Hastings on 14 October 1066. Harold died in the battle. William was crowned on Christmas Day.

**4.7 An embroidery**
> The Bayeux Tapestry is an embroidery: pictures stitched in wool on linen. It is about 68 meters long and about half a meter tall.

**4.8 Who made it?**
> Nobody knows who stitched it. It was probably ordered by Bishop Odo of Bayeux, William's half-brother, and probably sewn in England.

**4.9 Whose story?**
> It tells the story from the Norman side. It shows Harold swearing an oath to William. English accounts tell that part differently.

*Details:* The embroidery shows an oath on holy objects. Its words don't say what was promised. Norman writers say it was a promise to support William.

**4.10 A star with a tail**
> The embroidery shows people pointing at a star with a tail. It was Halley's comet, seen in 1066. It has come back many times, including 1910.

*Details:* 1910 is the year of the Paris flood in the Archive.

**4.11 Where is it now?**
> It usually hangs in a museum in Bayeux, in Normandy, France. It has also been lent to the British Museum, in London, for an exhibition.

**Guided item:**
- Prompt: Order these: Edward dies · William crowned · Battle of Hastings.
- Steps shown: "Which happened in January?" · "Which happened at Christmas?"
- Answer: Edward dies, Battle of Hastings, William crowned.
- Feedback (right): Yes. January, then October, then Christmas Day.
- Feedback (wrong): Use the months. January comes first. Christmas comes last.

## Bench 1. Row up the river

**Teach card:** A boat floats only where the water is deeper than its draft: how deep its hull sits.

## Bench 3. The dye pot

**Card:** Dip a ribbon. Then do the dye pot sums.

**Activity:** Three plant jars (woad, madder, weld). Tap a jar, then the white ribbon. Dipping in two jars mixes the colors: woad then weld gives green; madder then weld gives orange; woad then madder gives purple. Labeled **A simplified picture**: real dyeing needed a mordant (a fixing substance), heat and many dips, and results changed with the wool.

**Made-up recipe (shown for item C6):** *One pot of dye colors 4 ribbons.* **Made up for this job.**

**Worked example:** 8 ribbons · 4 ribbons per pot · 8 ÷ 4 = 2 · You need 2 pots.

### Set E. At the Keeper's Desk: The Great Sickness of 1347 (4 items)

**Folder card (before anything opens):**
> This folder is about a sickness long ago, when many people died. It is told simply and gently. Open it when you like, or not at all.

**Cards (all A true thing from history):**

**K.1** In 1347 a deadly sickness reached Europe. By 1351 it had spread across most of it. Later people called it the Black Death.

**K.2** It was plague, caused by a bacterium. Fleas carried it from rats to people. It could also pass from person to person.

*Details:* Some newer research suggests body lice may also have spread it.

**K.3** Historians estimate that a third or more of Europe's people died. Records are incomplete, so the estimates differ.

**K.4** Nobody then could see germs. Many people blamed bad, smelly air, or thought it was a punishment from God. They used the ideas they had.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Draft:** how deep a boat sits in the water.
- **Hoard:** a store of treasure buried or hidden together.
- **Saga:** a long story from medieval Iceland, written down long after the events.
- **Motte:** the earth mound of an early castle.
- **Bailey:** the fenced yard beside or below the motte.
- **Keep:** a castle's main tower.
- **Curtain wall:** the wall around a castle.
- **Gatehouse:** a strong building guarding a castle's entrance.
- **Concentric castle:** a castle with walls inside walls.
- **Siege:** surrounding a castle so nothing could get in or out.
- **Woad, madder, weld:** plants for blue, red and yellow dye.
- **Kermes:** a costly red dye made from insects.
- **Embroidery:** pictures or patterns stitched onto cloth.
- **Point of view:** whose side a story is told from.
- **Plague:** a disease caused by a bacterium, spread by fleas and people.
- **Bacterium:** a living thing too small to see without a microscope.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches.** Vikings as traders, settlers and raiders; why castle design changed; clothes as signals of rank; 1066, with the Bayeux Tapestry as one side's source. Math: division with remainders, date differences, an average.

**What it does not claim.** Willowbank, Léon's castle, the show and Bramble's ribbons are fiction. The deep ship and dye recipe are made up; the river is a simplified picture. The pack says plainly that Vikings raided, without showing it.

**Gentle topics.** Battles are named and dated; nobody is hurt on screen, and Harold's death is one plain sentence. Sieges are design problems only. The Black Death sits in an opt-in Keeper's Desk folder of five short cards, with no symptoms or images. It leaves out the persecution of Jewish communities during the plague years; a grown-up can raise that if they choose.

**Sources in brief.** Viking Ship Museum (Roskilde), *Nature* (2021), British Museum, York Museums Trust, English Heritage, Historic Royal Palaces, Turespaña, Britannica, Fordham Medieval Sourcebook. Full list in the source audit.

**Off-screen idea (adult-led).** Dye a scrap of white wool or cotton with onion skins in an old pan. It needs heat: a grown-up handles the stove and hot water, uses a pan kept for crafts, and lets everything cool before children touch it. Skip chemical mordants. Don't gather wild dye plants.
