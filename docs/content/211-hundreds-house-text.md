# The Hundreds House: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 211](../specs/211-hundreds-house.md). Use as written, after independent spec review and a math check. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion and is never gendered; `{courier}` is the explorer's courier. Pip is he/him, Dot she/her, Bramble she/her (with her magpie), the orchard keeper she/her, the librarian they/them, Hob Pennick he/him. Real-world facts are in the [source audit](../source-audits/211-hundreds-house.md); items still marked `[check]` stay off screen until cleared. Nothing here is scored or timed.

Number words follow American style: no "and" inside a whole number ("one hundred five"). A child who says "one hundred and five" is reading it right; every reading prompt accepts both.

## Labels used on every card

- **A story from Willowbank**: the town, the storehouse, its seeds and every character are made up.
- **A true thing about math**: checked math, explained simply.
- **A true thing from history**: something people really did or wrote.
- **A made-up model**: a simple game rule, shown on the card, that is not a real measurement.

## Arriving

**Place title:** The Hundreds House

**First visit** (A story from Willowbank):
> The seed storehouse by the orchard gate. Loose seeds in jars, packets on hooks, boxes on shelves. The orchard keeper calls it the Hundreds House.

**Five doors in the storehouse:** The Packing Table · The Label Desk · The Counting Shelf · The Seed Line · The Planting Orders

**Model card** (A made-up model, shown on every bench):

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. The door blew open (Act I, chapter 1: find and feed)

**Hook:** The storm burst the storehouse door. Packets tore. Seeds are everywhere.

**Short:**
> The storm threw the storehouse open. Seeds lie everywhere. "I can't plant what I can't count," says the orchard keeper.

**Longer:**
> The orchard keeper's storehouse stood through the storm, but its door did not. Wind tore the packets off their hooks and tipped a box across the floor. Loose seeds lie in drifts, like sand.
>
> "Supper needs the orchard," she says. "Next spring needs these seeds. First I have to know what's left."
>
> Pip offers to count every seed, one at a time. He gets to fourteen and sneezes.
>
> {guide} picks up an empty packet. "Ten seeds fill one of these. Ten packets fill a box. Pack as you go, and the counting packs itself."
>
> By lunchtime the floor is clear. Three full boxes, four packets, and six loose seeds sit on the bench.
>
> "Three hundred forty-six," says the orchard keeper. She writes it on the door in chalk. "That's enough to start."

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> Three boxes, four packets and six loose seeds: 300 + 40 + 6 = 346. Packing never adds or loses a seed. It only makes the count quicker to see. Real seed packets hold all sorts of amounts; in the Hundreds House, a packet is always ten.

### Scene 2. One hundred five beans (Act I, chapter 3: investigate and plant)

**Hook:** The garden beds are soft again. Bramble writes the bean label, and it comes out far too big.

**Short:**
> "One hundred five bean seeds," says the orchard keeper. Bramble writes 1005. Pip gasps. "That's a whole field of beans!"

**Longer:**
> The beds behind the café are dark and soft after the rain. The orchard keeper wants beans along the fence. "One hundred five seeds," she says. "Write the label, please."
>
> Bramble writes what she hears: 100, then 5. The label says 1005.
>
> Pip reads it and sits down on a bucket. "That's a whole field. We'd be eating beans until winter."
>
> Bramble frowns at the label. Her magpie frowns too, out of loyalty.
>
> {guide} sets out one box and five loose seeds. "Here is one hundred five. One box. No packets. Five seeds."
>
> "No packets," says Bramble slowly. "So the middle place is empty." She writes 1, then 0, then 5. "The zero sits in the packet place."
>
> "And 150?" asks Pip.
>
> "That's one box and five packets," says Bramble. "Fifty is a lot more than five."

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> Writing what you hear gives 1005, which is a thousand and five: about ten times too many. In 105, the zero says "no packets here" and keeps the 1 in the hundreds place. 150 has five packets instead: 100 + 50.

### Scene 3. The seed stall (Act II, chapter 1: the weekly market)

**Hook:** The market is back. Hob Pennick and the orchard keeper both sell pea seeds, and both say they have more.

**Short:**
> Hob has 254 pea seeds. The orchard keeper has 245. "Mine ends in 5, so it's bigger," she says. Is it?

**Longer:**
> The weekly market fills the green again. Hob Pennick, the farmer from the ford, has a pea stall. So does the orchard keeper. Each chalks a number on a board.
>
> Hob's board: 254. The orchard keeper's board: 245.
>
> "Five beats four," says the orchard keeper, pointing at the ends. "I have more."
>
> "Two hundreds each," says Dot, counting boxes on both tables. "Then look at the packets."
>
> Hob has five packets. The orchard keeper has four.
>
> "Then Hob has more," says Pip, "and the loose seeds don't matter."
>
> "They'd matter if the packets were the same," says Dot.
>
> The magpie takes one packet off Hob's table and flies to the roof.
>
> "Now it's 244," says Hob, gloomily. "Ten less."

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> Compare from the left: hundreds first, then tens, then ones. The first place that differs decides. 254 and 245 tie on hundreds; 5 tens beats 4 tens, so 254 > 245. Taking one packet changes only the tens digit: 254 becomes 244.

### Scene 4. Seeds for the shared plots (Act II, chapter 2: a garden across the seasons)

**Hook:** The town will share new garden plots. The librarian wants a record of every seed sent out.

**Short:**
> Each plot needs 300 bean seeds and 400 pea seeds. "Three and four make seven," says Pip. "Seven seeds?"

**Longer:**
> The librarian has a new ledger and a new rule: every seed that leaves the storehouse gets written down. "If we know what we planted," they say, "we'll know what grew."
>
> The first shared plot needs 300 bean seeds and 400 pea seeds.
>
> "Easy," says Pip. "Three and four make seven. Seven seeds."
>
> Dot puts three boxes of beans on the bench, then four boxes of peas. "Count these."
>
> "One, two, three, four, five, six, seven," says Pip. "Seven boxes." He stops. "Seven hundred seeds."
>
> "Same fact," says {guide}. "Bigger things."
>
> The librarian writes 700 in the ledger and draws a small box next to it, so nobody forgets what the 7 is counting.

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> 3 + 4 = 7 works for seeds, packets or boxes. 3 packets and 4 packets make 7 packets: 30 + 40 = 70. 3 boxes and 4 boxes make 7 boxes: 300 + 400 = 700. Always say what you are counting. Ten boxes would fill a crate; crates belong to the library's Ledger Room.

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer. Worked steps appear one tap at a time.

**T1. A packet is ten** (A true thing about math)
> Ten loose seeds fill one packet. A packet is ten ones, packed together. Packing does not change how many seeds there are.
Worked example: 1. Count ten seeds into the packet. 2. Close it. 3. One packet, still 10 seeds.

**T2. A box is ten packets** (A true thing about math)
> Ten full packets fill one box. One box is one hundred: ten tens, or a hundred seeds. Nothing is added or lost.
Worked example: 1. Count the packets by tens: 10, 20, 30 … 100. 2. Pack them in the box. 3. 1 box = 10 packets = 100 seeds.

**T3. Packets only** (A true thing about math)
> 30 means 3 packets and 0 loose seeds. The 0 holds the ones place. Add one loose seed and it becomes 31.
Worked example: 1. Three packets: 30. 2. No loose seeds: write 0. 3. One loose seed joins: 31.

**T4. Boxes only** (A true thing about math)
> 300 means 3 boxes, 0 packets and 0 loose seeds. Two zeros hold two empty places. 300 is far more than 30 or 3.
Worked example: 1. Three boxes. 2. No packets: 0 in the tens place. 3. No loose seeds: 0 in the ones place. 4. 300.

**T5. The tens words** (A true thing about math)
> Twenty, thirty, forty, fifty, sixty, seventy, eighty, ninety. Each names a number of tens. Forty has no u. Ninety keeps the e.
Worked example: 1. Four packets. 2. Four tens: forty. 3. Write 40.

**T6. Tens first, then ones** (A true thing about math)
> From twenty-one up, say and write the tens first. Sixty-four is 64. A short dash, called a hyphen, joins the two words.
Worked example: 1. Sixty: 6 tens. 2. Four: 4 ones. 3. Write 64, or sixty-four.

**T7. Teen or ty?** (A true thing about math)
> Fifteen is 1 ten and 5 ones: 15. Fifty is 5 tens: 50. Listen to the ending: teen or ty.
Worked example: 1. Fifteen: one packet, five loose. 2. Fifty: five packets. 3. 15 and 50 are far apart.

**T8. Past one hundred** (A true thing about math)
> After 99 comes 100. Write boxes, then packets, then loose seeds. One hundred eight is 108: one box, no packets, eight seeds.
Worked example: 1. One hundred fifteen. 2. One box: 1. One packet: 1. Five seeds: 5. 3. 115.

**T9. One number, four ways** (A true thing about math)
> 342 is 3 boxes, 4 packets and 2 seeds. Words: three hundred forty-two. Expanded form: 300 + 40 + 2. All one number.
Worked example: 1. 305: 3 boxes, 0 packets, 5 seeds. 2. Three hundred five. 3. 300 + 5. 4. The empty tens place needs a 0.

**T10. Count from anywhere** (A true thing about math)
> You can start counting at any number. When the ones reach 9, the next count fills a packet: 99, 100; 109, 110.
Worked example: 1. 97, 98, 99. 2. Ten packets fill a box. 3. 100, 101, 102.

**T11. Counting in steps** (A true thing about math)
> You can count in steps of 5, 10 or 100. By hundreds from 150: 150, 250, 350. The 50 stays.
Worked example: 1. By fives: 490, 495. 2. Next: 500. 3. Then 505.

**T12. Ten more, ten less** (A true thing about math)
> Ten more adds one packet. The tens digit goes up one; the ones digit stays. 56, then 66. Ten less: 46.
Worked example: 1. 56: 5 packets, 6 seeds. 2. Add a packet: 6 packets, 6 seeds. 3. 66.

**T13. A hundred more, and full packets** (A true thing about math)
> A hundred more adds a box: 456 becomes 556. If ten packets fill up, pack a box. 695 + 10 = 705.
Worked example: 1. 695: 9 packets. 2. One more packet: 10 packets. 3. Pack a box: 7 boxes, 0 packets, 5 seeds: 705.

**T14. Lines drawn to scale** (A true thing about math)
> Read both ends first: 0 to 100, or 0 to 1000? Halfway is the middle. Use halfway to help you place a number.
Worked example: 1. Line 0 to 100. 2. Halfway is 50. 3. 40 sits a little before halfway.

**T15. About how many?** (A true thing about math)
> Compare a pile with one packet of ten. A pile about four packets big holds about 40. "About" is a real answer.
Worked example: 1. One packet: 10 seeds. 2. The pile looks like four packets. 3. About 40.

**T16. Compare from the left** (A true thing about math)
> Line up the places. Look at hundreds first. If they match, look at tens, then ones. The first difference decides.
Worked example: 1. 254 and 245. 2. Hundreds: 2 and 2, same. 3. Tens: 5 beats 4. 4. 254 > 245.

**T17. Signs that point** (A true thing about math)
> The open side of < or > faces the bigger number. 254 > 245 says "254 is more than 245."
Worked example: 1. 99 and 100. 2. 100 is bigger. 3. 99 < 100: the open side faces 100.

**T18. Facts that grow** (A true thing about math)
> 3 + 4 = 7. So 3 tens and 4 tens make 7 tens: 30 + 40 = 70. And 300 + 400 = 700. Say the unit.
Worked example: 1. 3 packets + 4 packets = 7 packets. 2. 7 packets is 70. 3. 3 boxes + 4 boxes = 700.

**T19. Split, add, put back** (A true thing about math)
> 34 + 20: split 34 into 30 and 4. 30 + 20 = 50. Put the 4 back: 54.
Worked example: 1. 34 is 30 and 4. 2. 3 + 2 = 5, so 30 + 20 = 50. 3. 50 + 4 = 54.

**T20. Undo it** (A true thing about math)
> "I add 10 and get 45." To find the start, undo it: take 10 away. 45 − 10 = 35.
Worked example: 1. The last step added 10. 2. Undo: take 10 away. 3. 35. Check: 35 + 10 = 45.

## Real-world and history cards

**H1. Rods on a board** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> Long ago in China, people worked out sums with small rods laid on a board. Each place had its own spot. An empty spot meant "none here".
Keeper's note:
> A Chinese math book written more than 1,500 years ago, the *Sunzi Suanjing*, gives the rule: ones stand up, tens lie flat, hundreds stand up again. Turning the rods at each place kept neighbors from running together. A gap, with no rods, held an empty place, just as our 0 does in 305.

**H2. The soroban** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> In Japan, many children learn the soroban, a bead frame. Each rod is one place. The bead above the bar is worth 5. Each bead below is worth 1.
Keeper's note:
> The soroban grew from the Chinese bead frame, the suanpan. Early Japanese frames had more beads; later ones kept one bead above and four below, enough to show 0 to 9 on each rod. A rod with no beads moved to the bar shows zero, and still holds its place.

## Problem sets

Eighteen three-choice items put the right answer at A six times, B six times and C six times.

### Set A. Packets and boxes (Sprout to Sapling)

**A1 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Ten full packets go into one box. What is in the box now?
- A. All the seeds from all ten packets. *(right)*
- B. Only the seeds from one packet. *(tests: the box counts as one more packet)*
- C. Fewer seeds, because the box squashes them. *(tests: packing changes the amount)*
Hint: Did any seeds fall out?
Explanation: Packing moves seeds into a box. Every seed is still there.
Feedback A: Yes. Same seeds, packed together.
Feedback B: All ten packets went in. Their seeds came too.
Feedback C: Boxes don't squash seeds away. Open it and see.

**A2 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which shelf has the most seeds?
Pictures: a shelf of nine packets · a shelf with one box · a shelf of one packet and some loose seeds.
- A. Nine packets, because there are more things. *(tests: counting containers, not seeds)*
- B. One box, because it holds ten packets. *(right)*
- C. You can't tell without counting every seed. *(tests: withholding a judgment the packing already shows)*
Hint: How many packets fill a box?
Explanation: A box holds ten packets. Nine packets is one packet short of a box.
Feedback A: Nine packets is a lot of things. One box holds even more seeds.
Feedback B: Yes. A box is ten packets, more than nine.
Feedback C: The packing tells you. A box always beats nine packets.

**A3 (Seedling, number)**
Prompt: How many seeds are in one box?

Feedback A: Yes. Halfway along, half full.
Feedback B: Almost full would be near the far end.
Feedback C: Nearly empty would be near the start.

**D2 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Where does 40 go on a line from 0 to 100?
- A. A bit before halfway. *(right)*
- B. A bit after halfway. *(tests: spreading small numbers too far)*
- C. Right next to 0, because 40 is small. *(tests: using only one end)*
Hint: Halfway is 50. Is 40 more or less?
Explanation: 40 is a little less than 50, so just before the middle.
Feedback A: Yes. Just short of 50.
Feedback B: Past halfway is more than 50. 40 is less.
Feedback C: 40 is four packets out of ten. That's near the middle.

**D3 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: A spilled pile sits beside one packet. About how many seeds?

- A. About 10 *(tests: the pile matched to one packet)*
- B. About 40 *(right)*
- C. About 400 *(tests: wild guessing past the packet benchmark)*

Explanation: About four packets of ten: about 40. Counting would give 41.
Feedback A: The pile is much bigger than one packet.
Feedback B: Yes. About four packets. Close is good.

**D4 (Seedling, 3-choice)**

- B. 254 = 245 *(tests: same digits means equal)*

Explanation: Hundreds tie. 5 tens beat 4 tens, so 254 is more.

**D5 (Seedling, 3-choice)**

Explanation: 100 is one box. 99 is one seed short of a box.

### Set E. Facts that grow, and old ways with places (Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**E1 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: 3 + 4 = 7. So what is 30 + 40? And 300 + 400?
Answer: 70 and 700.
Worked steps: 1. 3 packets + 4 packets = 7 packets: 70. 2. 3 boxes + 4 boxes = 7 boxes: 700.
Tests: 7 (the unit dropped); 3040 (numbers written side by side).
Hint: What are you counting: seeds, packets or boxes?
Explanation: Same fact, bigger unit. Say the unit and you'll keep the zeros.

**E2 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: What is 34 + 20?
Answer: 54.
Worked steps: 1. Split 34: 30 and 4. 2. 3 + 2 = 5, so 30 + 20 = 50. 3. Put back the 4: 54.
Tests: 36 (20 read as 2 ones); 9 (3 + 4 + 2 + 0, digits without places).
Hint: Split 34 into tens and ones.
Explanation: Add the tens to the tens. The 4 ones wait, then come back.

**E3 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: I think of a number. I add 10 and get 45. What was my number?
Answer: 35.
Worked steps: 1. The last step added 10. 2. Undo it: 45 − 10 = 35. 3. Check: 35 + 10 = 45.
Tests: 55 (adding 10 again); 44 (taking away 1).
Hint: Go backward. What undoes adding 10?
Explanation: Taking 10 away undoes adding 10. Check by going forward.

**E4 (Keeper's Desk, number puzzle)**
Prompt: 10 more than my number is 100 less than 815. What is my number?
Answer: 705.
Worked steps: 1. 100 less than 815: 715. 2. My number plus 10 is 715. 3. Undo: 715 − 10 = 705. Check: 705 + 10 = 715.
Tests: 725 (adding 10 instead of undoing it); 615 (taking 100 away twice); 805 (taking 10 from 815 and forgetting the 100).
Hint: Start with the part you know: 100 less than 815.
Explanation: Find 715 first. Then undo the 10 more. Only 705 works.

**E5 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice; card H1)**
Prompt: Rods on the board: three standing, a gap, five standing. What number?
Picture: three upright rods in the hundreds spot, an empty tens spot, five upright rods in the ones spot.

- B. 3005 *(tests: reading the gap as two places)*
- C. 305 *(right)*

Explanation: Hundreds 3, tens none, ones 5: 305. The gap works like our 0.
Feedback A: The gap holds the tens place. Without it, 3 would be tens.
Feedback B: One gap, one empty place. Three places in all.
Feedback C: Yes. The empty spot is the zero.

**E6 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice; card H2)**
Prompt: A soroban shows hundreds, tens and ones rods. What number?

- A. 704 *(right)*
- B. 74 *(tests: skipping the empty rod)*
- C. 7 *(tests: counting beads moved, ignoring places and the 5-bead)*

Explanation: Hundreds: 5 + 2 = 7. Tens: 0. Ones: 4. 704.

Feedback B: The tens rod is empty, and it still counts: 0 tens.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **packet**: ten seeds packed together; one ten.
- **box**: ten packets packed together; one hundred.
- **ones, tens, hundreds**: the three places in a number up to 999.
- **digit**: one of the signs 0 to 9 used to write numbers.
- **place**: where a digit sits. The place tells what it counts.
- **zero (placeholder)**: a 0 that holds an empty place, like the 0 in 105.
- **expanded form**: a number split by place, like 300 + 40 + 2.
- **hyphen**: the short dash in words like sixty-four.
- **multiple of ten**: a number of whole tens, like 30 or 90.
- **multiple of a hundred**: a number of whole hundreds, like 300 or 900.
- **number line**: a line where each number has its own spot, drawn to scale.
- **estimate**: a careful "about" answer.
- **<** and **>**: signs for "is less than" and "is more than". The open side faces the bigger number.
- **counting rods**: small sticks once used in China to set out numbers by place.
- **soroban**: a Japanese bead frame. Each rod is one place.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** a hundred as ten tens; the multiples of 10 and 100 and their placeholder zeros; number words to one hundred and numerals to 1000, including 105 and 150; counting on and back within 1000 by 1s, 5s, 10s and 100s, across 100 and other roll-overs; 10 more or less and 100 more or less, and which digit changes; numbers on scaled lines and "about how many"; comparing and ordering to 1000 with < and >; using a known fact like 3 + 4 = 7 for 30 + 40 and 300 + 400.

**What it does not claim:** the screen checks tapped and typed answers; it does not see handwriting or independent spelling. Seed packets here always hold ten; real ones do not. Adding several tens or hundreds to a three-digit number, and numbers beyond 1000, are in other packs.
