# 301 · Ice and warmth: finite teaching copy

**Water Lab · Ice and warmth**

Willowbank's friends have a winter question: what changes when ice melts? Try a tiny-view model of pure water. Our jar keeps its water while energy can enter or leave.

Controls: Energy out / Energy in / Follow another particle / Start again. Temperature:−5 °C, 0 °C or +5 °C. Stage {n} of 6. The six exact count strings are “12 in ice · 0 in liquid”, “12 in ice · 0 in liquid”, “8 in ice · 4 in liquid”, “4 in ice · 8 in liquid”, “0 in ice · 12 in liquid”, “0 in ice · 12 in liquid”. Same water; same mass in this closed model. Ring status is “Following particle {id}: in ice.” or “Following particle {id}: in liquid.” with the selected integer 1–12. Each particle symbol stands for one water molecule. These are symbols, not a view through a real magnifier.

1. **Cold ice.** All 12 symbols are in an open, ordered ice pattern. Water molecules in ice still vibrate around their positions. This model stops here; adding energy warms the ice toward zero degrees Celsius.
2. **Ice at its melting point.** All 12 symbols are ice at 0 °C. Energy entering can melt some ice without raising the temperature; energy leaving can cool this ice below zero.
3. **Mostly ice, some liquid.** Eight symbols are in ice and four are in liquid. The temperature is 0 °C. Adding energy melts more ice; removing energy freezes more liquid without changing the temperature.
4. **Mostly liquid, some ice.** Four symbols are in ice and eight are in liquid. The temperature is 0 °C. Adding energy melts more ice; removing energy freezes more liquid without changing the temperature.
5. **All liquid at 0 °C.** There is no ice at this stage. The same 12 water-molecule symbols form a closer, irregular liquid group. They can move past their neighbors. Energy leaving can start freezing without lowering the temperature; energy entering can warm this liquid above zero.
6. **Warmer liquid.** All 12 symbols are liquid at +5 °C. In this single liquid phase, adding energy can raise the temperature and removing energy can lower it. This model stops here. The picture does not measure molecular speed or density.

**Try it back the other way.** Remove energy. Can you find two stages with the same temperature but different amounts of ice? Tell someone what changed and what stayed the same. This conversation isn't recorded or scored.

**About this model.** Pure water, ordinary air pressure, heated and cooled slowly, so the ice and water share one temperature. The jar is closed to water entering or leaving; energy may cross its boundary. Each button moves one model stage, not an equal amount of energy or time. Melting all the ice takes much more energy than warming the ice or water by five degrees. A real storm puddle may contain other substances. The steps don't predict its temperature. Symbols, sizes, arrows and spacing aren't to scale. Ice vibrates; liquid molecules move past neighbors. Melting rearranges molecules without making a new substance. The model stops at −5 and +5 °C; real water can be colder or hotter. No real ice or equipment is needed.

Primary references: ACS Energy/Thermometers, ACS Middle School Chemistry 2.5, USGS Water Density. These are links for a grown-up, not required external activities.
