# The Order of the Day: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 192](../specs/192-order-of-the-day.md). Use as written, after independent spec review and a math check. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion and is never gendered; `{courier}` is the explorer's courier. ## Labels used on every card

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. Fourteen or twenty? (Act II, chapter 3: Ember's birthday)

**Hook:** Ember writes her own birthday order. The baker bakes exactly what the slip says.

**Short:**
> Ember's slip says 2 + 3 × 4 honey buns. The baker bakes 14. Ember wanted 20. Same numbers, different order.

**Longer:**
> Ember writes her birthday order herself, in very large numbers: **2 + 3 × 4 honey buns.**
>
> The baker reads it twice. "Three trays of four is twelve. Add two. Fourteen." He bakes fourteen.
>
> Ember counts them. Then she counts them again, slower. "I meant two trays and three trays. Five trays of four. Twenty!"
>
> "Then the slip should say so," says the baker, kindly. "I do the times part first. Every baker does. That's how we read slips the same way."
>
> Pip leans over. "Put a fence around the bit you want first."
>
> Ember draws two curved lines: **(2 + 3) × 4.** The baker nods and lights the oven again.
>
> "Six more buns," says Bramble. Her magpie looks hopeful.

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> Without brackets, 2 + 3 × 4 means 3 × 4 first, then add 2: that's 14. Brackets say "do this part first": (2 + 3) × 4 is 5 × 4, which is 20. Same numbers, two answers. The brackets choose.

### Scene 2. One loaf or twenty-five? (Act III, chapter 1: the floating market)

**Hook:** A Vadoalto trader hands over an order slip. Pip reads it his own way.

**Short:**
> The trader's slip says 20 ÷ 4 × 5 loaves. Pip says one loaf. The trader laughs. "¡Veinticinco panes!" Twenty-five loaves.

**Longer** (Spanish line for a fluent reader's check):
> The boats from Vadoalto tie up at the landing. A trader passes the baker a slip for each boat: **20 ÷ 4 × 5 loaves.**
>
> Pip grabs it. "Times first! Four times five is twenty. Twenty divided by twenty is one. One loaf per boat."
>
> The trader laughs so hard the boat rocks. *"¡Veinticinco panes!"* Twenty-five loaves.
>
> "Times and divide share a step," says {guide}. "On the same step, go left to right."
>
> The baker runs his finger along the slip. "Twenty divided by four is five. Five times five is twenty-five."
>
> "Then why does my song say multiply first?" asks Pip.
>
> "A song is a shortcut," says {guide}. "Shortcuts can skip the important bit."

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> × and ÷ sit on the same step. So do + and −. On one step, work from left to right. 20 ÷ 4 × 5 is 5 × 5, which is 25.

### Scene 3. Bundles in the box (Act III, chapter 2: the seed exchange)

**Hook:** Nuria packs apple seedlings in bundles of 4. Some are loose. How many bundles?

**Short:**
> Nuria has 23 seedlings: bundles of 4, plus 3 loose. "How many bundles?" She writes a box for the unknown.

**Longer** (Spanish line for a fluent reader's check):
> Nuria counts the apple seedlings for Vadoalto. "Twenty-three. Some in bundles of four, and three loose ones. I forgot how many bundles."
>
> Dot writes it on the back of an order slip: **4 × ☐ + 3 = 23.**
>
> "The box is the number of bundles," says {guide}. "Undo the slip, last step first."
>
> "The last thing was adding three loose ones," says Nuria. "So take them away. Twenty."
>
> "Then undo the times," says Dot. "Twenty shared into fours."
>
> *"Cinco manojos,"* says Nuria. Five bundles. She checks: four times five is twenty, and three more is twenty-three.

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> Undoing runs the slip backward: take away the 3, then divide by 4. Then put 5 back in to check. Nuria's seedlings are made up.

## Teach cards

Each card is under 30 words. Worked steps appear one tap at a time.

**T1. A number sentence tells a story** (A true thing about math)
> A number sentence uses numbers and signs to tell what happens. Read the story first. Then write it.
Worked example: 1. Story: 3 trays, 4 buns on each. 2. Equal groups, so times. 3. Sentence: 3 × 4.

**T2. Read it without working it out** (A true thing about math)
> You can read a number sentence like a sentence. 3 × (1,250 + 375) means three lots of (1,250 + 375).
Worked example: 1. Look inside the brackets: one amount, 1,250 + 375. 2. Outside: × 3. 3. So it is 3 times as much. No adding needed.

**T3. Brackets mean "do this part first"** (A true thing about math)
> Brackets put a fence around one part. Work out the inside first. Then use that number.
Worked example: 1. (2 + 3) × 4. 2. Inside first: 2 + 3 = 5. 3. Then 5 × 4 = 20.

**T4. Brackets inside brackets** (A true thing about math)
> Start with the innermost brackets. Swap them for their number. Then work outward.
Worked example: 1. 2 × [3 + (7 − 1)]. 2. Innermost: 7 − 1 = 6. 3. Now 2 × [3 + 6] = 2 × 9 = 18.

**T5. Two readers, two answers** (A true thing about math)
> Read 8 + 2 × 5 two ways and you get two answers: 18 or 50. Writers and readers need one agreement.
Worked example: 1. Add first: 8 + 2 = 10, then × 5 = 50. 2. Times first: 2 × 5 = 10, then 8 + 10 = 18. 3. People agreed on 18.

**T6. The agreed order** (A true thing about math)
> Brackets first. Then × and ÷, left to right. Then + and −, left to right. Everyone who follows it gets the same answer.
Worked example: 1. 12 ÷ 3 + 4 × 2. 2. × and ÷: 4 + 8. 3. + and −: 12.

**T7. × and ÷ share a step** (A true thing about math)
> × and ÷ are on one step. + and − are on another. On one step, go left to right, whatever a song says.
Worked example: 1. 12 ÷ 2 × 3. 2. Left first: 12 ÷ 2 = 6. 3. Then 6 × 3 = 18, not 2.

**T8. Plan the steps** (A true thing about math)
> Read the story. Draw it. Plan each step and why. Do it. Then check the answer makes sense.
Worked example: 1. 3 boxes of 20 buns: 3 × 20 = 60. 2. Shared by 5 tables: 60 ÷ 5 = 12. 3. Each table eats 4: 12 − 4 = 8 left.

**T9. Undo it to check** (A true thing about math)
> Adding and taking away undo each other. So do times and divide. Undo an answer to check it.
Worked example: 1. 144 ÷ 8 = 18. 2. Undo with times: 18 × 8. 3. 144. It checks.

**T10. Last done, first undone** (A true thing about math)
> To undo two steps, undo the last step first. Like taking off boots before socks.
Worked example: 1. In: a number. × 3, then + 2. Out: 20. 2. Undo + 2: 20 − 2 = 18. 3. Undo × 3: 18 ÷ 3 = 6.

**T11. A box for the unknown** (A true thing about math)
> A box can stand for a number you don't know yet. Undo the sentence to find it. Then check.
Worked example: 1. ☐ + 7 = 15. 2. Undo + 7: 15 − 7 = 8. 3. Check: 8 + 7 = 15.

**T12. A letter for a number** (A true thing about math)
> A letter can stand for a number too. n is the number of bundles, not the bundles themselves.
Worked example: 1. 4 × n + 3 = 23. 2. Undo + 3: 20. 3. Undo × 4: n = 5. Check: 4 × 5 + 3 = 23.

**T13. Powers get their own step** (A true thing about math; Keeper's Desk)
> 2³ means 2 × 2 × 2. Powers and roots get a step of their own, just below brackets.
Worked example: 1. 5 + 2³ × 3. 2. Power: 5 + 8 × 3. 3. × and ÷: 5 + 24. 4. + and −: 29.

**T14. Undo by multiplying** (A true thing about math; Keeper's Desk)
> 5 × 1/5 = 1, so 1/5 is the reciprocal of 5. Dividing by 4 is multiplying by 1/4.
Worked example: 1. 20 ÷ 4 = 5. 2. 20 × 1/4 = 5. 3. Same answer. The reciprocal of 5 is 1/5, never −5.

## Real-world and history cards

**H1. The first equals sign** (A true thing from history; Seedling and up)
> In 1557, Robert Recorde, a Welsh doctor and teacher, printed a math book. He was tired of writing "is equal to". So he drew two lines.
Keeper's note:
> The book was *The Whetstone of Witte*. Recorde drew two long lines of the same length, because, he wrote, no two things could be more equal. His lines were much longer than ours. Other writers were slow to copy him: the sign next appeared in print about sixty years later.

**H2. The heap problems of Egypt** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> An Egyptian scroll from about 3,500 years ago has puzzles about an unknown "heap". One asks: a heap and its seventh together make 19. What is the heap?
Keeper's note:
> The scroll is the Rhind papyrus, copied by a scribe called Ahmes, now mostly in the British Museum. The scribe made a guess: 7. Seven and its seventh make 8, not 19. So the scribe scaled the guess up to fit. The answer is 16 5/8. This trick of guessing, then fixing, is called false position.

## Bench (a): Story Slips

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> Each slip tells a short café story. Some slips match their story. Some don't.

**Opening:** Read the story. Find the slip that tells it.
**Buttons:** Match · Build a slip · Don't work it out
**Don't work it out mode banner:** Read what the slip says. No adding allowed.

**Guided item:** 3 trays, 6 buns on each tray. Which slip matches?
- Step 1: Are the trays equal groups? *(Yes, 6 on each.)*
- Step 2: Equal groups means times: 3 × 6.

## Bench (b): The Bracket Board

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> Slide the brackets. The board works the slip out the agreed way and shows the new number.

**Opening:** Move the brackets. Watch the number change.
**Buttons:** Add brackets · Move · Clear · Show my steps
**Target banner:** Can you make the slip equal [number]?
**After a placement:** Inside first. Here are your steps, one line at a time.

**Guided item:** Put brackets in 1 + 4 × 5 to make 25.
- Step 1: Without brackets: 4 × 5 = 20, then 1 + 20 = 21.
- Step 2: To get 25, add first. Fence the 1 + 4.
- Step 3: (1 + 4) × 5 = 5 × 5 = 25.

## Bench (c): Two Readers

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> Pip reads along the line. The baker reads the agreed way. Watch both, then decide which one every reader would get.

**Opening:** Two readers, one slip. Who reads it the agreed way?
**The order steps (shown as a staircase):** Top step: brackets · Middle step: × and ÷, left to right · Bottom step: + and −, left to right
**Keeper's Desk staircase (four steps):** Brackets · Powers and roots · × and ÷, left to right · + and −, left to right
**Buttons:** Pip's way · The baker's way · Mark the steps

**Guided item:** 8 + 2 × 5. Which part comes first?
- Step 1: Any brackets? *(No.)*
- Step 2: Middle step: 2 × 5 = 10.
- Step 3: Bottom step: 8 + 10 = 18.

## Bench (d): The Plan Board

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> Each box on the board holds one step: what you do, why, and the number you get.

**Opening:** Plan each step. Say why. Then check it makes sense.
**Buttons:** Draw it · Add a step · Check it makes sense
**Check prompt:** Is your answer about right for the story?

**Guided item:** 5 tables, 6 guests at each, then 4 more guests arrive.
- Step 1: Equal groups first: 5 × 6 = 30.
- Step 2: 4 more join: 30 + 4 = 34.

## Bench (e): The Undo Oven

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> The oven runs a number through its steps. Run it backward to find what went in.

**Opening:** Run it forward. Then run it backward.
**Buttons:** Forward · Backward · Check by putting it back
**Box and letter banner:** The box (or letter) is a number you don't know yet.

**Guided item:** In goes a number. × 3, then + 2. Out comes 20.
- Step 1: Last step was + 2. Undo it: 20 − 2 = 18.
- Step 2: Then undo × 3: 18 ÷ 3 = 6.
- Step 3: Check: 6 × 3 + 2 = 20.

## Problem sets

Sixteen three-choice items put the right answer at A five times, B six times and C five times.

### Set A. Story and sentence (Sprout and Seedling)

**A1 (Sprout, order pictures)**
Prompt: The baker mixes, bakes, then slices. Put the pictures in order.
Answer: mixing bowl, then oven, then sliced loaf.
Tests: slicing before baking (order does not matter).
Hint: Can you slice bread that isn't baked yet?
Explanation: The order of the steps changes what you get.

**A2 (Sprout, 3-choice, pictures)**
Prompt: Ember says: jam on first, then fold. Which pancake is hers?
- A. Folded, with the jam inside. *(right)*
- B. Folded, with the jam on top. *(tests: order does not matter)*
- C. Flat, with no jam at all. *(tests: skipping a step)*
Hint: What happens to the jam when you fold?
Explanation: Jam first, then fold, hides the jam inside. The other order puts it on top.
Feedback A: Yes. Jam, then fold.
Feedback B: That one was folded first, then jammed. Order changes it.
Feedback C: Ember asked for jam. Look again.

**A3 (Sprout, 3-choice, pictures)**
Prompt: The slip says: a basket of rolls and a loaf. Which tray matches?
- A. A basket of rolls, and nothing else. *(tests: missing a part)*
- B. A basket of rolls and a loaf. *(right)*
- C. A loaf and a cake. *(tests: swapping an item)*
Hint: Check each thing on the slip.
Explanation: The tray matches when everything on the slip is there, and nothing else.
Feedback A: The loaf is missing.
Feedback B: Yes. Both things are there.
Feedback C: The slip asked for rolls, not cake.

**A4 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: 3 trays, 6 buns on each tray. Which slip matches?
- A. 3 + 6 *(tests: adding when there are equal groups)*
- B. 6 − 3 *(tests: grabbing the numbers in any way)*
- C. 3 × 6 *(right)*
Hint: Are there equal groups?
Explanation: Three equal groups of 6 is 3 × 6.
Feedback A: That's 3 buns and 6 more. Each tray has 6.
Feedback B: Nothing is taken away in this story.
Feedback C: Yes. Equal groups, so times.

**A5 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Story: 12 buns, and Pip eats 3. Which slip does NOT match?

- B. 12 + 3 = 15 *(right)*
- C. 9 + 3 = 12 *(tests: only one slip can fit a story)*
Hint: Does the number of buns go up or down?
Explanation: Pip eats some, so there are fewer. 9 + 3 = 12 fits too: the 9 left and 3 eaten.
Feedback A: That one matches: 12 take away 3.
Feedback B: Yes. Eating buns doesn't make more.
Feedback C: That fits too: 9 left plus 3 eaten makes the 12.

**A6 (Seedling, build a slip)**
Prompt: Build the slip: 4 bags of 5 rolls, then 2 more rolls.
Tiles: 4 · 5 · 2 · × · +
Answer: 4 × 5 + 2 (it makes 22).
Tests: 4 + 5 × 2 (the numbers in story order, but it means 14).
Hint: What happens first: the bags, or the extra rolls?
Explanation: Bags first, 4 × 5. Then add the 2. The agreed order does the times first anyway.

**B6 (Sapling, place brackets)**

### Set C. The agreed order (Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**C7 (Keeper's Desk, say why; spoken to a grown-up, not stored)**

Explanation: An agreement only works if both people use it.

**C9 (Keeper's Desk, number)**

Answer: (7 × 12 − 20) ÷ 4 = 16.
Check step: About 7 × 12 is 80. 80 − 20 = 60. 60 ÷ 4 = 15. Close to 16.
Tests: 7 × 12 − 20 ÷ 4 (no brackets, so it divides only the 20: that makes 79).
Hint: The sharing happens to everything that's left.
Explanation: Brackets keep the taking away before the sharing. The estimate says 16 is about right.

### Set E. Undo and unknowns (Seedling, Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**E1 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: The baker worked out 72 ÷ 8 = 9. Which check undoes it?
- A. Do 72 ÷ 8 again. *(tests: checking by repeating)*
- B. Work out 8 ÷ 72. *(tests: swapping the numbers is not undoing)*
- C. Work out 9 × 8. *(right)*
Hint: What undoes dividing?
Explanation: Times undoes divide. 9 × 8 = 72, so it checks.
Feedback A: Doing it again can repeat the same slip. Undo it instead.
Feedback B: Swapping numbers isn't undoing. Use the opposite operation.
Feedback C: Yes. 9 × 8 = 72.

**E2 (Seedling, number)**
Prompt: ☐ + 9 = 23. What number goes in the box?
Answer: 14.
Worked steps: 1. Undo + 9 by taking 9 away. 2. 23 − 9 = 14. 3. Check: 14 + 9 = 23.
Tests: 32 (adding instead of undoing: 23 + 9).
Hint: What undoes adding 9?
Explanation: Taking away undoes adding. Put 14 back to check.

**E3 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: In goes a number. × 4, then + 6. Out comes 38. What went in?
Answer: 8.
Worked steps: 1. Undo + 6: 38 − 6 = 32. 2. Undo × 4: 32 ÷ 4 = 8. 3. Check: 8 × 4 + 6 = 38.
Tests: 3.5 (undoing in the forward order: 38 ÷ 4 − 6); 158 (running it forward: 38 × 4 + 6).
Hint: Which step happened last?
Explanation: Last done, first undone. Undo the + 6 before the × 4.

**E4 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: There are 3 times as many rolls as loaves. Which equation fits?
Key: r is the number of rolls. l is the number of loaves.
- A. r = 3 × l *(right)*
- B. 3 × r = l *(tests: writing the words in order)*
- C. r = l + 3 *(tests: "3 times" read as "3 more")*
Hint: Try 2 loaves. How many rolls should there be?
Explanation: 2 loaves means 6 rolls. Only r = 3 × l gives 6.
Feedback A: Yes. Test it: 2 loaves, 6 rolls.
Feedback B: Test it: 2 loaves would mean less than one roll. Rolls should be more.
Feedback C: That's 3 more. With 2 loaves it gives 5, not 6.

**E5 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: Bags of 6 rolls, plus 5 loose, make 41. Solve 6 × n + 5 = 41.
Answer: n = 6 (six bags).
Worked steps: 1. Undo + 5: 41 − 5 = 36. 2. Undo × 6: 36 ÷ 6 = 6. 3. Check: 6 × 6 + 5 = 41.
Tests: 36 (stopping before undoing the × 6); a number that isn't whole (adding the 5 instead: 46 ÷ 6).
Hint: Last done, first undone. What was added last?
Explanation: n is the number of bags. Undo each step, then put 6 back to check.

**E6 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: A heap and its seventh together make 24. How big is the heap?
- A. 3 *(tests: stopping at the scale factor)*
- B. 21 *(right)*
- C. 17 *(tests: taking 7 away)*
Hint: Guess 7, like the scribe. 7 and its seventh make 8. Now scale up.
Explanation: 24 is 3 times 8, so the heap is 3 times 7: 21. Check: 21 + 3 = 24.
Feedback A: 3 is how much to scale the guess. Scale the 7.
Feedback B: Yes. 21 and its seventh, 3, make 24.
Feedback C: Check it: 17 and its seventh don't make 24.

**E7 (Keeper's Desk, true / not true from this source)**
Source: card H1, the first equals sign.
Prompt: Tap each sentence: true from this card, or not from this card.
- "Recorde drew two lines to mean 'is equal to'." *(true from this card)*
- "He chose them because no two things could be more equal." *(true from this card)*
- "Everyone used his sign by the next year." *(not from this card: it says others were slow)*

## Key terms (glossary)

- **number sentence**: numbers and signs that tell what happens, like 3 × 4 + 2.
- **expression**: a number sentence with no equals sign.
- **equation**: a number sentence with an equals sign: both sides are the same amount.
- **equals sign (=)**: two lines that mean "is the same amount as".
- **brackets**: a fence around part of a sentence that says "do this part first".
- **agreed order**: brackets, then × and ÷ left to right, then + and − left to right.
- **operation**: adding, taking away, times or dividing.
- **power**: a small raised number saying how many copies to multiply: 2³ = 2 × 2 × 2.
- **square root (√)**: the number that times itself makes this one: √100 = 10, because 10 × 10 = 100.
- **reciprocal**: the number that multiplies another to make 1: 1/5 for 5.
- **inverse**: the operation that undoes another one.
- **unknown**: a number you don't know yet, shown by a box or a letter.
- **solve**: find the unknown number.
- **check**: put your answer back in to see that it works.
- **estimate**: a quick "about" answer, to see if yours makes sense.
- **remainder**: what is left over after sharing into equal groups.
- **false position**: guess a number, see how far off it is, then fix the guess.

## Off-screen invitation (optional, grown-up-led)

**Four-card target** (Seedling and Sapling): deal four number cards from 1 to 9. Pick a target, like 24 or 10. Use +, −, ×, ÷ and brackets to hit it, and write the number sentence. Read it to someone else: do you both get the same answer? *Grown-up note: some deals have no answer. That's a finding, not a failure.*

**Kitchen slip** (Sprout): while cooking, say the steps out loud. Ask: what would happen in a different order?

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** writing a number sentence that matches a story, and spotting one that doesn't; brackets as "do this part first", including brackets inside brackets; the agreed order of operations (brackets, then × and ÷ left to right, then + and − left to right) as a convention that lets writers and readers agree; inverse operations to undo and check; planning multi-step problems and checking the answer makes sense; writing one- and two-step equations with a box or a letter, and solving by undoing; at the Keeper's Desk, powers and roots as one more step of the order, and reciprocals.

**What it does not claim:** powers and roots join the order only at the Keeper's Desk (T13, C8, C9); squares and square roots as inverses, and a minus sign beside a power, are left out. Equations stop at two steps with whole-number answers; longer algebra belongs to the Keeper's Desk. Mnemonics like PEMDAS and BIDMAS are named only to warn that their letters are not a queue.
