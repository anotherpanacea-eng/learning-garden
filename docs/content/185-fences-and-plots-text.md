## Story scenes

### Scene 1. The pegs are in the brook (Act I, chapter 4: mend the village)

**Hook:** The storm washed away the plot pegs and knocked the fences flat. Nobody knows where one plot ends and the next begins.

**Short:**
> The storm took the plot pegs. "Each plot was 6 meters by 4," says Hob. "We'll measure them again, and fence them."

**Longer:**
> The allotments are a sea of mud. The fences lie flat. The little wooden pegs that marked each corner are gone. Pip finds one in the brook, then another, then a duck sitting on a third.
>
> Hob Pennick leans on his wheelbarrow. "Every plot was 6 meters long and 4 meters wide," he says. "Same for everyone. We'll have to mark them out again."
>
> "And fence them," says Dot. "How much fence?"
>
> Pip starts counting posts. He loses count at eleven because the duck walks past.
>
> "Two questions," says {guide}. "How much ground is inside a plot? And how far is it all the way around? They sound alike. They aren't."
>
> Bramble's magpie lands on a fence post and looks at everyone as if the answer is obvious.

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> A 6 m by 4 m plot covers 24 square meters of ground. Its fence goes 6 + 4 + 6 + 4 = 20 meters around. One is ground inside; one is distance around.

### Scene 2. Fair plots (Act II, chapter 2: a garden across the seasons)

**Hook:** New families want plots. The town has a long strip of ground and wants to share it fairly.

**Short:**
> Three families each get 24 squares of ground. "Mine is the biggest," says Bramble. "It has the most fence."

**Longer:**
> Spring comes, and three new families ask for plots. Dot chalks squares on the ground, each one meter by one meter. "Twenty-four squares each," she says. "Fair."
>
> The first family gets 4 squares by 6. The second gets 3 by 8. Bramble, helping the third, lays out a plot 2 squares wide and 12 long, right along the path.
>
> "Mine's the biggest," says Bramble. "Look how much fence it needs."
>
> Pip counts the squares in all three. "Twenty-four. Twenty-four. Twenty-four."
>
> "Same ground," says {guide}. "Different fence. Count the edge of each one."
>
> Bramble counts. Her long plot needs 28 meters of fence. The 4 by 6 plot needs only 20. "So mine costs more to fence," she says slowly, "and grows the same carrots."
>
> "Exactly the same carrots," says Pip, who has been promised some.

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> All three plots cover 24 square meters. Fences: 4 by 6 needs 20 m, 3 by 8 needs 22 m, 2 by 12 needs 28 m. Area alone does not tell you the fence.

### Scene 3. How much land for the wet meadow? (Act IV, chapter 4: the proposal for the low fields)

**Hook:** Before the town meeting, the orchard keeper wants to know how much of her low field the wet meadow in *Some of Each* would take.

**Short:**
> The low field is an L shape. "How much would the wet meadow take?" asks the orchard keeper. "Show me in squares."

**Longer:**
> The orchard keeper unrolls a plan of the low field. It is not a rectangle. It is a big rectangle with one corner missing, where the old barn stands.
>
> "*Some of Each* puts a wet meadow here," she says, tapping the plan. "Thirty meters by twenty. How much of my field is that? I want to say a number at the meeting, not just 'a lot'."
>
> Nuria looks over her shoulder. "In Vadoalto we measure the reed beds every spring," she says. "*Paso a paso.* Step by step."
>
> Dot splits the L into two rectangles with a ruler. Pip takes the missing corner away from the big rectangle instead. They get the same answer, which annoys them both.
>
> "Two ways, one answer," says {guide}. "That's how you know it's right."

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> The low field: 60 m by 40 m, with a 20 m by 20 m corner missing. That is 2400 − 400 = 2000 square meters. The wet meadow, 30 m by 20 m, is 600. That leaves 1400 for the orchard. The made-up field is about half an acre (see card H2).

Nuria's Spanish line, *Paso a paso* ("step by step"), is checked by a fluent reader before build.

## Teach cards

Each card is at most 30 words. Worked steps appear one tap at a time.

**T1. Area is covering** (A true thing about math)
> Area is how much flat ground a shape covers. We measure it with squares. One square is one square unit.
Worked example: 1. Cover the tray with squares. 2. Count them: 12. 3. The area is 12 square units.

**T2. No gaps, no overlaps** (A true thing about math)
> Squares must sit edge to edge. A gap leaves ground uncounted. An overlap counts some ground twice.
Worked example: 1. Spot the gap. 2. Slide the square over. 3. Now every bit is covered once.

**T3. Same squares, same area** (A true thing about math)
> Move the same squares into a new shape. The area stays the same. Longer does not mean more ground.
Worked example: 1. 12 squares in a block. 2. Move them into a long line. 3. Still 12 square units.

**T4. Count in rows** (A true thing about math)
> Squares in a rectangle sit in equal rows. Count one row, then count on by rows.
Worked example: 1. One row has 6 squares. 2. Count on: 6, 12, 18, 24. 3. Four rows: 24 squares.

**T5. Rows times squares in a row** (A true thing about math)
> 4 rows of 6 is 4 × 6 = 24. Turn the rectangle: 6 rows of 4. Same squares, same 24.
Worked example: 1. Rows: 4. 2. In each row: 6. 3. 4 × 6 = 24 square units.

**T6. Length times width** (A true thing about math)
> Each meter along one side makes one more square in a row. So area = length × width, in square meters.
Worked example: 1. Plot: 7 m by 5 m. 2. 7 × 5 = 35. 3. Area: 35 square meters (35 m²).

**T7. Choose the square** (A true thing about math)
> Small things: square centimeters (cm²). Plots and fields: square meters (m²). A length unit cannot measure area.
Worked example: 1. A seed tray: cm². 2. A plot: m². 3. A fence: meters, since it is a length.

**T8. Split one side** (A true thing about math)
> Cut a rectangle with one line. Both pieces keep the same height. Add the two areas to get the whole.
Worked example: 1. 7 × 8: split 8 into 5 + 3. 2. 7 × 5 = 35 and 7 × 3 = 21. 3. 35 + 21 = 56.

**T9. L-shapes: split or take away** (A true thing about math)
> Split an L into two rectangles and add. Or take the missing corner from the whole rectangle. Both give one answer.
Worked example: 1. Split: 8 × 4 = 32 and 5 × 2 = 10. 2. 32 + 10 = 42. 3. Take away: 48 − 6 = 42.

**T10. Perimeter goes around** (A true thing about math)
> Perimeter is the distance all the way around the edge. Walk every side once and add. It is a length, in meters.
Worked example: 1. Square bed, 3 m each side. 2. 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 12. 3. Perimeter: 12 m.

**T11. Find the missing sides** (A true thing about math)
> Across a shape, the short sides on one edge add up to the long side opposite. Find every missing side before adding.
Worked example: 1. Bottom edge: 8 m. 2. Top: 3 m step, then a missing side. 3. 8 − 3 = 5 m.

**T12. Same ground, different fence** (A true thing about math)
> Equal areas can need different fences. Equal fences can hold different areas. Check each one; never assume.
Worked example: 1. 3 × 4 and 2 × 6: both 12 m². 2. Fences: 14 m and 16 m. 3. Different.

**T13. Estimate a bumpy shape** (A true thing about math; Sapling)
> A pond has curved edges. Count whole squares. Count a part square only if half or more is covered. Say "about".
Worked example: 1. Whole squares: 6. 2. Half or more covered: 2 more. 3. About 8 square meters.

## Real-world and history cards

**H1. Measuring after the flood** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> Nearly 2,500 years ago, a Greek writer, Herodotus, visited Egypt. People told him how land was measured again after the Nile flood washed part of a field away.
Keeper's note:
> Herodotus wrote that each farmer had a plot and paid tax on it. When the river carried part of a plot away, measurers came to find how much land was left, and the tax went down. He guessed that this is how geometry began. Nobody can be sure. A painting in an Egyptian tomb, more than 3,000 years old, shows men measuring a field with a rope.

**H2. A day's plowing** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> In old England, an acre was about the land a team of oxen could plow in a day. Later it got a fixed size.
Keeper's note:
> The fixed acre is 4,840 square yards. One old shape was a long strip, 1 chain wide and 10 chains long (a chain is 22 yards, about 20 m). Any shape with the same area is still one acre. An acre is about 4,047 square meters.

**H3. A field in a scroll** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> An Egyptian scroll copied about 3,500 years ago, the Rhind papyrus, has a problem about the area of a rectangle of land.
Keeper's note:
> The scroll is a book of worked problems for scribes. Today most of it is in the British Museum in London. Scribes measured land in their own units, much bigger than a meter.

## Bench (a): The Tile Tray

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> Each tile is one square unit. Tiles snap edge to edge. Real ground is never this tidy.

**Opening:** Cover the plot with tiles. Leave no gaps. Don't stack them.
**Buttons:** Add a tile · Take a tile · Move · Count · Compare two plots
**After covering:** Every bit is covered once. How many tiles?
**If a gap is left:** There's ground with no tile on it. Can you cover it?
**If tiles overlap:** Two tiles share some ground. Slide one over.

**Guided item:** Which plot covers more ground: the block or the long strip?
- Step 1: Cover both with tiles. *(Block: 12 tiles. Strip: 10 tiles.)*
- Step 2: More tiles, more ground. *(The block.)*
- Step 3: The strip is longer, but it covers less.

## Bench (b): The Row Planter

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> Each square is one meter by one meter. Plots have whole-meter sides.

**Opening:** Plant one row of squares. Then add rows.
**Buttons:** Add a row · Take a row · Turn the plot · Show the side lengths · Units: cm² or m²
**Running line:** "{rows} rows of {n}: {total} squares."
**After turning:** Same squares. The numbers swapped places. The area stayed.

**Guided item:** A plot has 4 rows of 6 squares. What is its area?
- Step 1: How many in one row? *(6.)*
- Step 2: Count on by rows: 6, 12, 18, 24.
- Step 3: 4 × 6 = 24 square meters.

## Bench (c): The Split Board

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> Plots drawn on squares, each one square meter. Cuts follow the grid lines.

**Opening:** Draw one line to cut the plot into two rectangles.
**Buttons:** Cut here · Undo cut · Show each piece · Take away a corner · Write it as numbers
**After a cut:** Both pieces keep the height. Add the two areas.
**After a second, different cut:** A different cut. Same total.

**Guided item:** Split a 4 × 12 plot into 4 × 10 and 4 × 2.
- Step 1: The big piece: 4 × 10 = 40.
- Step 2: The small piece: 4 × 2 = 8. It is still 4 tall.
- Step 3: 40 + 8 = 48. So 4 × 12 = 48.

## Bench (d): The Fence Walk

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> Pip walks the fence line. Each side lights up once as he passes it.

**Opening:** Walk Pip all the way around. Add each side once.
**Buttons:** Walk · Add this side · Find a missing side · Show inside lines
**Running line:** "So far: {total} m. Sides left: {left}."
**At a missing side:** This side has no number. Look at the side opposite.
**If an inside line is chosen:** That line is inside the plot. The fence only goes around the edge.

**Guided item:** A plot is 5 m long and 2 m wide. How much fence?
- Step 1: The two sides you can't see match the ones you can.
- Step 2: 5 + 2 + 5 + 2.
- Step 3: 14 m of fence.

## Bench (e): Same Ground

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> One square is one square meter. A rope shows the fence. Whole-meter sides only.

**Two modes:** Keep the ground (move the same tiles, watch the fence) · Keep the fence (bend the same rope, watch the ground)
**Table headers:** Length · Width · Area (m²) · Fence (m)
**After a new rectangle:** Write it in the table. What changed? What stayed?

**Guided item:** Make 12 squares into a different rectangle. Does the fence change?
- Step 1: 3 by 4. Fence: 3 + 4 + 3 + 4 = 14 m.
- Step 2: 2 by 6. Fence: 2 + 6 + 2 + 6 = 16 m.
- Step 3: Same 12 squares. Different fence.

**Puzzles** (Sapling and up; each has one answer):
- P1. Make a rectangle from 24 squares with exactly 20 m of fence. *(4 m by 6 m.)*
- P2. A rectangle covers 15 m² and needs 16 m of fence. What are its sides? *(3 m and 5 m.)*

**Open challenge** (no answer stored): Draw a plot with straight sides and square corners, and 40 m of fence. Show a grown-up.

## Problem sets

Fifteen three-choice items put the right answer at A five times, B five times and C five times.

### Set A. Covering and counting (Sprout and Seedling)

**A1 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which picture covers the plot the right way?
- A. Tiles with a gap in the middle. *(tests: gaps allowed)*
- B. Tiles stacked over each other. *(tests: overlaps allowed)*
- C. Tiles edge to edge, none on top. *(right)*
Hint: Look for bare ground, and for tiles on top of tiles.
Explanation: Edge to edge covers every bit of ground just once.
Feedback A: The gap is ground with no tile. It would not get counted.
Feedback B: Stacked tiles count the same ground twice.
Feedback C: Yes. Every bit covered, once.

**A2 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Both plots use the same tiles. Which covers more ground?
- A. They cover the same ground. *(right)*
- B. Bramble's long plot, because it is longer. *(tests: longer means more area)*
- C. You can't tell until you measure every side with a ruler. *(tests: withholds; the same tiles already answer it)*
Hint: Count the tiles in each plot.
Explanation: Same tiles, same ground. The shape changed; the area did not.
Feedback A: Yes. Same tiles, same ground.
Feedback B: Longer is not more. Count the tiles in each.
Feedback C: No ruler needed. The tiles are the same, so the ground is too.

**A3 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Pip moves the tiles into a new shape. The ground covered...
- A. ...gets bigger. *(tests: rearranging changes area)*
- B. ...stays the same. *(right)*
- C. ...gets smaller. *(tests: rearranging changes area)*
Hint: Did Pip add or take any tiles?
Explanation: No tiles added or taken, so the area stays the same.
Feedback A: Pip didn't add any tiles. Count them.
Feedback B: Yes. Moving tiles keeps the area.
Feedback C: Pip didn't take any tiles away. Count them.

**A4 (Seedling, number)**
Prompt: A plot is covered by 3 rows of 4 tiles. Area?
Answer: 12 square units.
Worked steps: 1. One row: 4 tiles. 2. Three rows: 4, 8, 12. 3. 12 square units.
Tests: 14 (counting the edges around, the perimeter); 7 (adding 3 and 4).
Hint: Count the tiles inside, not the edges.
Explanation: Area counts the squares that cover the plot.

**A5 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Dot writes "the area is 12". What should she add?
- A. Nothing. 12 is enough. *(tests: bare number for area)*
- B. Square units: 12 square units. *(right)*
- C. Units long: 12 units long. *(tests: length unit for area)*
Hint: 12 what?
Explanation: Area counts squares, so say square units.
Feedback A: 12 what? Say what was counted.
Feedback B: Yes. Area is counted in squares.
Feedback C: "Long" is for lengths. Area counts squares.

**A6 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Plot P: a strip of 10 squares. Plot Q: 3 by 4. Which is bigger?
- A. P, because it stretches much further. *(tests: longer means more area)*
- B. The same, because both look long. *(tests: undifferentiated "big")*
- C. Q, because it covers more squares. *(right)*
Hint: Count the squares inside each.
Explanation: Q covers 12 squares, P covers 10. Q has more area.
Feedback A: P is longer, but count the squares inside.
Feedback B: Count each. Are they really the same?
Feedback C: Yes. 12 squares is more ground than 10.

### Set B. Rows, columns and length times width (Seedling and Sapling)

**B1 (Seedling, number)**
Prompt: A plot has 4 rows of 6 squares. What is its area?
Answer: 24 square meters.
Worked steps: 1. One row: 6. 2. Count on: 6, 12, 18, 24. 3. 4 × 6 = 24.
Tests: 10 (adding 4 and 6); 20 (going around the edge).
Hint: How many squares in one row? How many rows?
Explanation: 4 rows of 6 is 4 × 6 = 24 squares.

**B2 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Hob turns the plot. Now it has 6 rows of 4. Area?
- A. 24 square meters, the same squares. *(right)*
- B. More than before, because it is taller now. *(tests: turning changes area)*
- C. 10 square meters. *(tests: adding the sides)*
Hint: Did any squares come or go?
Explanation: 6 × 4 = 4 × 6 = 24. Turning keeps the area.
Feedback A: Yes. Same squares, turned.
Feedback B: Taller, but no new squares. Count them.
Feedback C: 6 + 4 adds two sides. Area counts squares inside.

**B3 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Why does 4 × 6 count all the squares?
- A. Because 4 and 6 add up to the area. *(tests: adding sides for area)*
- B. Because there are 4 rows, with 6 in each row. *(right)*
- C. Because a rectangle has 4 sides. *(tests: confusing sides with rows)*
Hint: Point to the rows. How many in each?
Explanation: 4 rows of 6 is 4 lots of 6. That is 4 × 6.
Feedback A: 4 + 6 = 10, which is not the count.
Feedback B: Yes. Equal rows make multiplying work.
Feedback C: The 4 here counts rows, not sides.

**B4 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: A plot is 7 m long and 5 m wide. What is its area?
Answer: 35 m².
Worked steps: 1. Each row has 7 squares. 2. There are 5 rows. 3. 7 × 5 = 35 m².
Tests: 12 (adding the sides); 24 (the perimeter).
Hint: Length × width.
Explanation: 7 squares in a row, 5 rows: 35 square meters.

**B5 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which unit fits the area of a seed tray?
- A. Square centimeters, cm². *(right)*
- B. Square meters, m². *(tests: a square too big for the job)*
- C. Centimeters, cm. *(tests: length unit for area)*
Hint: A seed tray is small. And area needs squares.
Explanation: A small area fits small squares: square centimeters.
Feedback A: Yes. Small squares for a small tray.
Feedback B: One square meter is bigger than the whole tray.
Feedback C: Centimeters measure length. Area needs squares.

**B6 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: A plot covers 32 m². One side is 8 m. The other?
Answer: 4 m.
Worked steps: 1. 8 squares in each row. 2. How many rows make 32? 3. 32 ÷ 8 = 4 m.
Tests: 24 (taking away 8); 16 (halving 32).
Hint: 8 × what = 32?
Explanation: 8 × 4 = 32, so the other side is 4 m.

**B7 (Sapling, number)**
Picture: a pond on a 1 m grid. 10 whole squares; 4 squares half or more covered; 2 slivers.
Prompt: About how much ground does the pond cover?
Answer: about 14 m².
Worked steps: 1. Whole squares: 10. 2. Half or more covered: count 4 more. 3. Slivers: leave out. 4. About 14 m².
Tests: 10 (whole squares only); 16 (every touched square counted whole).
Hint: Count a part square only if half or more is covered.
Explanation: It is an estimate. The true area could be a little more or less.

### Set C. Splitting rectangles and L-shapes (Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

Plot L: 8 m along the bottom, 6 m up the left side, with a 3 m by 2 m corner missing at the top right.

**C1 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: Split a 7 × 8 plot into 7 × 5 and 7 × 3. Area?
Answer: 56 m².
Worked steps: 1. 7 × 5 = 35. 2. 7 × 3 = 21. 3. 35 + 21 = 56.
Tests: 38 (7 × 5 + 3, the 7 dropped on the small piece); 15 (adding 7 and 8).
Hint: Both pieces are 7 tall.
Explanation: The two pieces add up to the whole: 7 × 8 = 56.

**C2 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: 6 × 13 is cut into 6 × 10 and 6 × 3. Which matches?
- A. 6 × 13 = 6 × 10 + 3 *(tests: dropping the factor on one piece)*
- B. 6 × 13 = 6 + 10 + 3 *(tests: adding sides, not areas)*
- C. 6 × 13 = 6 × 10 + 6 × 3 *(right)*
Hint: How tall is the small piece?
Explanation: Both pieces are 6 tall: 60 + 18 = 78.
Feedback A: The small piece is 6 tall too. Its area is 6 × 3.
Feedback B: Those are side lengths. Add the two areas.
Feedback C: Yes. The 6 stays on both pieces.

**C3 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: Split Plot L into 8 × 4 and 5 × 2. Total area?
Answer: 42 m².
Worked steps: 1. Bottom piece: 8 × 4 = 32. 2. Top piece: 5 × 2 = 10. 3. 32 + 10 = 42 m².
Tests: 48 (the whole 8 × 6, as if no corner were missing); 28 (the fence around).
Hint: Find each piece's area. Then add.
Explanation: Two pieces, no gaps, no overlaps: 32 + 10 = 42.

**C4 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: The top piece of Plot L has no width written. How wide?
- A. 5 m, because 8 − 3 = 5. *(right)*
- B. 3 m, the same as the missing corner. *(tests: copying a nearby number)*
- C. 11 m, because 8 + 3 = 11. *(tests: adding when it should take away)*
Hint: The bottom is 8 m. The missing corner takes 3 m of it.
Explanation: The whole bottom is 8. Take away the 3 m corner: 5 m.
Feedback A: Yes. 8 − 3 = 5 m.
Feedback B: 3 m is the missing corner. What is left of 8?
Feedback C: The top is shorter than the bottom. Take away.

**C5 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: Low field: 60 by 40 m, minus a 20 by 20 m corner. Area?
Answer: 2000 m².
Worked steps: 1. Whole rectangle: 60 × 40 = 2400. 2. Missing corner: 20 × 20 = 400. 3. 2400 − 400 = 2000 m².
Tests: 2400 (keeping the missing corner); 2800 (adding the corner instead of taking it away).
Hint: Take the missing corner away from the whole.
Explanation: Taking away gives the same answer as splitting: 1200 + 800 = 2000.

**C6 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Bramble split Plot L into 8 × 4 and 5 × 6: 62. Why?
- A. Her pieces overlap, so some squares count twice. *(right)*
- B. She should have multiplied 32 by 30. *(tests: multiplying the pieces)*
- C. Nobody can say until every square is counted one by one. *(tests: withholds; the overlap shows why)*
Hint: Does the 5 × 6 piece start at the bottom or the top?
Explanation: Both pieces hold a 5 × 4 block: 62 − 20 = 42.
Feedback A: Yes. Pieces must not overlap.
Feedback B: Areas of pieces add. Multiplying makes far too much.
Feedback C: The picture already shows it: the pieces overlap.

**D6 (Keeper's Desk, number)**

### Set E. Same ground, different fence (Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**E1 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Plots 3 by 4 and 2 by 6 both cover 12 m². Their fences?
- A. The same, because the ground is the same. *(tests: same area means same perimeter)*
- B. Different: 14 m and 16 m. *(right)*
- C. You can't compare fences without a rope. *(tests: withholds; side lengths are enough)*
Hint: Add the four sides of each.
Explanation: 3 + 4 + 3 + 4 = 14. 2 + 6 + 2 + 6 = 16.
Feedback A: Same ground, but count the edges. Are they equal?
Feedback B: Yes. Same area, different fence.
Feedback C: The side lengths are enough. Add them up.

**E2 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: Bramble's plot is 2 m by 12 m. How much fence?
Answer: 28 m.
Worked steps: 1. Long sides: 12 + 12 = 24. 2. Short sides: 2 + 2 = 4. 3. 24 + 4 = 28 m.
Tests: 24 (the area, 2 × 12); 14 (only two sides).
Hint: Four sides: two long, two short.
Explanation: 28 m. The 4 by 6 plot covers the same 24 m² with 20 m.

**E3 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Hob has 20 m of fence. Which plot holds the most ground?
- A. They all hold the same, since the fence is the same. *(tests: same perimeter means same area)*
- B. 1 m by 9 m, because it is longest. *(tests: longer means more area)*
- C. 5 m by 5 m: 25 m². *(right)*
Hint: Find each area. 1 × 9? 5 × 5?
Explanation: 1 × 9 = 9 m². 5 × 5 = 25 m². Same fence, different ground.
Feedback A: Same fence, but work out each area.
Feedback B: Longest, but only 9 m². Try 5 × 5.
Feedback C: Yes. The square holds the most here.

**E4 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: An acre strip is 1 by 10 chains; another, 2 by 5. Extra fence?
Answer: 8 chains.
Worked steps: 1. Both: 10 square chains, one acre. 2. Fences: 1 + 10 + 1 + 10 = 22 and 2 + 5 + 2 + 5 = 14. 3. 22 − 14 = 8 chains.
Tests: 0 (same area, so same fence); 22 (one fence, not the difference).
Hint: Find both fences. Then take away.
Explanation: Same acre, but the long strip needs 8 more chains of fence.

**E5 (Keeper's Desk, true / not true from this source)**
Source: card H1, Measuring after the flood.
Prompt: Tap each sentence: true from this card, or not from this card.
- "Herodotus wrote that land was measured again after the flood." *(true from this card)*
- "He thought geometry may have begun this way." *(true from this card)*
- "This proves geometry began in Egypt." *(not from this card: he guessed, and nobody can be sure)*
- "Herodotus measured the fields himself." *(not from this card: people told him)*
Hint: Read the card again. Who told whom?
Explanation: A writer's guess is a clue. It is not proof.

**E6 (Keeper's Desk, say why; spoken to a grown-up, not stored)**
Prompt: Bramble says more ground always needs more fence. Is she right?
Model answer for the grown-up: No. A 1 m by 9 m plot covers 9 m² with 20 m of fence. A 4 m by 4 m plot covers 16 m² with only 16 m. More ground, less fence.
Tests: bigger area means bigger perimeter.
Hint: Try a long thin plot and a square one.
Explanation: Area and fence can change in different directions. Check both.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **area**: how much flat surface a shape covers, counted in squares.
- **square unit**: one square used to measure area.
- **square centimeter (cm²)**: a square 1 cm on each side.
- **square meter (m²)**: a square 1 m on each side.
- **tiling**: covering a shape with tiles, no gaps and no overlaps.
- **row**: squares side by side in a line across.
- **length and width**: the two side lengths of a rectangle.
- **rectilinear shape**: a shape with only straight sides and square corners.
- **compound shape**: a shape made of rectangles joined together.
- **perimeter**: the distance all the way around the edge.
- **acre**: an old unit of land area, 4,840 square yards.
- **chain**: an old unit of length, 22 yards, about 20 m.

## Off-screen invitation (optional, grown-up-led)

**Sticky-note tiling** (Sprout and Seedling): cover a placemat or a book with square sticky notes, edge to edge. Count them. Then move the same notes into a long line. *Grown-up note: ask "Is it still the same amount of covering?" before counting again.*

**String fence** (Sapling): lay a loop of string around a book, then reshape the same loop into a long thin rectangle and a square on squared paper. Count the squares inside each. *Grown-up note: the string stays the same length; watch the area change.*

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** area as covering with unit squares, with no gaps or overlaps; area kept when tiles move; rectangle area as rows × squares in a row, then length × width, in cm² or m²; splitting a rectangle to show the distributive property (7 × 8 = 7 × 5 + 7 × 3); area of L-shapes by splitting or subtracting; perimeter as distance around, including missing sides of L-shapes; rectangle formulas used both ways; estimating a bumpy area by part squares; same area with different perimeters, and the reverse.

**What it does not claim:** these are made-up plots and fields, not real survey work. It does not teach triangle, parallelogram or circle area (see spec 186), unit conversion between cm² and m², or areas with fractions. The history cards are short: Herodotus reports what he was told and offers a guess.
