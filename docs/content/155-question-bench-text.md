## Story scenes (A story from Willowbank)

### Act IV, chapter 1, *the old map and Hollis's account*: My hat did it

**Hook:** Bramble says her hat and boots got wet the same year. Does that mean the hat did it?

**Short:**
> "Wet hat, wet boots, same year," says Bramble. "My hat didn't soak my boots. The rain did both." Puff looks at the ice cream, then at the sun hat.

**Longer:**
> Page 4 sets the fine harvest beside the first big flood, five years apart. Half the café is sure one caused the other.
>
> "My hat and my boots both got wet that year too," says Bramble. "My hat didn't soak my boots. The rain did both."
>
> Puff has kept the café's summer week in a notebook: hot days, ice creams, sun hats from Dot's stall. "Ice creams and hats went up together," Puff says slowly. "So ice cream makes people buy hats?"
>
> "Or the sunshine made people buy both," says {guide}.
>
> Puff looks from the ice cream to the hat. "Show me."

**Keeper's note:**
> Two things changing together is a correlation. It can come from a hidden cause, a backwards arrow, or chance. A pattern is worth noticing; it can't pick the right explanation until the others are checked.

**Short:**

**Longer:**

### Act V project, *the night garden*: Do moths like white flowers?

**Hook:** The night garden is planted. Hob wonders if moths like the white flowers best.

**Short:**
> "Do moths like white flowers?" asks Hob. "We can't change a moth," says {guide}. "But we can count visits, night after night."

**Longer:**
> The night garden is in: white jasmine, pale stocks, red sweet williams. At dusk Hob Pennick leans on the fence. "Do the moths like the white ones best?"
>
> Pip reaches for the Change tray. "We'll do a fair test!"
>
> "What would we change, though?" says {guide}. "We can't make a moth try every flower."
>
> "We watch," says {guide}. "Same time each night, same patch, for several nights. We count visits to white flowers and to red ones."
>

**Keeper's note:**
> Not every good question is a fair test. Some are answered by watching over time, finding a pattern, sorting or research. Choosing the way is part of the question. Hob's dim lantern is a kept-the-same factor.

## Bench (a): From wonder to question

### Teach card (A true thing from science)
> A question you can answer names something to notice. You might watch it, try it, or look it up.

### Worked example: sharpening Pip's wonder (one step per tap)
1. Pip's wonder: *Why does bread go hard?* It's a start. What could we notice?
2. We could notice how soft the bread is. Feel it, or press it.
3. What could we compare? Bread kept in the tin, and bread left on the board.
4. The sharper question: *Does bread in the tin stay soft longer than bread on the board?*

### The four mats
**Watch it** (maybe for many days) · **Try it** (set up a test) · **Look it up or ask** · **Evidence can't settle it** (taste or choice)

### Wonder Board slips (each begins "I wonder...")
Each slip, its best mat, and the feedback on placing it:
- Which cloth dries the baker's trays fastest? **Try it.** "Set up the cloths and compare."
- When do the swallows come back each spring? **Watch it.** "Note the date, year after year."
- How old is the stone bridge? **Look it up or ask.** "The town records know."
- Which cake at the café is nicest? **Evidence can't settle it.** "People honestly like different cakes."
- Does the brook rise after a night of rain? **Watch it.** "Check the flood post after rain."
- Do seeds sprout faster in the warm shed? **Try it.** "Same seeds, warm and cool shed."

If a slip lands on another mat: "Could that work? Which way fits best?" Moving it back is always allowed.

### Guided item: the miller's ducks
**Wonder:** *Why are the mill-pond ducks so odd?*
- Step 1 (shown): What could you notice? *Where the ducks gather.*
- Step 2 (shown): What could you compare? *Morning and evening.*
- Prompt: Pick the sharper question.
- Choices: Why are the ducks so very odd? · The ducks love mornings best of all. · Are more ducks by the mill in mornings? ✓

### Sharpening frame chips
**Something to notice:** how soft · how far · how many visits · how long it takes · where they gather
**Something to compare:** tin or board · long wings or short · white flowers or red · morning or evening · warm shed or cool shed
Frame reads back: "Does [notice] change with [compare]?" A grown-up can help reword it aloud.

## Bench (b): Change, measure, keep

### Teach card (A true thing from science)
> In a fair test, change one thing. Measure one thing. Keep everything else the same, so you know what made the difference.

### Worked example: the baker's drying cloths (one step per tap)
1. Question: *Does the kind of cloth change how fast the trays dry?*
2. Change: the kind of cloth. That's what we choose.
3. Measure: the time until each tray is dry. That's what we read.
4. Keep the same: the trays, the water on each, the shelf. Otherwise we couldn't tell why.

### Factor tokens
cloth kind · time to dry · tray size · water on each tray · which shelf

### Why? chips (on a Keep token)
- If it changed too, we couldn't tell which change made the difference. ✓
- It makes the test look tidier. (`keep-for-neatness`)
- So the answer matches our guess. (`change-the-data`)

## Bench (c): Once is not enough (A made-up model)

### Teach card (A true thing from science)
> One try can fool you, so repeat it. The range puts a number on the spread: highest minus lowest.

### Worked example: long-wing spinner (one step per tap)
1. First drop: 2.4 seconds. One dot on the line.
2. Second drop: 2.6 seconds. Not quite the same.
3. Third drop: 2.5 seconds. The dots sit close together.
4. Spread: from 2.4 to 2.6. That's a range of 0.2 seconds.

**On screen:** *A made-up model: invented times to show the idea.*

### Guided item
Short-wing dots: 1.8, 2.0, 2.2.
- Step 1 (shown): Find the lowest dot.
- Step 2 (shown): Find the highest dot.
- Prompt: What is the spread?
- Answer chips: 1.8 to 2.2 ✓ · 2.0 to 2.2 · 1.8 to 2.0
- Feedback: right: "Yes. Lowest 1.8, highest 2.2." Others: "Check both ends. Use the lowest and the highest dot."

### Contrast cards
- **Separate spreads** (long and short wings): "Every long time is longer. These tries support: long wings fell more slowly."
- **Overlapping spreads** (Bramble's two spinners, set B6): "The spreads overlap. Can't tell yet."
- **An odd reading** (set B7): "Mark it, keep it, drop again."

### Keeper's Desk: two scales and a target (A made-up model)
> **Teach card:** Precise means repeats agree with each other. Accurate means they're close to the true value. You can have one without the other.

The baker's test weight is exactly 500 g; readings as in B8. The target picture shows the bakery dots tightly clustered off to one side, the market dots spread around the middle.

## Bench (d): Two things together (A made-up model)

### Teach card (A true thing from science)
> Two things can go up together without one causing the other. Ask: what else could make both happen?

### The café's summer week
| Day | Temperature | Ice creams sold | Sun hats sold |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mon | 18 °C (64 °F) | 12 | 2 |
| Tue | 22 °C (72 °F) | 20 | 4 |
| Wed | 25 °C (77 °F) | 31 | 6 |
| Thu | 16 °C (61 °F) | 9 | 1 |
| Fri | 28 °C (82 °F) | 40 | 8 |
| Sat | 21 °C (70 °F) | 18 | 3 |

**On screen:** *Made-up numbers for a made-up café.*

### Worked example: sort by temperature (one step per tap)
1. Tap **Sort by temperature**. Coolest first: Thursday, Monday, Saturday, Tuesday, Wednesday, Friday.
2. Ice creams now read 9, 12, 18, 20, 31, 40. Up every time.
3. Hats now read 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8. Up every time too.
4. Puff says ice cream makes people buy hats. But warm, sunny days could cause both.

### Escape-route cards
- **Hidden cause:** something else makes both go up.
- **Backwards arrow:** the second thing causes the first.
- **Just chance:** they happened together by luck.

### Guided item
- Step 1 (shown): Both rose on warmer days.
- Prompt: What else could make both go up?
- Choices: The hats · Warm, sunny weather ✓ · Puff's notebook
- Feedback: hats: "That's Puff's idea. Is there something behind both?" Weather: "Yes. Sun brings out ice creams and hats." Notebook: "The notebook records sales. It doesn't make anyone buy."

### Match the escape route
- Ice creams and hats both rise on warm days → **Hidden cause.** "The weather is behind both."
- More umbrellas are open when it rains → **Backwards arrow.** "Rain comes first."
- The magpie chattered on three market days in a row → **Just chance.** "Three days is too few to show more."

## Bench (e): I changed my mind

### Teach card (A true thing from science)
> Lock in your guess first. Then look. If they differ, change your idea. Never change the result.

### The ice case (A made-up model; the direction is real)
**Setup:** Two ice cubes, the same size. One sits on a metal baking tray, one on a wooden board. Both have been in the room all day.
- Prompt: Which ice cube will melt first?
- Choices to lock in: On the metal tray · On the wooden board · Both at the same time
- **Lock in my guess** (required before **Show the result**)
- Result card: "Metal tray: melted in about 9 minutes. Wooden board: about 20 minutes. *Made-up times.* The real result goes the same way."
- Why card: "Metal carries the room's warmth into the ice faster than wood. That's also why metal feels cold."
- Guess matched: "Your guess fits. Can you say why?" Guess differed: "A surprise teaches you something. Let's update the idea."
- Frame: "I guessed [guess]. I saw [result]. So now I think [metal melts ice faster]."
- Record line (never changes): "Result: metal tray first."

### Worked example: an idea that moved (A true thing from science; one panel per tap)
1. **The old idea.** Most scientists in 1915 thought the continents had always stayed put. That was reasonable for what they knew.
2. **A new idea.** Alfred Wegener, a scientist who studied weather, said the continents were once joined and had moved apart.
3. **His clues.** Coasts that fit like puzzle pieces. The same fossils on lands an ocean apart.
4. **Why people doubted.** Nobody could show what could push a whole continent.
5. **New evidence.** Scientists mapped magnetic stripes in ocean-floor rock. In the 1960s they showed new seafloor forming and spreading.
6. **The idea changed.** Most scientists changed their minds. Today we call it plate tectonics.

### Guided item
- Step 1 (shown): Wegener's clues were good, but one thing was missing.
- Prompt: What was missing?
- Choices: A force that could move continents ✓ · Any clues for the idea at all · A good map of the coasts
- Feedback: force: "Yes. Without a way it could happen, many stayed doubtful." Clues: "He had clues: coasts, rocks and fossils." Map: "Maps of the coasts were part of his clues."

### Sort: held because of evidence, or anything goes?
- "We think plates move because the seafloor stripes show it." → Held because of evidence
- "Science changed once, so any idea is as good as another." → Anything goes. Feedback: "Changing with better evidence is the opposite of anything goes."

## My question card

**Card heading:** My question card

**Prompts on the card:**
1. My question is...
2. I'll find out by... (watch it · try it · look it up or ask)
3. For a test: I'll change... I'll measure... I'll keep the same...
4. My guess is... (locked once a result is shown)
5. What would change my mind? (a result that goes the other way · a result that's the same both times · talk it over with a grown-up)

## Key terms

- **Testable question:** names something you could change or compare, and something to notice.
- **Change (independent variable):** the one thing you choose to make different.
- **Measure (dependent variable):** what you read or count to see what happened.
- **Keep the same (controlled variables):** everything else, held steady.
- **Spread / range:** how far apart repeated results are. Highest minus lowest.
- **Mean:** add the results, then share the total equally.
- **Precise:** repeats agree closely with each other.
- **Accurate:** results are close to the true value.
- **Correlation:** two things that change together.
- **Hidden cause:** a third thing that makes two others change together.
- **Evidence:** what you saw or measured that supports an idea.
- **Plate tectonics:** Earth's outer layer is in pieces that move slowly. It grew from Wegener's continental drift.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** the thinking under a science project: sharpening a wonder, choosing a way to find out, naming what changes, is measured and stays the same, reading repeats, asking "what else could explain this?", and changing an idea after a surprise. It builds on the Fair Test Bench, Water Evidence Shelf and Field Notebook.

**What it doesn't claim:** that your explorer can plan or run a real investigation alone. All numbers are made up except the dates in the continental drift story. Spoken "say why" answers are never stored.

**The real science, in brief:** Wegener proposed continental drift in 1912 (book 1915); most geologists doubted it because no force to move continents was known. Seafloor evidence in the 1960s, including Vine and Matthews's 1963 explanation of magnetic stripes, led to plate tectonics by the late 1960s and early 1970s. Ice melts faster on metal than on wood because metal conducts heat well. Sources: USGS, NASA Earth Observatory, Geological Society of London, Nuffield Foundation; full list in the source audit.
