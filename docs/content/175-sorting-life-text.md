# Sorting Life: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 175](../specs/175-sorting-life.md). Use as written, after review. `{guide}` is never gendered. Pronouns: the librarian they/them; Dot and Bramble she/her; Pip he/him; Nuria she/her; Léon he/him. Sources: [audit](../source-audits/175-sorting-life.md). Nothing is scored, timed or locked. The old Keeper is never named here.

**Privacy rule:** examples use real animals, plants and Willowbank's cards only. Nothing asks about the child's body, family or home. No human inheritance and no human chromosome numbers.

**Labels used throughout:**
- **A story from Willowbank** marks the town, its people and the card box (fiction).
- **A true thing about living things** marks real science.
- **A true thing from history** marks real people, dates and studies.
- **A made-up model** marks the drought model and the Moving Band.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. The spilled card box (Act I, mend the village)

**Hook:** The storm tipped the librarian's box of animal cards. Pip offers to sort them. He has a system.

**Short:**
> Pip sorts the cards into "wet ones" and "flying ones." The bat goes with the birds. The librarian says to sort by bodies instead.

**Longer:**
> Pip spreads the cards across the table. "Easy. Wet ones here. Flying ones there."
>
> Trout, frog and otter go in the wet pile. Dipper and bat go in the flying pile.
>
> "The bat has fur," says {guide}. "Do any birds have fur?"
>
> Pip looks at the bat card. Fur. Big ears. No beak.
>
> "Maybe it's a very fluffy bird," he says.
>
> "Bat mothers feed their babies milk," says the librarian. "Birds don't. Scientists sort by bodies, not by where an animal lives or how it moves."
>
> Pip moves the otter next to the bat. Then the sheep card, after a long look.
>
> "Fur, milk," he says. "This is the fur pile now."
>
> "That pile has a name," says the librarian. "Mammals."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about living things):**
> Bats are the only mammals that can truly fly. They have fur and feed their young milk, like otters and sheep. Wings alone never make an animal a bird.

>

>

### Scene 4. Reeds nobody planted (Act IV, the orchard's wet corner)

**Hook:** The low corner of the orchard stays wet now. Reeds grow there that nobody planted. The orchard keeper wants to know why.

**Short:**
> New reeds grow where the ground stays wet. The librarian says when a place changes, who lives there changes too.

**Longer:**
> The orchard keeper stands in the wet corner with her boots sinking. "Reeds. I never planted a single one."
>
> "Seeds blow in all the time," says the librarian. "Most never grow. Now the ground stays wet, and wet suits reeds."
>
> "So the reeds moved in," says Pip.
>

>

>

**Keeper's note (A true thing about living things):**
> In Spain's Sierra de Guadarrama, butterfly groups found in 2004-05 lived about 293 meters higher than similar groups in 1967-73. The mountain had warmed.

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer; steps appear one tap at a time. All are **A true thing about living things** unless marked.

**T1. Bodies, not homes**
> Scientists sort animals by their bodies. Where an animal lives, or how it moves, can trick you.

Worked example: *A trout and an otter both swim.*
1. Trout: gills, fins, scales.
2. Otter: fur, lungs, feeds young milk.
3. Different bodies, different groups: fish and mammal.

**T2. Five groups with backbones**
> Mammals have fur or hair and feed young milk. Birds have feathers. Fish have gills and fins. Reptiles have dry scales. Amphibians have moist skin.

Worked example: *A wall lizard.*
1. Look at its skin: dry and scaly.
2. Which group has dry scales? Reptiles.
3. Reptile.

**T3. Tricky animals**
> A dolphin lives in the sea and is a mammal. A penguin cannot fly and is a bird. Check the body.

Worked example: *A penguin.*
1. It swims and cannot fly.
2. It has feathers and a beak.
3. Feathers decide: bird.

**T4. The big groups**
> Plants make their own food from light. Animals eat other living things. Fungi soak up food they break down. Micro-organisms are too small to see alone.

Worked example: *A mushroom.*
1. Does it make its own food? No.
2. Does it eat other living things? No.
3. It soaks up food from rotting wood: a fungus.

**T5. Decisive or shared?**
> A decisive feature separates the groups you are comparing. A shared feature cannot decide. Color and size never decide.

Worked example: *A bat and a dipper.*
1. Both have wings: shared, so it cannot decide.
2. Bat has fur; dipper has feathers.
3. Fur and feathers decide.

**T6. Claim, evidence, reason**
> Say the group. Give two pieces of evidence. Say why they settle it.

Worked example: *An otter.*
1. Claim: it is a mammal.
2. Evidence: it has fur; it feeds its young milk.
3. Reason: only mammals have both.

**T7. A key splits**
> A key asks yes or no questions. Each question splits a group in two. Keep going until each ending holds one.

Worked example: *Trout, frog, dipper.*
1. Feathers? Yes: dipper.
2. No: fins? Yes: trout.
3. No: frog. Three animals, two questions.

**T8. A table remembers**
> A table puts animals down the side and features across the top. Ticks show the evidence for your key.

Worked example: *Bat row.*
1. Feathers: no tick.
2. Fur: tick. Wings: tick.
3. The row shows why the key sends the bat where it does.

**T9. Pick the graph**
> Counts in groups: bar graph. One thing measured over time: line graph. Two measurements compared: scatter graph.

Worked example: *Beak depth every year for ten years.*
1. One measurement: beak depth.
2. It changes over time.
3. Line graph.

**T10. One name for everyone (A true thing from history)**
> Scientific names have two parts: the genus, then the species. Carl Linnaeus spread this way of naming in the 1750s.

Worked example: *Cinclus cinclus.*
1. Cinclus: the dipper genus.
2. cinclus: this species.
3. Same name in Spanish, French and English books.

**T11. Same bones, different jobs**
> A bat's wing, a whale's flipper and a horse's leg have matching bones. Matching bones suggest a shared ancestor long ago.

Worked example: *Find the upper bone.*
1. In the bat's wing, one long bone near the body.
2. In the flipper, one short thick bone in the same place.
3. Same bone, shaped for a different job.

**T12. Islands and beaks**
> Galápagos finches have different beaks for different foods. They came from one ancestor. When food changes, the group's beaks can change over generations.

Worked example: *A dry year.*
1. Small seeds run out. Big hard seeds remain.
2. Deep-beaked finches crack them and raise young.
3. Next generation: deeper beaks on average.

**T13. A moving band**
> Each kind lives where conditions suit it. When a mountain warms, the suitable band moves up. Populations follow. Nobody decides.

Worked example: *A band from 2,400 to 2,600 meters.*
1. It warms. The band moves up 100 meters.
2. Young butterflies settle in the new band.
3. The population now sits higher.

**T14. The zoom (Keeper's Desk)**
> A cell holds a nucleus. The nucleus holds chromosomes. Each chromosome is one long, coiled DNA molecule. A gene is one section of that DNA.

Worked example: *Zoom in four taps.*
1. Cell, then its nucleus.
2. A chromosome, then uncoil it: one long DNA.
3. Highlight one stretch: a gene.

### Set A. Five trays (Sprout, Seedling)

**A1 (Sprout, guided).** A trout has gills, fins and scales. Which tray?
- Steps shown: 1. Look at its body. 2. Gills and fins. 3. Which group has gills?
- A. Amphibians `water-means-group` / **B. Fish** / C. Reptiles `scales-only`
- Hint: Which group breathes with gills all its life?
- Explanation: Gills and fins belong to fish. Frogs live by water but have moist skin.
- Feedback A: Frogs swim too, but they have moist skin. Look at the gills.
- Feedback B: Yes. Gills and fins make it a fish.
- Feedback C: Reptiles have dry scales and lungs. Gills decide this one.

**A2 (Sprout).** A bat has fur and feeds its babies milk. Which group?
- **A. Mammals** / B. Birds `flies-means-bird` / C. Insects
- Hint: Fur and milk go together in one group.
- Explanation: Fur and milk make a bat a mammal. Flying does not decide the group.
- Feedback A: Yes. Fur and milk: mammal, even with wings.
- Feedback B: Birds have feathers. A bat has fur.
- Feedback C: Insects have six legs and no fur. Look at the fur.

**A3 (Seedling).** A dolphin lives in the sea and breathes air. Which group?
- A. Fish `water-means-fish` / B. Amphibians / **C. Mammals**
- Hint: Fish breathe with gills. Who breathes air and feeds young milk?
- Explanation: Dolphins breathe air and feed calves milk. Home does not decide the group.
- Feedback A: Fish breathe water with gills. A dolphin comes up for air.
- Feedback B: Amphibians have moist skin and start life in water. Dolphins feed calves milk.
- Feedback C: Yes. Air and milk make it a mammal.

**A4 (Seedling, match).** Match each card to its group.
- Answer: wall lizard, reptile; fire salamander, amphibian; dipper, bird; otter, mammal; penguin, bird.
- Wrong matches tested: salamander to reptile `looks-like-lizard`; penguin to fish `swims-means-fish`; dipper to fish `water-means-fish`.
- Hint: Check skin, feathers and fur. Ignore where each one lives.
- Explanation: A salamander's skin is moist, not scaly. A penguin has feathers, so it is a bird.

**A5 (Seedling, number).** The box holds 12 cards. 4 are mammals. How many are not?
- Answer: 8.
- Steps: 1. All cards: 12. 2. Take away mammals: 12 − 4. 3. 8 cards.
- Wrong answers tested: 16 (added instead of took away); 4 (counted only mammals).
- Hint: Start with 12 and take the mammals away.
- Explanation: 3 birds, 2 fish, 2 amphibians and 1 reptile make 8.

**D5 (order the cards).** Order from biggest to smallest: chromosome, cell, gene, nucleus.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Vertebrate:** an animal with a backbone.
- **Mammal:** fur or hair; feeds its young milk.
- **Amphibian:** moist skin; young usually start in water.
- **Fungus:** a living thing that soaks up food it breaks down. Mushrooms and molds are fungi.
- **Micro-organism:** a living thing too small to see without a microscope.
- **Decisive feature:** a feature that separates the groups you are comparing.
- **Key:** yes/no questions that sort living things.
- **Genus and species:** the two parts of a scientific name.
- **Homologous:** matching bones, used for different jobs.
- **Evolution:** change in a group of living things over many generations.
- **Range:** the area where a kind of living thing is found.
- **Chromosome:** one long, coiled DNA molecule in a cell's nucleus.
- **Gene:** a section of DNA that affects a feature.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** sorting vertebrates into five groups by body features; the broad groups of living things; giving two decisive reasons; following and building keys and choosing a graph; and, at older depths, evidence for evolution from bones, islands, fossils and DNA, how species' ranges move, and a gentle model of chromosomes and genes.

**What it does not claim:** it does not teach invertebrate groups, plotting graphs, human ancestry, human inheritance or human chromosome numbers. Finch species counts differ by source (13 to 18). The five vertebrate groups are one useful scheme; in family-tree classification, birds sit inside the reptile branch, descended from dinosaurs. Franklin did not share the 1962 prize: she died in 1958, and Nobel Prizes are not given after death. The drought model and the Moving Band are made-up models; their limits are stated on screen. Patterns of range change differ by place and species.

**Sources in brief:** Linnaeus's dates and finch, coral, fungi, homology, whale-hippo, DNA history and range-shift figures are in the [source audit](../source-audits/175-sorting-life.md), with items still marked [check].

**Off-screen (optional, adult-led):** sort a pile of animal pictures from old magazines, first by the child's own rule, then by body features. No live animals handled. Outdoors, watch birds from a distance and talk about which features you can see.
