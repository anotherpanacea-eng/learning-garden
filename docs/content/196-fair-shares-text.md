## Story scenes

### Scene 1. Ember's square tart (Act II, chapter 3: Ember's birthday)

**Hook:** Ember's birthday tart is square. The baker cuts it into quarters, corner to corner. Bramble is not sure.

**Short:**
> The baker cuts Ember's square tart corner to corner. Four triangles. "Those aren't quarters," says Bramble. "Quarters are little squares." The baker smiles.

**Longer:**
> Ember's birthday tart is square, with honey on top. The baker cuts it from corner to corner, twice. Four triangles sit on the board.
>
> "Those aren't quarters," says Bramble. "Quarters are little squares. My grandmother always cut them that way."
>
> "Then let's cut a second tart your grandmother's way," says the baker. He makes little squares.
>
> {guide} lays a grid of baking paper over both. "Count the squares under each piece."
>
> Pip counts, slowly, with his nose nearly on the honey. "Four under each triangle. Four under each little square."
>
> "Same size," says Dot. "Different shape."
>
> Bramble looks at the triangles for a long time. "Fine," she says. "They're quarters. But I'm having a square one."
>
> Ember, who has been very patient, eats a triangle.

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> The tart is 16 grid squares, so each quarter covers 4. A quarter means one of four equal parts, by size. Shape doesn't matter. The baker also sets out 12 honey buns: three quarters for guests (9), one quarter kept back (3).

### Scene 2. Three pies, four traders (Act III, chapter 1: the floating market)

**Hook:** Four traders from Vadoalto are hungry. The baker has three pies. Pip says it can't be done.

**Short:**
> Four traders, three pies. "Not enough," says Pip. "Someone gets none." The baker cuts each pie into quarters. Everyone gets three.

**Longer:**
> The traders from Vadoalto tie up their boats. Four of them, all hungry. The baker has three pies left.
>
> "Not enough," says Pip. "Three pies, four traders. Someone gets none."
>
> The baker cuts every pie into quarters. He hands out one quarter from each pie, round and round, until the plates are full.
>
> "Three quarters each," says the oldest trader, counting. "Three pies, shared by four."
>
> Later, two boats unload their reeds. One boat brought 3 bundles. The other brought 5. The orchard keeper has 40 apples to pay with.
>
> "Half each?" asks Pip.
>
> "The second boat brought more reeds," says Dot. "Fair isn't always half."
>
> They count the bundles: 8 in all. They share the apples into 8 equal heaps.

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> 3 ÷ 4 = 3/4 of a pie each. Check: four plates of 3/4 make 12/4, which is 3 pies. For the apples: 40 ÷ 8 = 5 in each heap. Boats get 15 and 25. The first boat gets 3/8 of all the apples.

### Scene 3. A letter about pies (Act III, chapter 4: letters up and down the river)

**Hook:** Nuria writes from Vadoalto. They cut pies in thirds there. She has a question.

**Short:**
> Nuria writes: "¡Hola desde Vadoalto! (Hello from Vadoalto!) We cut pies in thirds. You cut in quarters. Is 2/3 more than 3/4?"

**Longer** (Spanish lines for a fluent reader's check):
> *¡Hola desde Vadoalto!* (Hello from Vadoalto!)
>
> Dear friends in Willowbank,
>
> Our baker cuts every pie in thirds. Your baker cuts in quarters. My brother says two thirds is more than three quarters, because his pieces are bigger.
>
> I say he is only counting pieces. But our pies are bigger than yours, too. Now we are both confused.
>
> Who gets more pie? Please write back.
>
> *Un abrazo* (A hug),
>
> Nuria

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> On the same pie, 3/4 is more than 2/3: each is one piece short of whole, and a quarter is smaller than a third. On a bigger pie, 2/3 can be more pie. Always ask: of what whole?

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer. Worked steps appear one tap at a time.

**T1. Fair shares are equal parts** (A true thing about math)
> Fair shares are equal parts of one whole. Equal means the same size. Count the parts, not the cuts.
Worked example: 1. Two straight cuts across a tray bake. 2. Three parts. 3. All the same size, so each is one third.

**T2. Same size, new shape** (A true thing about math)
> Equal parts can look different. Cut a square corner to corner: four triangles. Cut it in strips: four strips. Each is a quarter.
Worked example: 1. The square covers 16 grid squares. 2. Each triangle covers 4. 3. Each strip covers 4. 4. Same size: all quarters.

**T3. Naming a fraction** (A true thing about math)
> The bottom number, the denominator, says how many equal parts make the whole. The top number, the numerator, counts the parts we mean.
Worked example: 1. A pie in 4 equal parts. 2. We take 3. 3. Three quarters, written 3/4.

**T4. A fraction of a set** (A true thing about math)
> To find a quarter of 12 buns, share them into 4 equal groups. One group is a quarter.
Worked example: 1. 12 buns into 4 equal groups. 2. 3 in each group. 3. 1/4 of 12 is 3. 4. 3/4 is three groups: 9.

**T5. Same fraction, different whole** (A true thing about math)
> Half of a big pie is more pie than half of a small pie. Same fraction, different whole. Always ask: half of what?
Worked example: 1. Half of 8 rolls is 4. 2. Half of 12 rolls is 6. 3. Both are halves; the wholes differ.

**T6. Sharing more than one whole** (A true thing about math)
> Share 3 pies among 4 friends: cut each pie into quarters. Each friend gets 3 quarters. So 3 ÷ 4 = 3/4.
Worked example: 1. 3 pies, 4 quarters each: 12 quarters. 2. 12 quarters, 4 friends: 3 each. 3. Check: 4 × 3/4 = 3 pies.

**T7. Find one, then scale** (A true thing about math)
> To change a recipe, find the amount for one first, then multiply. Adding the difference doesn't work.
Worked example: 1. 4 pies need 8 cups of flour. 2. 1 pie needs 8 ÷ 4 = 2 cups. 3. 6 pies need 6 × 2 = 12 cups.

**T8. Fair, but not equal** (A true thing about math)
> Share 40 apples 3 to 5: that makes 3 + 5 = 8 equal parts. One part is 5 apples.
Worked example: 1. 40 ÷ 8 = 5. 2. 3 parts: 15. 5 parts: 25. 3. The first share is 3/8 of all, since 8 parts make the whole.

**T9. Jumps on a line** (A true thing about math)
> On the counter line, 0 to 1 is one whole. Split it into equal spaces. Jump one space at a time from 0.
Worked example: 1. Five equal spaces: each is 1/5. 2. Three jumps land on 3/5. 3. 3/5 = 1/5 + 1/5 + 1/5.

**T10. Past one, and whole numbers** (A true thing about math)
> Keep jumping past 1. Four quarter-jumps land on 1, so 4/4 = 1. Eight land on 2. And 3 = 3/1.
Worked example: 1. 4/4 lands on 1. 2. 5/4 is one quarter past 1. 3. 8/4 lands on 2.

**T11. Zoom in** (A true thing about math)
> Between any two fractions there are more. Split quarters into eighths to find them. 0.5 and 5/10 name the same point.
Worked example: 1. Mark 1/4 and 1/2. 2. Split into eighths: 2/8 and 4/8. 3. 3/8 sits between them.

**T12. Same top, same bottom** (A true thing about math)
> Same denominator: same-size parts, so more parts is more. Same numerator: same count of parts, so bigger parts win.
Worked example: 1. 5/6 and 2/6: five sixths beats two. 2. 3/4 and 3/8: quarters are bigger than eighths. 3. 3/4 is more.

**T13. Use half and one** (A true thing about math)
> Is it more than half? Less? Close to one? Landmarks can settle a comparison fast.
Worked example: 1. Half of 7 is 3.5, so 3/7 is less than half. 2. Half of 8 is 4, so 5/8 is more. 3. 5/8 is bigger.

**T14. Same-size parts** (A true thing about math)
> When landmarks don't help, cut both into same-size parts. Then compare the counts.
Worked example: 1. Thirds and quarters both fit twelfths. 2. 2/3 = 8/12; 3/4 = 9/12. 3. 9 twelfths beats 8: 3/4 is more.

**T15. Measure to the nearest quarter inch** (A true thing about math)
> Each inch on the strip splits into halves and quarters. Find the mark nearest the end. Say its value, not "the third line".
Worked example: 1. The end is between 4 1/4 and 4 1/2. 2. It's nearer 4 1/2. 3. Say "4 1/2 inches".

**T16. A line plot** (A true thing about math)
> A line plot is a number line with one X for each thing measured. Leave empty marks where nothing landed.
Worked example: 1. Two breadsticks are 4 1/2 inches. 2. Two Xs stack above 4 1/2. 3. Nothing at 4 1/4: leave it empty.

**T17. Say it exactly** (A true thing about math)
> Give the unit: 9 inches, not 9. Write money with two places: $3.50, not $3.5. Name numerator and denominator correctly.
Worked example: 1. "The loaf is 9." 2. Nine what? 3. "The loaf is 9 inches."

## History cards

**H1. Bread for ten** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> About 3,500 years ago in Egypt, a scribe named Ahmes copied a scroll of math problems. The first six share loaves of bread among ten people.
Keeper's note:
> The scroll is now called the Rhind papyrus; most of it is in the British Museum. In problem 3, six loaves are shared by ten. Each person gets 1/2 + 1/10 of a loaf: six tenths. Egyptian scribes wrote most fractions as sums of different unit fractions. A table in the scroll splits 2/5 into 1/3 + 1/15.

**H2. Which is more, and by how much?** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> A Chinese book called *The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art* is about 2,000 years old. It gives rules for fractions, including how to compare two of them.
Keeper's note:
> Its first chapter is about measuring fields, and it holds the fraction rules. One rule finds which of two fractions is more, and by how much. In the year 263, a mathematician named Liu Hui wrote notes explaining why the book's methods work.

## Bench (a): The Cut Board

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> Pies and tarts on a grid. Each grid square is the same size. Pieces are fair when they cover the same number of squares.

**Opening:** Cut the tart into fair shares.
**Buttons:** Cut · Undo cut · Show the grid · Count squares · Check sizes
**After Check sizes (equal):** Every piece covers the same number of squares. Fair shares.
**After Check sizes (unequal):** These pieces cover different numbers of squares. Try moving a cut.
**Gallery prompt:** Find another way to make four fair shares.

**Guided item:** Quarter the square tart a new way.
- Step 1: Cut it into strips. *(Four strips, 4 squares each.)*
- Step 2: Now cut corner to corner. *(Four triangles.)*
- Step 3: Count the squares under one triangle. *(4. Same size: also quarters.)*

## Bench (b): The Share Tray

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> Buns, pies and apples that never crumble. Every bun is the same; every pie on one tray is the same size.

**Opening:** Share the tray into equal groups.
**Buttons:** Add a group · Deal one round · Take back · Pack a group · Show the sentence
**Sentence pattern:** "[fraction] of [whole] is [amount]." · "[pies] shared by [people] is [fraction] each."

**Guided item:** Find 3/4 of 12 honey buns.
- Step 1: Make 4 equal groups. *(3 buns in each.)*
- Step 2: One group is 1/4: 3 buns.
- Step 3: Pack three groups. *(9 buns: 3/4 of 12.)*

**Pie mode opening:** More people than pies? Cut, then deal.
**Recipe card mode:** The recipe card shows flour for 4 pies. Find one pie first.

## Bench (c): The Counter Line

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> A brass line along the counter, from 0 to 3. Each whole is the same length. You choose how many equal spaces.

**Buttons:** Split each whole · Jump forward · Jump back · Zoom in · Name the point
**Jump readout:** "[n] jumps of 1/[d]: [n]/[d]."
**At a whole number:** "[n]/[d] lands on [w]."
**Keeper's Desk extension:** The line stretches left past 0 to the cold cellar's −2.

**Guided item:** Where does 3/4 go?
- Step 1: Find 0 and 1. That's one whole.
- Step 2: Split 0 to 1 into 4 equal spaces.
- Step 3: Jump three spaces from 0. *(3/4, before 1.)*

## Bench (d): The Comparing Shelf

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> Fraction bars on the same whole, all the same length. Bars on different wholes stay apart, labeled.

**Buttons:** Same whole · Mark half · Mark one · Cut to same-size parts · Order them
**Symbol strip:** < · = · >
**Symbol reminder:** The open side faces the bigger amount.

**Guided item:** Which is more, 3/8 or 3/4 of a pie?
- Step 1: Put both on the same pie.
- Step 2: Both have 3 pieces. Which pieces are bigger? *(Quarters.)*
- Step 3: 3/4 > 3/8.

## Bench (e): The Measuring Strip

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> A strip marked in inches, halves and quarters. Breadsticks snap to the nearest quarter inch.

**Buttons:** Lay the breadstick · Read the end · Add an X · Show the totals
**Plot reminder:** One X for each breadstick. Every quarter-inch mark stays on the line.

**Guided item:** Measure the honey breadstick.
- Step 1: Line its end up with 0.
- Step 2: The other end is just short of 4 1/2.
- Step 3: Nearest mark: 4 1/2. Say "4 1/2 inches".

## Problem sets

Twenty-one three-choice items put the right answer at A seven times, B seven times and C seven times.

### Set A. Equal parts (Sprout and Seedling)

**A1 (Sprout, 3-choice, pictures)**
Prompt: Which tart is shared fairly for four friends?
- A. Four pieces, one much bigger than the rest. *(tests: counting pieces, not size)*
- B. Four pieces, all the same size. *(right)*
- C. Three pieces, all the same size. *(tests: too few shares)*
Hint: Fair means the same size, for each friend.
Explanation: Four friends need four pieces, all the same size.
Feedback A: Four pieces, but one friend gets more. That's not fair.
Feedback B: Yes. Four friends, four same-size pieces.
Feedback C: Same size, but one friend gets none.

**A2 (Sprout, 3-choice, pictures)**
Prompt: Four triangles cover as much as four strips. Are triangles fair shares too?
- A. Yes. Each covers the same amount. *(right)*
- B. No. Fair pieces must look like the strips. *(tests: equal means same shape)*
- C. We can't know without tasting them. *(tests: withholding; the grid shows the sizes)*
Hint: Look at the grid under each piece.
Explanation: Fair means same size. A different shape can be the same size.
Feedback A: Yes. Same size, different shape.
Feedback B: Shape doesn't matter. The grid shows each covers the same.
Feedback C: The grid already shows it. Each covers the same squares.

**A3 (Sprout, 3-choice, pictures)**
Prompt: Which pie has half eaten?
- A. A pie in three pieces, one piece gone. *(tests: any one piece is a half)*
- B. A pie with one small slice gone. *(tests: half means "a piece")*
- C. A pie in two same-size pieces, one gone. *(right)*
Hint: A half is one of two same-size parts.
Explanation: Two same-size parts make halves. One is gone.
Feedback A: Three pieces make thirds. Half needs two same-size parts.
Feedback B: That slice is small. Half is one of two equal parts.
Feedback C: Yes. Two equal parts, one eaten.

**A4 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Three straight cuts make four equal parts. What is each part?
- A. 1/3, because there are three cuts. *(tests: counting cuts)*
- B. 1/4, because there are four equal parts. *(right)*
- C. 1/2, because each piece is a part. *(tests: calling any part a half)*
Hint: Count the parts, not the cuts.
Explanation: Four equal parts, so each is one quarter: 1/4.
Feedback A: Count the pieces. Three cuts made four.
Feedback B: Yes. Four equal parts, so quarters.
Feedback C: A half is one of two. Here there are four.

**A5 (Seedling, sort)**
Prompt: Sort each tray: quarters, or not quarters?
- Square cut corner to corner → quarters
- Square cut into four strips → quarters
- Square cut into four small squares → quarters
- Round pie in four pieces, one huge → not quarters
- Tray bake in four pieces of 2, 2, 3 and 5 squares → not quarters
- Tray bake in three pieces of 4 squares each → not quarters (thirds)
Tests: equal parts must match in shape; four pieces always means quarters; three equal parts called quarters.
Hint: Count the squares under each piece. Are there four?
Explanation: Quarters are four parts, all the same size. Shape can differ.

**A6 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: The tray has 12 squares. Ember's piece covers 3. Is it a quarter?
- A. Yes. Four pieces that size fill all 12 squares. *(right)*
- B. No. It isn't a square piece. *(tests: equal means same shape)*
- C. No. A quarter would cover 4 squares. *(tests: reading the denominator as a count of squares)*
Hint: How many pieces this size fill the tray?
Explanation: 4 × 3 = 12. Four equal pieces fill it, so each is a quarter.
Feedback A: Yes. Four pieces of 3 squares make the whole tray.
Feedback B: Shape doesn't matter. Four of them fill the tray.
Feedback C: The 4 counts pieces, not squares. Four pieces of 3 fill 12.

### Set B. Parts of a set, parts of an amount (Seedling and Sapling)

**B1 (Seedling, number)**
Prompt: What is 1/4 of 12 honey buns?
Answer: 3 buns.

Tests: 4 (giving the denominator); 8 (taking 4 away from 12).
Hint: Make 4 equal groups.
Explanation: One quarter is one of four equal groups: 3 buns.

**B2 (Seedling, 3-choice)**

- A. 3 buns. *(tests: stopping at one group)*
- B. 4 buns. *(tests: reading the bottom number as the answer)*
- C. 9 buns. *(right)*
Hint: Find one quarter first. Then take three.
Explanation: One quarter is 3 buns. Three quarters is 3 + 3 + 3 = 9.
Feedback A: That's one quarter. You need three of them.
Feedback B: The 4 says how many groups. How many buns in three groups?
Feedback C: Yes. Three groups of 3.

**B3 (Seedling, 3-choice, pictures)**

- B. Half of the big pie. *(right)*
- C. We can't tell until the pies are cut. *(tests: withholding; both wholes are shown)*

Feedback A: Both are halves, but of different wholes. Half of what?
Feedback B: Yes. Half of a bigger whole is more.

**B4 (Sapling, 3-choice)**

- B. Dot. 12 is more than 8. *(tests: looking only at the whole)*
- C. Neither. Each gets 4 rolls. *(right)*

Feedback A: True for one whole. Here the wholes differ. Count the rolls.
Feedback B: Dot's whole is bigger, but her fraction is smaller. Count the rolls.
Feedback C: Yes. 4 rolls each.

**B5 (Sapling, number with bar model)**

Answer: 12 loaves.

Tests: 4 (one fifth only); 15 (taking 5 from 20).
Hint: What is one fifth of 20 loaves?
Explanation: 3/5 of 20 must be less than 20. 12 loaves fits.

### Set C. Sharing several wholes, recipes and traders (Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**C1 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: 3 pies are shared fairly by 4 traders. How much does each get?
- A. 4/3 of a pie. *(tests: dividing the bigger number by the smaller)*
- B. Nothing fair. 3 is less than 4. *(tests: "can't divide a smaller number by a bigger one")*
- C. 3/4 of a pie. *(right)*
Hint: Cut every pie into 4. Deal the pieces.
Explanation: 12 quarters, 4 traders: 3 quarters each. 3 ÷ 4 = 3/4.
Feedback A: 4/3 is more than a whole pie each. That needs more than 4 pies.
Feedback B: It can be shared. Cut each pie into quarters first.
Feedback C: Yes. Check: four lots of 3/4 make 3 pies.

**C2 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: 4 pies need 8 cups of flour. How much for 6 pies?
Answer: 12 cups.
Worked steps (double number line): 1. 4 pies: 8 cups. 2. 1 pie: 8 ÷ 4 = 2 cups. 3. 6 pies: 6 × 2 = 12 cups.
Tests: 10 (adding 2 because there are 2 more pies); 2 (stopping at one pie).
Hint: How much flour for one pie?
Explanation: Find one first, then multiply. Adding the difference gives too little.

**C3 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: 3 loaves cost $12. What do 5 loaves cost?
Answer: $20.
Worked steps: 1. One loaf: $12 ÷ 3 = $4. 2. Five loaves: 5 × $4 = $20.
Tests: $14 (adding $2 for 2 more loaves); $4 (stopping at one loaf).
Hint: What does one loaf cost?
Explanation: Each loaf costs $4, so 5 loaves cost $20.

**C4 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: 6 loaves are shared fairly by 10 rowers. How much does each get?
Answer: 6/10 of a loaf (3/5 and 1/2 + 1/10 are accepted too).
Worked steps: 1. Cut each loaf into 10 equal parts: 60 tenths. 2. 60 ÷ 10 = 6 tenths each. 3. Check: 10 × 6/10 = 6 loaves.
Tests: 10/6 (dividing the bigger number by the smaller); "0 r 6" (stopping at a remainder).
Hint: Cut each loaf into 10. Deal the pieces.
Explanation: 6 ÷ 10 = 6/10. The scroll in card H1 says 1/2 + 1/10: the same amount.

**C5 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: One boat brought 3 bundles, one brought 5. Share 40 apples fairly by bundles.
Answer: 15 apples and 25 apples. The first boat gets 3/8 of the apples.
Worked steps (bar model): 1. 3 + 5 = 8 equal parts. 2. 40 ÷ 8 = 5 apples in each part. 3. 3 × 5 = 15 and 5 × 5 = 25. 4. 15 + 25 = 40: all shared.
Tests: 20 and 20 (sharing equally, ignoring the bundles); 3/5 as the first boat's fraction (using one share as the whole).
Hint: How many equal parts in all?
Explanation: The whole has 8 parts, so 3 parts is 3/8, not 3/5.

**C6 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: Five traders each eat 3/8 of a pie. How much in all? Pies to bake?
Answer: 15/8 of a pie is eaten; bake 2 pies.
Worked steps: 1. 3/8 + 3/8 + 3/8 + 3/8 + 3/8. 2. 15 eighths: 15/8. 3. 8 eighths is one pie, so 15/8 is one pie and 7/8 more. 4. Bake 2 pies.
Tests: 15/40 (making the pieces smaller too); 5 3/8 (adding 5 instead of taking five lots).
Hint: Count the eighths. How many make one pie?
Explanation: Each piece stays an eighth. You can't bake 7/8 of a pie, so bake 2.

- C. No. Both are three tenths: one point. *(right)*
Hint: Split 0 to 1 into tenths. Count three.
Explanation: 0.3 and 3/10 are two names for one point.
Feedback A: One line holds them all. Count three tenths.
Feedback B: 0.3 is less than 1. It's three tenths.
Feedback C: Yes. Two names, one place.

**D6 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Is there a fraction between 1/4 and 1/2?
- A. Yes. Zoom to eighths: 3/8 sits halfway. *(right)*
- B. No. They're next to each other. *(tests: the line has no room between)*
- C. Only one: 1/3. *(tests: withholding; zooming finds more, like 3/8 and 5/16)*
Hint: Split the quarters in half. What's between?
Explanation: 1/4 = 2/8 and 1/2 = 4/8, so 3/8 is between. Zoom again for more.
Feedback A: Yes. And zooming again finds even more.
Feedback B: Zoom in. Eighths show a point between.
Feedback C: 1/3 fits, but so do 3/8, 5/16 and many more.

**D7 (Keeper's Desk, order and symbols)**
Prompt: Order on one line: −1, 3/4, 0.5, 7/4, −1/2. Then pick symbols.
Answer: −1 < −1/2 < 0.5 < 3/4 < 7/4. Symbols: 0.75 = 3/4; −1 < −1/2; 7/4 ≠ 4/7; 3/4 ≥ 0.75 is true.
Worked steps: 1. Negatives go left of 0. 2. −1 is farther left than −1/2. 3. 0.5 is half; 3/4 is more; 7/4 is past 1.
Tests: −1/2 placed left of −1 (ordering negatives by size); ≥ read as only >.
Hint: Leftmost is least. Which is farthest left?
Explanation: Order by place on the line, for every kind of number.

### Set E. Which is more? (Seedling, Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**E1 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which is more of the tray: 2/6 or 5/6?
- A. 5/6 > 2/6. More same-size parts. *(right)*
- B. 2/6 > 5/6. Smaller numbers are bigger. *(tests: over-using "bigger number, smaller part")*
- C. They're equal. Both are sixths. *(tests: looking only at the bottom number)*
Hint: Same-size parts. Who has more of them?
Explanation: Sixths are all the same size. Five beats two.
Feedback A: Yes. Same-size parts, more of them.
Feedback B: That idea is for bottom numbers. Here sixths match: count them.
Feedback C: Both are sixths, but 5 of them is more than 2.

**E2 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which is more pie: 3/8 or 3/4?
- A. 3/8, because 8 is more than 4. *(tests: bigger bottom number means bigger fraction)*
- B. The same. Both have 3 pieces. *(tests: looking only at the top number)*
- C. 3/4, because quarters are bigger pieces. *(right)*
Hint: Three pieces each. Which pieces are bigger?
Explanation: Cutting into 8 makes smaller pieces than cutting into 4.
Feedback A: More cuts make smaller pieces. Eighths are smaller.
Feedback B: Three pieces each, but quarters are bigger.
Feedback C: Yes. 3/4 > 3/8.

**E3 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Use half. Which is more, 3/7 or 5/8?
- A. 5/8. It's more than half; 3/7 is less. *(right)*
- B. They're equal. Both are close to half. *(tests: "close" taken as equal)*
- C. 3/7. It's more than half. *(tests: misusing the half landmark)*
Hint: Half of 7 is 3.5. Half of 8 is 4.
Explanation: 3 is less than 3.5. 5 is more than 4. So 5/8 is bigger.
Feedback A: Yes. One is below half, one above.
Feedback B: Close isn't equal. One is on each side of half.
Feedback C: Half of 7 is 3.5. 3 is less.

**E4 (Sapling, order)**
Prompt: Order smallest to largest: 2/3, 3/5, 7/10.
Answer: 3/5, 2/3, 7/10.
Worked steps: 1. Same-size parts: thirtieths. 2. 2/3 = 20/30; 3/5 = 18/30; 7/10 = 21/30. 3. 18, 20, 21.
Tests: 2/3, 3/5, 7/10 (ordered by bottom number or by top number); 7/10, 3/5, 2/3 (bigger bottom number taken as a smaller fraction).
Hint: Cut all three into thirtieths.
Explanation: In same-size parts, just compare the counts.

**E5 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which is more: 7/5 or 4/3?
- A. They're equal. Both are one and a bit. *(tests: stopping at the landmark)*
- B. 7/5. In fifteenths, 21/15 beats 20/15. *(right)*
- C. 7/5, because 7 > 4 and 5 > 3. *(tests: right answer, digit reasoning)*
Hint: Both are more than 1. Try fifteenths.
Explanation: 7/5 = 21/15 and 4/3 = 20/15. 7/5 is a little more.
Feedback A: Both pass 1. Same-size parts show which is more.
Feedback B: Yes. One fifteenth more.
Feedback C: Right fraction, risky reason. Bigger digits don't always mean bigger. Use same-size parts.

**E6 (Keeper's Desk, order)**
Prompt: Order smallest to largest: 3/4, 7/8, 5/6, 11/12.
Answer: 3/4, 5/6, 7/8, 11/12.
Worked steps: 1. Each is one piece short of 1. 2. Missing pieces: 1/4, 1/8, 1/6, 1/12. 3. Smallest missing piece is closest to 1. 4. Check in 24ths: 18, 20, 21, 22.
Tests: 3/4, 7/8, 5/6, 11/12 (taking the gap 1/8 as bigger than 1/6, because 8 is more than 6).
Hint: How far is each from one whole?
Explanation: Smaller gap to 1, bigger fraction. Same-size parts always work too.

**E7 (Keeper's Desk, say why; spoken to a grown-up, not stored)**
Prompt: Pip drew 3/4 and 2/3. "3/4 looked longer, so it's true." Settled?
Model answer for the grown-up: A drawing can be a little off, so it doesn't prove it. A reason that always works: on the same whole, 3/4 = 9/12 and 2/3 = 8/12. Or: each is one piece short of a whole, and a quarter is smaller than a third.
Tests: a picture or one example taken as proof; comparing by digits.
Hint: Would your reason work even with a messy drawing?
Explanation: A reason about the numbers convinces even someone who doubts the drawing.

### Set F. Measures and mixed problems (Seedling, Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**F1 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: The breadstick ends just short of the 4 1/2 mark. How long?
- A. 4 1/2 inches. *(right)*
- B. The second line after 4. *(tests: recording the tick, not its value)*
- C. 4 inches, the last number passed. *(tests: always going back to the whole number)*
Hint: Which mark is nearest the end?
Explanation: The nearest mark is 4 1/2. Say its value and the unit.
Feedback A: Yes. Nearest mark, with its unit.
Feedback B: Lines have values. That mark is 4 1/2 inches.
Feedback C: It's much nearer 4 1/2 than 4.

**F2 (Sapling, plot then number)**
Prompt: Plot the six breadsticks. How many are longer than 4 1/2 inches?
Lengths on the card: 4, 4 1/2, 4 1/2, 4 3/4, 5 and 5 inches.
Answer: 3 breadsticks (4 3/4, 5 and 5). The plot has two Xs at 4 1/2, two at 5, one each at 4 and 4 3/4, and an empty mark at 4 1/4.
Tests: 2 (counting only the 5s); leaving out the empty 4 1/4 mark (plotting like a bar chart).
Hint: Look to the right of 4 1/2. Count the Xs.
Explanation: Every quarter mark stays, even an empty one. Three Xs sit past 4 1/2.

**F3 (Sapling, fix it)**
Prompt: Fix each sentence so it says exactly what it means.
- "A pie slice costs $3.5." → "$3.50" *(two places for cents; $3.5 can be misread as $3.05)*
- "In 3/4, the top number is the denominator." → "the top number is the numerator" *(the denominator is the bottom number)*
- "The loaf is 9." → "The loaf is 9 inches." *(give the unit)*
Hint: Read each one as a stranger would. What could they misread?
Explanation: Exact words and units mean nobody has to guess.

**F4 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: Four jugs hold 3/4, 1/2, 7/8 and 7/8 cup. Share equally. How much each?
Answer: 3/4 cup each.
Worked steps: 1. In eighths: 6/8, 4/8, 7/8, 7/8. 2. Total: 24/8 = 3 cups. 3. 3 ÷ 4 = 3/4 cup each.
Tests: 18/22 (adding tops and adding bottoms); 7/8 (picking the most common amount).
Hint: Change all to eighths. Add them. Then share by 4.
Explanation: Pour it all together, then share: total ÷ 4.

**F5 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Ember has 20 berries. Altogether, she eats 3/4 of them. How many?
- A. 23 berries. *(tests: "altogether" means add)*
- B. 15 berries. *(right)*
- C. 80 berries. *(tests: multiplying by 4; more than she had)*
Hint: What is 1/4 of 20? Then 3/4?
Explanation: 1/4 of 20 is 5, so 3/4 is 15. Less than 20, which makes sense.
Feedback A: "Altogether" doesn't always mean add. What is 3/4 of 20?
Feedback B: Yes. And 15 is less than 20, so it makes sense.
Feedback C: She only had 20. 3/4 of them must be fewer.

**F6 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: The line shows 1/4 above, 0.25 and 25% below. How many points?
- A. Three points, one for each kind. *(tests: three different amounts)*
- B. Two: 25% sits at 25. *(tests: reading 25% as the number 25)*
- C. One point, with three names. *(right)*
Hint: 25% means 25 out of 100.
Explanation: 1/4 = 25/100 = 0.25 = 25%. One amount, three ways to write it.
Feedback A: One line, one amount. Three names for it.
Feedback B: 25% is 25 hundredths, not 25 wholes.
Feedback C: Yes. One amount, three names.

## "I'm stuck" worked examples

Step 2 of the ladder: an easier case, then how to start this one.

- **Equal-part items:** "A sandwich cut corner to corner: two triangles, same size. Halves. Start: are your pieces the same size?"
- **Count-the-parts items:** "One cut across a loaf makes two parts. Start: count the pieces, not the cuts."
- **Fraction-of-a-set items:** "1/2 of 6 apples: two groups of 3. Start: how many equal groups?"
- **Same-fraction items:** "Half a grape or half a melon? Same fraction, more melon. Start: what is each whole?"
- **Several-wholes items:** "2 pies, 4 friends: halves, one half each. Start: cut each whole into as many parts as people."
- **Recipe and price items:** "2 apples cost $4, so 1 costs $2. Start: what does one cost?"
- **Unequal-share items:** "Share 10 nuts 2 to 3: 5 parts of 2. Start: add the parts first."
- **Line items:** "Two half-jumps from 0 land on 1. Start: how big is each jump?"
- **Comparing items:** "1/2 or 1/4 of the same pie? Halves are bigger. Start: same whole?"
- **Measuring items:** "The end is right on 3: 3 inches. Start: which mark is nearest?"

## Key terms (glossary)

- **whole**: the one thing, or group, being shared.
- **equal parts**: parts that are the same size. They can be different shapes.
- **fraction**: a number that names equal parts of a whole.
- **unit fraction**: one equal part, like 1/3 or 1/8.
- **numerator**: the top number. It counts the parts we mean.
- **denominator**: the bottom number. It says how many equal parts make the whole.
- **half, quarter**: one of two, or one of four, equal parts.
- **number line**: a line where every number has its own place.
- **benchmark**: a landmark number, like 1/2 or 1, to compare with.
- **line plot**: a number line with an X for each thing measured.
- **inch**: a unit of length, about the width of a grown-up's thumb.
- **share in a ratio**: share so each gets the right number of equal parts.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** equal parts as equal size, including different shapes; fractions of a set and of an amount; unit and non-unit fractions; the same fraction of different wholes; sharing several wholes (3 ÷ 4 = 3/4); scaling a recipe from one; sharing in a ratio; fractions on a number line, past 1, between fractions, and beside decimals and negative numbers; comparing fractions by part size, by landmarks and by same-size parts; measuring to the nearest quarter inch and making a line plot.

**What it does not claim:** these are made-up pies and models, not real recipes or measurements. It does not teach simplifying, mixed-number conversion, adding fractions or multiplying fractions as rules; those are in nearby packs. Percent and negative numbers appear only as recaps.
