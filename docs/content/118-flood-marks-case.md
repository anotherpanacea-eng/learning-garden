## 1. Why this case

Hollis kept a flood post. Downstream, Les Saules does the same thing differently: it marks great floods on walls, with a date and a line. Across France, towns do this by law, and the marks are public records anyone can look up. The case uses that likeness for three things: the explorer's first sustained reading in French, a measurement lesson that matters (two records can only be compared if they measure from the same zero), and a short piece of real history about why towns mark floods at all.

French and English are offered side by side, at the explorer's choice, the same as Nuria's Spanish letters. French reading is never required to finish the case.

**Passage-amount boundary:** the letter and history panels each have an explicit Short / Longer choice, kept separately in this case's eventual frozen source. Ask only questions supported by the currently supplied passages. Letter tasks 1a and 1b work from either amount; 1c and 1d require the Longer letter. History task 4a works from either amount; 4c requires the Longer history panel, and 4d requires the grandfather's words in the Longer letter. In a shorter route, leave those optional questions absent and their responses unobserved, with an optional **Read the longer passage** action. Opening it supplies the relevant text and records support before the extra task appears. Withholding a source-dependent question never blocks keeping the final letter. Task 4b may use the already-dried page 2 in Act III, without introducing a later page.

Cast follows spec 120: the librarian uses they/them and the miller she/her. Language review must preserve that distinction in any new English/French lines.

## 2. Hook (fiction, labelled Story)

> {courier} comes back from Les Saules with a rolled-up drawing tied with string, and a letter from Léon.

**Léon's letter, short**

> **Français :** Bonjour, Willowbank ! Sur le mur de la maison du bac, il y a des marques. Chaque marque montre la hauteur d'une crue. La tempête est arrivée jusqu'à la troisième. Et chez vous ? Léon
>
> **English:** Hello, Willowbank! On the wall of the ferry house there are marks. Each mark shows how high a flood came. The storm reached the third one. What about yours? Léon

**Léon's letter, longer**

> **Français :**
> Bonjour, Willowbank !
> Merci pour votre registre. Ici, nous n'avons pas de poteau comme le vôtre. Nous avons des marques sur le mur de la maison du bac. On les appelle des *repères de crues*. Chaque repère porte un nom et une ligne : l'eau est montée jusque-là. Le plus haut est très ancien. Mon grand-père disait qu'il ne fallait jamais l'effacer, « pour que personne n'oublie ». Après la tempête, nous avons ajouté un nouveau repère. Je vous envoie un dessin du mur. Vous aussi, vous allez marquer la tempête ?
> Léon
>
> **English:**
> Hello, Willowbank!
> Thank you for your records. Here we don't have a post like yours. We have marks on the wall of the ferry house. We call them *repères de crues*, flood marks. Each mark has a name and a line: the water came up to here. The highest is very old. My grandfather used to say we must never paint over it, "so that nobody forgets". After the storm, we added a new mark. I'm sending you a drawing of the wall. Will you mark the storm too?
> Léon

**Glossary** (tap a word in the French): *le mur* (wall); *la maison du bac* (ferry house); *la marque* (mark); *la hauteur* (height); *la crue* (flood, when a river rises); *le repère* (marker, reference point); *jusque-là* (up to there); *effacer* (to wipe out, paint over); *oublier* (to forget); *ajouter* (to add).

## 4. Step 1: What does Léon say?

**Learn card:**

> You can understand a lot of a French letter before you know every word.
>
> 1. Look for words that look like English: *marque* (mark), *tempête* (tempest, storm), *ajouté* (added).
> 2. Look for numbers and names.
> 3. Read the sentence around a word you don't know. Then check the glossary.
>
> Worked example: *Chaque marque montre la hauteur d'une crue.* *Marque* looks like mark. *Hauteur* is in the glossary: height. *Montre* isn't in the glossary, but *each mark ___ the height of a flood* only makes sense one way: **shows**.

**Guided first task (1a):** the short letter in French only, with three words highlighted to guess from context before the glossary opens.

Tasks (each is a three-choice question; replies are authored for every choice):

- **1a.** *Sur le mur de la maison du bac, il y a des marques.* Where are the marks? *Fits:* on the ferry house wall. *Others:* on the ferry; on a post by the river.
- **1b.** *La tempête est arrivée jusqu'à la troisième.* What does Léon say about the storm? *Fits:* it reached the third mark. *Others:* it came three times; it lasted three days.
- **1c.** (Longer letter) *Mon grand-père disait qu'il ne fallait jamais l'effacer.* What did his grandfather say about the highest mark? *Fits:* never paint over it. *Others:* paint it every year; it was painted by mistake.
- **1d.** (Longer letter) Why keep the highest mark, in Léon's grandfather's words? The explorer finds and taps the quotation: « pour que personne n'oublie ». *Answer: "so that nobody forgets".*

**I'm stuck (1b):**

1. *Hint:* "*Troisième* looks like *trois*, which is three. What could be 'the third' on a wall of marks?"
2. *Similar example:* "*Le chat est monté jusqu'à la deuxième branche.* *Deuxième* is like *deux*, two. The cat climbed up to the second branch. Now: *jusqu'à la troisième*."
3. **Skip for now.**

**Replies to likely errors:**

| Chosen | Misconception | Reply |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "It came three times" for 1b | Reading a number word as a count | "*Troisième* is 'third', like 3rd, not 'three times'. The third what?" |
| "On the ferry" for 1a | Taking one familiar word (*bac*) for the whole place | "*Bac* is the ferry. But it's *la maison du bac*. What's a *maison*?" |

## 5. Step 2: Reading the wall

**Léon's drawing** (fiction). Lines on the wall, with their heights in metres above the stone floor of the ferry landing. Léon has written the heights beside them in pencil.

| Mark (French, as on the wall) | English | Height above the landing floor |
| --- | --- | --- |
| La grande crue | The great flood | 2.30 m |
| La tempête | The storm | 1.85 m |
| Crue du printemps | The spring flood | 1.10 m |
| Crue d'automne | The autumn flood | 0.75 m |

**Learn card:**

> 1 metre is 100 centimetres. To change metres to centimetres, multiply by 100: 1.85 m is 185 cm.
>
> To find how much higher one mark is than another, subtract. Do it in centimetres if decimals feel slippery: 230 − 185 = 45 cm.

Tasks (Check my working):

- **2a.** Put the marks in order from lowest to highest. *Answer: autumn 0.75, spring 1.10, storm 1.85, great flood 2.30.* (Note: this confirms Léon's "the third one" counted from the bottom.)
- **2b.** The storm in centimetres. *Answer: **185 cm**.*
- **2c.** How much lower was the storm than the great flood? *Answer: 2.30 − 1.85 = **0.45 m**, or 45 cm.*
- **2d.** How much higher was the storm than the spring flood? *Answer: **0.75 m**, or 75 cm.*

**I'm stuck (2c):**

1. *Hint:* "Change both to centimetres first: 2.30 m is how many cm?"
2. *Similar example:* "1.50 m − 1.20 m: 150 cm − 120 cm = 30 cm, or 0.30 m. Now 2.30 m − 1.85 m."
3. **Skip for now.**

**Replies to likely errors:**

| Entered | Misconception | Reply |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1.55 for 2c | Taking the smaller digit from the larger in each column, instead of regrouping | "Check by adding back: does 1.85 + 1.55 make 2.30?" |
| 0.55 for 2c | Regrouping slip | "Try it in centimetres: 230 − 185." |
| 18.5 for 2b | Multiplying by 10 instead of 100 | "There are 100 centimetres in a metre, not 10." |
| 1.1 placed below 0.75 in 2a | Reading 1.1 as smaller than 0.75 because it has fewer digits | "1.10 m is more than one whole metre. 0.75 m is less than one." |

## 6. Step 3: Measured from where?

**Question panel:**

> Pip puts Léon's drawing next to Willowbank's flood post. "Our storm mark says 160 centimetres. Theirs says 185. So it was worse at Les Saules."
>
> Then Léon's postscript, which nobody had read: *P.S. Le sol du bac est à soixante centimètres au-dessus de la rivière ordinaire.* (The ferry landing floor is sixty centimetres above the ordinary river.)
>
> Hollis measured from the brook's ordinary level (spec 120, page 2).

**Learn card:**

> A height always means **height above something**. That something is the **zero**, or reference level.
>
> Two heights can be compared only if they share a zero. If they don't, change one so it does.
>
> Worked example: a shelf is 1.20 m above a table, and the table is 0.80 m above the floor. The shelf is 1.20 + 0.80 = 2.00 m above the floor.

**Guided first task (3a):** a side-view drawing of the ferry landing with the ordinary river, the landing floor and the storm mark, and the two measurements labelled.

Tasks:

- **3a.** How high was the storm at Les Saules above the **ordinary river**? *Answer: 1.85 + 0.60 = **2.45 m**, or 245 cm.*
- **3b.** And at Willowbank, above the ordinary brook? *Answer: **160 cm**, as the post says.*
- **3c.** "So was the storm worse at Les Saules?" Choices:
  - "It rose higher above ordinary there, but a big river isn't a small brook. Higher doesn't mean worse." ← fits the records
  - "Yes, 185 is more than 160."
  - "No, the marks are measured differently, so they tell us nothing."

  Reply to the second: "185 is from the landing floor. 160 is from the brook. What zero does each use?" Reply to the third: "Different zeros mean you have to convert first, not that the marks are useless. Try 3a."
- **3d.** "If Willowbank and Les Saules wanted to compare every flood from now on, what should they agree?" Free choice: measure from the ordinary water level; use the same units; write the date the same way; note where the mark is. Each has a reply; none is marked wrong.

**I'm stuck (3a):**

1. *Hint:* "The storm mark is 1.85 m above the floor. The floor is 0.60 m above the river. Stack them."
2. *Similar example:* the learn card's shelf, table and floor.
3. **Skip for now.**

**Replies to likely errors:**

| Entered | Misconception | Reply |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1.25 for 3a | Subtracting the floor height | "The floor is *above* the river, so the mark is even further above the river. Add." |
| 1.85 for 3a | Ignoring the change of zero | "That's above the floor. The question asks above the river." |
| 85 for 3b | Mixing the two towns' figures | "160 cm is Willowbank's storm mark, already measured from the brook." |

## 7. Step 4: Marks across France (History)

**Label on every panel in this step: History: this happened. Sources are listed in section 10.**

**Reading panel, short**

> **English:** In France, many towns mark the height of great floods on walls and bridges. In 2003, a French law said towns in flood areas must find their old flood marks, put up new ones after great floods, and keep them where people can see them. The government keeps a list of these marks that anyone can look up.
>
> **Français :** En France, beaucoup de villes marquent la hauteur des grandes crues sur les murs et les ponts. En 2003, une loi a demandé aux villes exposées aux inondations de recenser leurs anciens repères de crues, d'en poser de nouveaux après les grandes crues, et de les garder visibles. L'État tient une liste de ces repères, que tout le monde peut consulter.

**Reading panel, longer** (English and French) adds the Toulouse example:

> **English:** One of the most famous floods in France came down the river Garonne, which rises in the Pyrenees mountains in Spain and flows through Toulouse. On 23 June 1875, after days of heavy rain and melting snow, the Garonne rose higher in Toulouse than at any time since. The district of Saint-Cyprien, on the river's left bank, was flooded, and many houses fell. More than two hundred people died. Marks of the 1875 flood can still be found on walls in Toulouse today.
>
> **Français :** Une des crues les plus célèbres de France est celle de la Garonne, qui prend sa source dans les Pyrénées, en Espagne, et traverse Toulouse. Le 23 juin 1875, après des jours de fortes pluies et de fonte des neiges, la Garonne est montée à Toulouse plus haut que jamais depuis. Le quartier de Saint-Cyprien, sur la rive gauche, a été inondé, et beaucoup de maisons se sont effondrées. Plus de deux cents personnes sont mortes. On trouve encore aujourd'hui des repères de la crue de 1875 sur des murs de Toulouse.

**Glossary additions:** *la loi* (law); *recenser* (to list, to count up); *poser* (to put up); *visible* (visible); *consulter* (to look up); *prendre sa source* (to rise, of a river); *la rive gauche* (left bank); *s'effondrer* (to fall down, collapse); *les plus hautes eaux connues* (the highest known water, a phrase used on some markers).

**Care with the hard part.** Child text names the deaths once, plainly, in the longer version only. No images of the dead or of grief. A grown-up note says the case is about how towns remember floods, and that French towns still flood today.

Tasks:

- **4a.** "Why would a law ask towns to keep flood marks visible?" Choices:
  - "So people remember how high water has come, even generations later." ← fits the reading
  - "So the walls look historic for visitors."
  - "So the river knows where to stop."

  Reply to the third: "A nice thought, but rivers don't read. Who are the marks for?"
- **4b.** "Which of Hollis's pages is most like a flood mark?" Choices: page 2, the flood ledger; page 1, the old map; page 3, the letter about the Ways. Page 2 fits; replies explain the likeness and one difference (a ledger can be lost in a parcel; a wall stays put).
- **4c.** "The Garonne rises in Spain and floods in France. What does that have in common with Willow Brook?" Free choice with replies: the water crosses a border; a warning has to cross it too; towns in two countries share one river.
- **4d.** "Léon's grandfather said *pour que personne n'oublie*. Why might people forget a flood?" Free choice with replies: the people who saw it grow old; new people move in; nobody wants to remember something sad; the water goes away and everything looks normal.
