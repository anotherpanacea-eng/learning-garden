## Arriving

Hook (A story from Willowbank): After the storm, everyone in the valley is hungry. But not everyone wants berries. Who eats what, and where does it all begin?

Doors:
- **Who Eats What?**: Sort the animals by their food.
- **Who Changed This Place?**: Find who built, dug or grew it.
- **Built to Survive**: What does each part do?
- **Trace It Back**: Follow the food to the Sun.
- **The Osprey Ledger** (Keeper's Desk): Why did a poison pile up?

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. Not everyone wants berries (Act I, chapter 1: find and feed)

**Short:** Pip is sharing out berries. "Everyone likes berries," he says. The tiny tracks lead to someone who does not.

**Longer:** The café cupboard was nearly empty, so Pip went round the garden sharing out berries from the hedge.

"Everyone likes berries," he said.

The rabbit by the gate twitched her nose and went back to the clover. A shrew under the wet leaves ignored the berries and dug out a beetle instead.

"Rude," said Pip.

"Not rude," said {guide}. "Different. The rabbit eats plants. The shrew eats small animals. And foxes eat both."

Dot landed on the gatepost. "Real ladybugs eat aphids," she said. "I prefer toast."

"So who wants what?" asked Pip. "I want everyone to get the right supper."

"Let's sort them out," said {guide}. "By what each one really eats."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** Scientists sort animals by their diet, which means the food they usually eat. A real red fox eats berries, beetles, worms and mice, so it is an omnivore.

### Scene 2. The magpie's hat (Act II, chapter 2: a garden across the seasons)

**Short:** Dot's new record book lists the garden's changes. A stone has lifted. There are worm piles on the path. And Bramble's magpie is building in her hat.

**Longer:** Now that Willowbank kept records, Dot wrote down every change in the garden. "One path stone, lifted," she read out. "Little curly piles of earth on the lawn. And a nest."

"Where?" asked Pip.

Dot pointed at Bramble's hat, which was on a fencepost. The magpie was weaving a twig into the brim.

"People change places," said Bramble. "Animals just live in them."

"Then who lifted the stone?" asked {guide}. "Who made the curly piles?"

They lifted the stone together. Underneath was a thick tree root, pushing out toward the damp ground. The curly piles were worm casts.

"So everyone's changing the garden," said Dot. "For their own reasons."

Bramble looked at her hat. "My magpie's reason had better be good."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** Worms pull dead leaves underground and leave soil in little piles called casts. Their tunnels let air and water into the soil.

### Scene 3. Wet feet (Act III, chapter 2: the seed exchange)

**Short:** Nuria brings reeds from Vadoalto. "They love wet ground," she says. The orchard keeper asks how anything can live with its roots in mud.

**Longer:** The boat from Vadoalto came in low in the water, full of bundles of tall green reeds.

"These grow where it's always wet," said Nuria. "Our whole riverbank is full of them."

The orchard keeper frowned. "My apple trees sulk if their roots stay wet. Roots need air. How does a reed manage?"

Nuria took out a small knife and cut a reed stem across. Inside were rows of tiny open spaces, like a honeycomb.

"Air goes down through these," she said. "All the way to the roots in the mud."

"So the reed has a sort of snorkel," said Pip.

"A snorkel inside its stem," said {guide}. "You can't see it from outside. But it's how the reed stays alive in wet ground."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** Reeds have air spaces running through their stems and underground stems. Air can move through them down to roots in wet mud, where there is little air in the soil.

### Scene 4. The pond that wasn't there (Act III, chapter 3: the route by the high ford)

**Short:** Near the ford, a meadow has turned into a new pond. At one end is a wall of sticks and mud. Someone built this.

**Longer:** Near the high ford, beside the path that the ribbons marked, the meadow was full of water. A pond stretched across it, with dead grass standing up through it.

"That's not on the map," said {courier}.

At the far end was a long, low wall of sticks, mud and stones. A brown head slid under the water and was gone.

"A beaver," said {guide}. "It built a dam across the stream. The water backed up and made this pond."

"Why would anyone want a pond?" asked Pip, who did not like wet paws.

"Deep water keeps a beaver safe," said {guide}. "Its home has a door under the water, where hunters can't follow."

{courier} looked at the huge chewed stumps by the bank. "With teeth like that, I bet it eats fish."

"Bark, leaves and water plants," said {guide}. "No fish at all."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** Beavers eat only plants: bark, twigs, leaves and water plants. Their big front teeth keep growing all their lives, and gnawing wood wears them down.

### Scene 5. Who is awake? (Act V, path 1: a night garden)

**Short:** In the dark garden, an owl calls. "The owl's supper has nothing to do with the Sun," says Pip sleepily. "It's night." Is he right?

**Longer:** The night garden plan had a blanket in it, and Pip was already under it.

A soft call came from the old oak: *hoo, hoo-hoo.* Something small rustled under the hedge, then went very still.

"An owl," whispered the orchard keeper. "Hunting mice."

"At least the owl doesn't need the Sun," mumbled Pip. "It's night. No Sun anywhere."

"Let's follow it back," said {guide}. "The owl eats a mouse. What did the mouse eat?"

"Seeds," said the orchard keeper. "From the beech and the hedge."

"And how did the seeds grow?"

Pip opened one eye. "In the sunshine. Oh."

"So the owl's energy came from the Sun," said {guide}. "Just by a long way round."

But Pip was asleep again.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** A real tawny owl hunts mostly at night and eats mostly small animals such as mice and voles. Every link in its food chain still goes back to plants, and plants to sunlight.

### Desk scene. A pond far away (Act IV, chapter 3: what other places did, Keeper's Desk)

**Short:** The librarian opens a real folder: ospreys, a salt marsh and a poison that would not go away.

**Longer:** "The Gazette's argument is about water," said the librarian, "but the archive holds another kind of story too. A real place where people changed a food chain without meaning to, and then found out."

They opened a folder of real photographs: a salt marsh, a fish hawk called an osprey, and a pale, thin eggshell.

"People sprayed a poison called DDT to kill insects," said the librarian. "It did not break down. It went into tiny water life, then fish, then birds."

"And the birds?" asked Pip quietly.

"Their eggshells grew thin. Fewer chicks hatched. Then scientists showed what was happening, and people stopped using it there. The ospreys came back."

"So someone kept a record," said Dot.

"Several someones," said the librarian.

## Teach cards

Each worked example reveals one step per tap.

**T1. What animals need** (A true thing about science). Animals need food, water, air and a safe place. Animals can't make their own food. They eat plants or other animals.
Worked example: (1) A rabbit is hungry. (2) It can't make food. (3) It eats clover.

**T2. Three food groups.** A **herbivore** eats plants. A **carnivore** eats animals. An **omnivore** eats plants and animals.
Worked example: (1) Rabbit: clover. Herbivore. (2) Owl: mice. Carnivore. (3) Fox: berries and mice. Omnivore.

**T3. Food decides, not looks.** Big and fierce doesn't mean meat-eater. Tiny doesn't mean plant-eater. Look at what it eats.
Worked example: (1) An elephant is huge. (2) It eats leaves and bark. (3) Herbivore.

**T4. Teeth are a clue** (Seedling). Wide flat back teeth grind plants. Sharp pointed teeth hold and cut meat. Teeth are a clue. Food is the rule.
Worked example: (1) A cow has wide flat teeth. (2) A clue: plants? (3) It eats grass. Herbivore.

**T5. Living things change places.** Animals and plants change where they live. People do too. A beaver builds a dam. Roots push stones.
Worked example: (1) Before: a stream. (2) The beaver builds a dam. (3) After: a pond.

**T6. Every change meets a need.** Living things change places to get food, water, shelter or safety. Say who changed it, and why.
Worked example: (1) The bird built a nest. (2) Why? (3) Shelter for its eggs.

**T7. Parts that help** (Sapling). A **structure** is a part of a living thing. Its **function** is the job it does. Some jobs help it survive or grow.
Worked example: (1) Structure: hedgehog spines. (2) Function: they prick. (3) Help: hungry animals leave it alone.

**T8. Inside parts count too.** Some structures are outside, like feathers. Some are inside, like a heart or a reed's air spaces.
Worked example: (1) A hare's heart is inside. (2) It pumps blood. (3) Blood carries food and oxygen everywhere.

**T9. Claim, evidence, reasoning.** A **claim** says what you think. **Evidence** is what you saw. **Reasoning** joins them. The owl didn't choose its feathers. They just work.
Worked example: (1) Claim: soft feathers help the owl hunt. (2) Evidence: its flight is nearly silent. (3) Reasoning: mice can't hear it coming.

**T10. Plants catch sunlight.** Plants use sunlight to make their own food. That food holds energy from the Sun. Soil gives water and minerals, not energy.
Worked example: (1) Sunlight falls on clover leaves. (2) The clover makes food. (3) The food stores the Sun's energy.

**T11. Follow the food back.** Ask, "Where did its energy come from?" Keep asking. Every chain here ends at plants, and plants at the Sun.
Worked example: (1) Fox: from a rabbit. (2) Rabbit: from clover. (3) Clover: from sunlight.

**T12. Energy goes one way.** An animal uses food energy to move, grow, mend and keep warm. Most leaves as warmth. It never goes round again.
Worked example: (1) A fox runs. (2) Its body warms. (3) The warmth spreads into the air. It doesn't come back.

**T13. Some poisons stay** (Keeper's Desk). Some poisons break down quickly. Some don't. A **persistent** poison stays in an animal's body, meal after meal.
Worked example: (1) A small fish eats poisoned plankton. (2) The poison stays. (3) Next meal adds more.

**T14. Packed tighter up the chain.** Each step up, only a little eaten food builds new bodies. The rest leaves as waste and breathed-out gas. A persistent poison is all kept.
Worked example: (1) Plankton: 1,000 units of living stuff, 1,000 of poison. (2) Small fish: only 100 units of living stuff, all 1,000 of poison. (3) Poison in each unit: 1 in plankton, 10 in small fish.

**T15. Energy down, poison up.** Energy gets smaller up a food chain. A persistent poison gets more concentrated. The top hunter's body holds it most densely.
Worked example (energy in the whole level; poison in each unit of living stuff): (1) Plankton: 1,000 energy, 1 poison. (2) Small fish: 100 and 10. (3) Big fish: 10 and 100. (4) Osprey: 1 and 1,000.

Real-figures card (A true thing from history): In 1967, scientists measured DDT in a salt marsh on Long Island, New York. Plankton held about 0.04 parts per million. One gull held 75 parts per million.

## History cards

**The Book of Animals (A true thing from history).** About 1,200 years ago, al-Jahiz lived in Basra, in today's Iraq. His Book of Animals said mosquitoes seek blood, flies hunt mosquitoes, and other animals hunt flies. Some historians call it an early food chain.

**Darwin's worms (A true thing from history).** In 1881, Charles Darwin wrote a book about earthworms. In his English garden, he watched them for years. Worms, he showed, slowly mix the soil and bury stones.

**Elton's food chains (A true thing from history).** In 1927, Charles Elton wrote Animal Ecology. He used "food chains" to show who eats whom. He saw there are many small animals at the bottom and few big hunters at the top.

**Rachel Carson (A true thing from history).** In 1962, Rachel Carson, an American scientist, wrote Silent Spring. She warned that some sprays harm wildlife far from where they are used. In 1972, the United States stopped most uses of DDT.

**Keeper's note: Ratcliffe's eggshells (A true thing from history).** In 1967, Derek Ratcliffe, a British scientist, weighed falcon, hawk and eagle eggshells kept in collections. Shells laid since the late 1940s were lighter and thinner. Later work tied the change to DDT.

- C. Hedgehogs have spines. Hedgehogs are brown. *(misconception: a fact with no link to survival)*
Hint: Which one says what the spines do?
Feedback: A: Hedgehogs didn't choose spines. What do spines do? B: Yes. It joins the part, its job and survival. C: True facts, but nothing joins them to survival.
Explanation: A strong argument says what the part does and how that helps.

**3.4 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Reed roots sit in wet mud with little air. What helps?
- A. Air spaces inside the stem carry air down. *(right)*
- B. Bright petals bring bees. *(misconception: mixes up a part's function)*
- C. The reed chose to grow tubes. *(misconception: living things choose their parts)*
Hint: Think about what is inside the stem.
Feedback: A: Yes. An inside structure keeps the roots alive. B: Petals help make seeds, not breathe. C: Reeds don't choose. The air spaces just work.
Explanation: The reed's inside air spaces carry air to its roots in the mud.

**3.5 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Apple blossom has bright petals and scent. Which job do they help?
- A. Keeping hungry animals away *(misconception: every part is for defense)*
- B. Making seeds, by bringing bees *(right)*
- C. Taking in water *(misconception: mixes up a part's function)*
Hint: Who visits blossom in spring?
Feedback: A: Petals don't scare animals. They invite bees. B: Yes. Bees carry pollen, and seeds can form. C: Roots take in water. Petals do another job.
Explanation: Not every part is for defense. Petals help the tree make seeds.

**3.6 (Sapling, build an argument)**
Prompt: Build an argument about the heron's long legs.
Claim tiles: Long legs help the heron catch food. · The heron is tall and gray. · The heron grew long legs to reach fish.
Evidence tiles: It stands in the shallows and catches fish. · It sleeps standing up. · It flies with its neck tucked in.
Reasoning tiles: Long legs let it stand in deeper water, where it finds more fish. · Long legs look nice. · Fish like long legs.
Answer: claim 1, evidence 1, reasoning 1.
Misconception tested: wanting or trying makes a structure.
Hint: Which reason joins the legs to the fish?
Explanation: Claim, evidence and reasoning must join up: legs, wading, fish.

### Set 4. Back to the Sun (Sapling 4.1-4.7)

**4.1 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: A fox ate a rabbit. Where did the rabbit's energy come from?
- A. Clover it ate, which grew in sunlight *(right)*
- B. The rabbit made its own *(misconception: animals make their own energy)*
- C. The Sun warming its fur *(misconception: sunlight feeds animals directly)*
Hint: What did the rabbit eat?
Feedback: A: Yes. Clover passed on the Sun's energy. B: Animals can't make food. They eat it. C: Sun warms fur, but food energy comes from eating.
Explanation: The rabbit's energy came from clover, and the clover's from sunlight.

**4.2 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Where does clover get its energy?
- A. From the soil *(misconception: plants get energy from soil)*
- B. From sunlight, caught by its leaves *(right)*
- C. From rainwater *(misconception: water gives plants energy)*
Hint: What do plants use to make their own food?
Feedback: A: Soil gives water and minerals, not energy. B: Yes. Leaves use sunlight to make food. C: Plants need water, but it isn't their energy.
Explanation: Plants make their food using sunlight. That's where the energy starts.

**4.3 (Sapling, order with arrows)**
Prompt: Build the energy chain. Label sunlight and feeding links.
Cards: fox · Sun · rabbit · clover
Answer: Sun ⇢ clover → rabbit → fox.
Misconception tested: arrows drawn from eater to food.
Hint: Start with the Sun. Who uses sunlight first?
Explanation: Sun ⇢ clover carries sunlight energy. Clover → rabbit and rabbit → fox carry food energy from food to eater.

**4.4 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: An owl hunts at night. Where did its energy first come from?
- A. The Moon *(misconception: night animals run on different energy)*
- B. The Sun, through seeds and mice *(right)*
- C. The owl's own body *(misconception: animals make their own energy)*
Hint: What did the mouse eat? How did that grow?
Feedback: A: Moonlight is too dim to grow seeds. Follow the food. B: Yes. The seeds grew in sunlight. C: The owl gets energy by eating.
Explanation: Even night hunters trace back to plants, and plants to the Sun.

**4.5 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Where does most of the fox's food energy end up?
- A. Stored forever in the fox *(misconception: energy stays put)*
- B. Back in the soil, ready for new clover *(misconception: energy goes round like matter)*
- C. Used to move and keep warm, then out as warmth *(right)*
Hint: What does a fox do all day and night?
Feedback: A: The fox uses it up and needs more food. B: Matter goes round. Energy doesn't. C: Yes. Energy flows one way.
Explanation: Energy is used for moving, growing and warmth, and leaves as warmth.

**4.6 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Trout eat mayfly young. Mayfly young eat algae. Trace the trout's energy back.
- A. Algae, and then sunlight *(right)*
- B. The river water *(misconception: water gives energy)*
- C. It stops at the mayfly *(misconception: energy comes from the food, full stop)*
Hint: Ask, "Where did its energy come from?" Twice.
Feedback: A: Yes. Algae use sunlight, like plants. B: Water carries the trout. It isn't food energy. C: Keep going. What did the mayfly eat?
Explanation: Water chains work the same way: algae catch sunlight, and it passes up.

**4.7 (Sapling, number with steps)**
Given: a field's clover stores 1,000 units of energy. About a tenth passes up each step.
Prompt: How many units reach all the field's foxes?
Answer: 10.
Steps: (1) Clover to all the rabbits: 1,000 ÷ 10 = 100. (2) Rabbits to all the foxes: 100 ÷ 10 = 10.
Misconception tested: all the energy passes up (answering 1,000).
Hint: Divide by 10 for each arrow.
Explanation: Most energy leaves as warmth at each step, so only 10 units reach all the foxes together. (A made-up round number; the real share varies.)

### Set 5. Up the chain (Keeper's Desk 5.1-5.7)

**5.1 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: A persistent poison gets into a pond. Where will it be most concentrated?
- A. In the water, where it entered *(misconception: highest where it enters)*
- B. Spread evenly through every animal *(misconception: it harms all levels equally)*
- C. In the osprey at the top *(right)*
Hint: Where is the poison packed into the least living stuff?
Feedback: A: The water holds very little. Animals pile it up. B: Each step up holds more, not the same. C: Yes. All the poison is packed into the least living stuff.
Explanation: A persistent poison is kept while living stuff shrinks, so each unit of the top hunter holds the most.

**5.2 (Keeper's Desk, number with steps)**
Given: ledger set to "stays", a tenth passes up. All the plankton: 1,000 units of living stuff, 1,000 of poison.
Prompt: How much poison is in each unit of big fish?
Answer: 100.
Steps: (1) Living stuff: 1,000 → 100 → 10 in all the big fish. (2) Poison kept: still 1,000. (3) 1,000 ÷ 10 = 100.
Misconception tested: reading the total as the concentration (answering 1,000), or thinking the concentration stays put because the total does (answering 1).
Hint: Find the big fish's living stuff first. Then divide.
Explanation: The same poison sits in a tenth as much living stuff each step. (Made-up model.)

**5.3 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Switch the poison to "breaks down". What does the osprey's "each unit" meter show?
- A. 0. None of it reaches the osprey. *(right)*
- B. Still 1,000 *(misconception: persistence makes no difference)*
- C. 1, the same as the plankton *(misconception: a poison that breaks down still passes up unchanged)*
Hint: A poison that breaks down can't be saved up.
Feedback: A: Yes. It broke down before anything ate it. B: Only a poison that stays can pile up. C: It breaks down before it is eaten, so none passes up.
Explanation: Here a poison that breaks down never leaves the plankton. Persistence is what lets a poison build up the chain.

**5.4 (Keeper's Desk, number with steps)**
Given: in a real 1967 study, plankton held about 0.04 ppm of DDT, and one gull up to 75 ppm.
Prompt: About how many times higher was the gull?
Answer: 1,875 (about 1,900 times).
Steps: (1) 75 ÷ 0.04. (2) Same as 7,500 ÷ 4. (3) = 1,875.
Misconception tested: subtracting instead of dividing (answering about 75).
Hint: "How many times" means divide.
Explanation: The gull held nearly two thousand times the plankton's level. Real chains don't rise by a neat ten each step.

**5.5 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Energy shrinks up the chain, but a persistent poison gets more concentrated. Why?
- A. The poison turns into energy *(misconception: mixing up poison and energy)*
- B. They're the same thing, measured twice *(misconception: mixing up poison and energy)*
- C. Energy is used and leaves as warmth; the poison is kept *(right)*
Hint: What happens to energy after a meal? And the poison?
Feedback: A: A poison is a substance, not energy. B: Energy and poison behave differently. C: Yes. One leaves, one stays.
Explanation: Most eaten energy leaves as warmth, but a persistent poison is kept, so it is packed into less living stuff.

**5.6 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which is the best evidence that DDT harmed the ospreys?
- A. Ospreys drank lots of pond water *(misconception: the poison comes mainly from drinking water)*
- B. Big birds cope best, so it can't be DDT *(misconception: top hunters are big enough to cope)*
- C. Thin shells came with DDT, and ospreys recovered after it stopped *(right)*
Hint: Look for a change that came and went with DDT.
Feedback: A: The poison came mostly through food, not water. B: The top hunter carries the most concentrated poison. C: Yes. Two pieces of evidence point the same way.
Explanation: The harm appeared with DDT and eased after it stopped. That pattern is strong evidence.

**5.7 (Keeper's Desk, say why, to a grown-up)**
Prompt: Why is poison more concentrated in ospreys than in small fish? Use the ledger.
Answer to listen for: the poison stays (persistent); only a small share of living stuff passes up each step, but all the poison is kept; so each unit of osprey holds the most; the harm itself is shown by real evidence (thin eggshells), not by the model; claim, evidence and reasoning.
Misconception tested: the poison thins out going up.
Hint: Start with "The poison stays, so..."
Explanation: A good argument names persistence, shrinking living stuff, and the top of the chain.

## "I'm stuck" worked examples (step 2 of the ladder)

- **Sorting:** A sheep eats grass and hay. Which basket? Start: are grass and hay plants?
- **Looks can fool:** A hippo is huge and eats grass. Start: forget the size, look at the plate.
- **Teeth:** Pointed teeth at the front. Start: pointed teeth hold and cut meat.
- **Changers:** Before: a bank. After: a hole with a rabbit. Start: who is standing by the hole?
- **Needs:** A squirrel buries nuts in autumn. Start: what will it need in winter?
- **Structures:** A duck has webbed feet. Start: what are its feet doing in the water?
- **Arguments:** Holly has prickly leaves. Start: what happens to a mouth that bites them?
- **Trace back:** A frog eats a fly. The fly ate nectar. Start: where did the flower get its energy?
- **Energy tenths:** A field's grass stores 100 units. Start: 100 ÷ 10 = 10 reach all its rabbits.
- **Ledger:** a fifth passes up, poison stays. Start: small fish hold 1,000 ÷ 5 = 200 units of living stuff. Then 1,000 poison ÷ 200 = 5 in each unit.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Diet:** the food an animal usually eats.
- **Aphid:** a tiny insect that sucks juice from plants.
- **Vole:** a small animal like a mouse, with a short tail.
- **Worm casts:** little piles of soil that earthworms leave behind.
- **Osprey:** a large bird, sometimes called a fish hawk, that dives for fish.
- **Herbivore:** an animal that eats plants.
- **Carnivore:** an animal that eats other animals.
- **Omnivore:** an animal that eats plants and animals.
- **Scavenger:** an animal that eats animals that have already died.
- **Need:** something a living thing must have to stay alive, like food, water, air or shelter.
- **Shelter:** a safe, covered place to live.
- **Dam:** a wall that holds back water.
- **Burrow:** a hole an animal digs to live in.
- **Structure:** a part of a living thing.
- **Function:** the job a part does.
- **Claim:** what you think is true.
- **Evidence:** what you saw or measured.
- **Reasoning:** how the evidence supports the claim.
- **Energy:** what makes things move, grow, warm up or happen.
- **Food chain:** a line of arrows showing who eats whom.
- **Producer:** a living thing that makes its own food. Here, plants and algae, using sunlight.
- **Living stuff:** the bodies of all the living things at one step of a chain. Scientists call it **biomass**.
- **Minerals:** natural materials in soil and rock. Plants take in tiny amounts with water.
- **Oxygen:** a gas in air and water that living things need.
- **Pollen:** a fine powder that flowers pass to each other so seeds can form.
- **Algae:** tiny plant-like living things in water that make food from sunlight.
- **Plankton:** tiny living things that drift in water.
- **Poison:** something that harms living things.
- **Pesticide:** a spray or powder made to kill pests, such as insects.
- **Concentrated:** packed in, so each bit holds more.
- **Persistent:** lasting a long time without breaking down.
- **Build-up up the chain:** a poison growing more concentrated at each step up a food chain. Scientists call it **biomagnification**.
- **ppm (parts per million):** how many grams of something are in a million grams.
- **DDT:** a pesticide used widely in the middle 1900s. It breaks down very slowly.

## Off-screen invitations (grown-up-led)

1. **Who changed this?** On a walk, a grown-up helps spot a change made by a living thing: a nest, a molehill, worm casts, a root lifting a path. Look, don't touch. Nothing is collected or recorded.
2. **Teeth in a book.** Look at animal skull pictures in a library book or museum. Which teeth grind? Which hold and cut?
3. **Draw a chain.** Draw a food chain from a favorite animal picture book. Add arrows from food to eater, and the Sun at the start.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** sorting wild animals as herbivores, carnivores or omnivores by what they eat (looks and teeth are only clues); how living things, people included, change where they live to meet their needs; how a plant's or animal's parts, inside and outside, help it survive, grow and make seeds or young, argued with claim, evidence and reasoning; how the energy in the food chains shown here traces back through plants to the Sun and flows one way; and, at the Keeper's Desk, how a persistent poison builds up a food chain, with DDT and fish-eating birds as the real case.

**What it does not claim:** the Osprey Ledger and the energy tenths are made-up models with stated rules, not measurements. Real food chains are tangled webs. Not every chain starts with sunlight: around deep-sea vents, bacteria make food from chemicals in the water (the Down to the Deep pack, spec 171). The ledger's meters show amounts of poison, not harm; the real evidence of harm is the thin eggshells. The pack covers wild and farm animals only. It never asks what your child eats, gives no diet advice and does not sort people. If your child asks, people are animals too, and scientists call humans omnivores; what a family eats is its own choice.

**Kindness line:** This pack is about what animals eat. It never asks what you eat.
