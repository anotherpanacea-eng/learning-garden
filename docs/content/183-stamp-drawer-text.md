# The Stamp Drawer: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 183](../specs/183-stamp-drawer.md). Use as written, after independent spec review and a math check. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion and is never gendered; `{courier}` is the explorer's courier. Pip is he/him, Dot she/her, Bramble she/her (with her magpie), Nuria she/her, Léon he/him. Real-world facts are in the [source audit](../source-audits/183-stamp-drawer.md); items still marked `[check]` stay off screen until cleared. Nothing here is scored or timed.

## Labels used on every card

- **A story from Willowbank**: the town, its stamps, letters and every character are made up.
- **A true thing about math**: checked math, explained simply.
- **A true thing from history**: something people really did or wrote.
- **A made-up model**: a simple game rule, shown on the card, that is not real measurement.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. A drawer with no stamps (Act II, chapter 1: the weekly market)

**Hook:** Parcels pile up for the market. Dot opens her stamp drawer. It is full of blank shapes, and not one stamp.

**Short:**
> The stalls are back, and so are parcels. Dot opens her stamp drawer: blank shapes, no stamps. "Willowbank needs its own," she says.

**Longer:**
> Every week more stalls come back to the green, and every stall sends parcels. Dot marks them with an inky thumb. "That's not a stamp," says Pip. "That's a thumb."
>
> Dot pulls out a long wooden drawer. Inside are blank stamp shapes: squares, triangles, a circle, one with six sides. No pictures yet.
>
> "Willowbank has never had its own stamps," she says. "Every town should. But first I need to know what I've got."
>
> "Shapes," says Pip.
>
> "Which shapes?" says Dot. "What does each one have?"
>
> {guide} picks up a triangle and turns it over. "Three sides. Three corners. No square corner."
>
> "Good," says Dot. She writes it on a card. "Fourteen to go."

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> Dot's drawer holds 15 blank shapes. Each gets a property card: sides, corners, equal sides, square corners, parallel sides and lines of symmetry. A shape's card, not its look, decides its name.

### Scene 2. The greedy square (Act II, chapter 4: the first show)

**Hook:** Dot is staying. She designs the town's first stamps. Bramble sorts them onto shelves and finds a square that won't sit still.

**Short:**
> Dot is staying, so Willowbank gets stamps. Bramble sorts them onto shelves. "The square can't go with the rectangles," she says. Pip disagrees.

**Longer:**
> After the show, Dot sits at the counter with her drawer. "If I'm staying," she says, "this town gets proper stamps."
>
> Bramble helps. She chalks labels on three shelves: RECTANGLES. SQUARES. RHOMBUSES. Her magpie steals the circle.
>
> She holds up the square stamp. "This goes on the square shelf. Obviously."
>
> "It has four square corners," says {guide}. "And opposite sides parallel. That's everything a rectangle needs."
>
> "But rectangles are long."
>
> "Long isn't on the list," says Pip, who learned this five minutes ago.
>
> Bramble tries the square on the rhombus shelf. Four equal sides. It fits there too.
>
> "Fine," she says. "It goes on both. It's a very greedy square." She rubs out the shelves and chalks a tree instead.
>
> The magpie keeps the circle.

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> A square is a special rectangle and a special rhombus. Every square is a rectangle; most rectangles are not squares. Long ago, Euclid sorted these shapes apart instead (see the history card).

### Ending card. A stamp from upstream (Act II ending, into Act III)

**Short:**
> The letter from Vadoalto has a stamp. Its shape isn't in Dot's drawer. "Tell me what it has," says Dot, "and we'll know what it is."

**Prompts by depth:** Sprout: Is it round, or does it have corners? · Seedling: Count its sides and corners. · Sapling: Are its sides equal? Are its angles equal?

### Scene 3. Riddles in the post (Act III, chapter 4: letters up and down the river)

**Hook:** Letters come from Vadoalto and Les Saules. Nuria and Léon each describe a stamp, with no picture. Which shapes are they?

**Short:**
> Nuria and Léon each describe a stamp, with no picture. Dot tips out the drawer. "Only one shape fits each," says {guide}.

**Longer** (Spanish and French lines for fluent readers' checks):
> Nuria's letter has a drawing of her flood post, and a riddle.
>
> *¡Hola desde Vadoalto! Mi sello tiene cuatro lados iguales y ningún ángulo recto.* (Hello from Vadoalto! My stamp has four equal sides and no right angle.)
>
> Léon's letter, slightly damp, has one too.
>
> *Bonjour des Saules ! Mon timbre a trois côtés et un angle droit. Aucun côté n'est égal à un autre.* (Hello from Les Saules! My stamp has three sides and a right angle. No two sides are equal.)
>
> Pip grabs the square. "Four equal sides!"
>
> "And four right angles," says {guide}. "Nuria said none."
>
> They check every stamp. Only the rhombus fits Nuria. Only the 3, 4, 5 triangle fits Léon.
>
> "Now we send ours," says Dot. "Round ones. Let's see them guess a shape with no straight sides."

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> A good property riddle fits exactly one stamp in the drawer. Leave out "no right angle" and Nuria's riddle fits the square too.

## Teach cards

Each card is under 30 words. Worked steps appear one tap at a time.

**T1. Sides and corners** (A true thing about math)
> A side is a straight stick. A corner, or vertex, is where two sides meet. Join every corner and the shape is closed.
Worked example: 1. Three sticks. 2. Three clay balls. 3. Join every end: a closed triangle.

**T2. Turned, thin or tall** (A true thing about math)
> A shape keeps its name when you turn it, stretch it thin or stand it tall. Its sides and corners decide its name.
Worked example: 1. A tall rectangle. 2. Four straight sides, four square corners. 3. Still a rectangle.

**T3. Tick marks and triangle families** (A true thing about math)
> Tick marks show equal sides. Equilateral: all three sides equal. Isosceles: at least two equal. Scalene: no two sides equal.
Worked example: 1. Ticks on two sides. 2. The third side has no tick. 3. Isosceles.

**T4. Square corners** (A true thing about math)
> A right angle is a square corner. Fit the corner card into it. If it fits exactly, it's a right angle. A right triangle has one.
Worked example: 1. Stamp s10, turned on its point. 2. The corner card fits one corner exactly. 3. A right triangle, even turned.

**T5. Parallel sides** (A true thing about math)
> Parallel sides keep the same gap all along, like train tracks. Slide the ruler to check. They can be different lengths, or tilted.
Worked example: 1. Trapezoid s5: top 2 cm, bottom 6 cm. 2. Slide the ruler: the gap stays the same. 3. Parallel.

**T6. Two questions for a triangle** (A true thing about math)
> Ask about angles: is there a right angle? Ask about sides: how many are equal? One triangle answers both questions.
Worked example: 1. Stamp s11: the corner card fits. 2. Two sides have ticks. 3. A right isosceles triangle.

**T7. The Venn drawer** (A true thing about math)
> Each loop has a property. Put a shape in every loop it fits. The middle is for shapes with both. Outside means neither.
Worked example: 1. Loops: "4 sides" and "has a right angle". 2. The square has both. 3. It goes in the middle.

**T8. The Carroll grid** (A true thing about math)
> Two yes-or-no questions make four boxes. Every shape goes in exactly one box. An empty box tells you something too.
Worked example: 1. Rhombus: parallel sides? Yes. 2. Right angle? No. 3. Box: "parallel, no right angle".

**T9. Two keys for regular** (A true thing about math)
> Regular means all sides equal and all angles equal. Both keys must turn. If either key sticks, the shape is irregular.
Worked example: 1. Square: sides key turns. 2. Angles key turns. 3. Regular. 4. Rhombus: only the sides key turns. Irregular.

**T10. The family tree** (A true thing about math)
> Trapezoid: at least one pair of parallel sides. Parallelogram: two pairs. Rectangle: plus four right angles. Rhombus: plus four equal sides. Square: both.
Worked example: 1. Stamp s4: two parallel pairs. 2. No right angles, sides not all equal. 3. A parallelogram (and a trapezoid).

**T11. Properties pass down** (A true thing about math)
> A square has everything a rectangle has, and more. So every square is a rectangle. The arrow goes one way: most rectangles aren't squares.
Worked example: 1. Drag a rectangle's sides until they're equal. 2. "Square" lights up. 3. "Rectangle" stays lit.

**T12. Diagonals** (A true thing about math)
> A diagonal joins opposite corners. In every parallelogram, diagonals cut each other in half. A rectangle's are equal. A rhombus's cross at a right angle.
Worked example: 1. The 4 by 2 rectangle. 2. Measure both diagonals. 3. Both about 4.5 cm: equal.

**T13. Parts of a circle** (A true thing about math)
> The center is the middle point. A radius goes from the center to the edge. A diameter goes edge to edge, through the center. Circumference: all the way round.
Worked example: 1. Set the compass to 3 cm. 2. Draw the circle. 3. Center to edge: a radius, 3 cm. 4. All the way round: the circumference.

**T14. Twice the radius** (A true thing about math)
> A diameter is two radii, end to end. Diameter = 2 × radius. Radius = diameter ÷ 2.
Worked example: 1. Radius 3 cm. 2. Two radii: 3 + 3 = 6. 3. Diameter 6 cm. 4. Backward: diameter 8 cm, radius 4 cm.

**T15. Drawing to order** (A true thing about math)
> Rule the base from the ruler's zero. Estimate the angle first: more or less than a square corner? Protractor center on the corner, zero along the side.
Worked example: 1. Order: a rectangle 6 cm by 4 cm. 2. Rule 6 cm. 3. Square corners at both ends; rule 4 cm up. 4. Check: both diagonals about 7.2 cm.

**T16. Side and turn** (A true thing about math)
> For a regular shape, draw a side, turn, and repeat. Turn the same amount each time. All the turns together make one whole turn.
Worked example: 1. A square: four turns. 2. 360 ÷ 4 = 90. 3. Turn 90° at each corner.

**T17. Marks and names** (A true thing about math)
> Capital letters name corners. AB is the side from A to B. Arrows mark parallel sides. A small square marks a right angle.
Worked example: 1. Rectangle ABCD. 2. Arrows on AB and DC: AB ∥ DC, parallel. 3. A small square at A: AB ⊥ AD, perpendicular.

**T18. Turn symmetry** (A true thing about math)
> Turn a shape about its center. If it fits its outline before a full turn, it has turn symmetry. Count the fits in one turn.
Worked example: 1. The 4 by 2 rectangle. 2. A half turn: it fits. 3. A full turn: it fits again. 4. Two fits: order 2.

## Real-world and history cards

**H1. The Cape triangles** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> In 1853, the Cape of Good Hope, in southern Africa, sold its first stamps. They were triangles. Postmasters cut them apart with scissors.
Keeper's note:
> There were two values, a penny and fourpence, printed in London. Nobody is sure why they were triangles; old accounts tell different stories. By 1864 the Cape used rectangles with rows of little holes, much easier to tear apart.

**H2. Euclid's list** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> About 2,300 years ago, in Alexandria, Euclid wrote a geometry book called the *Elements*. It names the circle, its center and its diameter.
Keeper's note:
> Euclid sorted shapes apart. His square has equal sides and right angles. His oblong has right angles but unequal sides. His rhombus has equal sides but no right angles. So, for Euclid, a square was never an oblong. His isosceles triangle had exactly two equal sides. Today we choose families, so properties pass down the tree.

**H3. A book for craftspeople** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> Around the year 990, in Baghdad, Abu al-Wafa wrote a book of drawing methods for craftspeople. It shows how to draw regular shapes.
Keeper's note:
> Some of his methods use a compass whose opening never changes, sometimes called a rusty compass. He thought one fixed opening led to fewer mistakes. Many of his constructions turn up again, centuries later, in European books.

### Set A. Build it, draw it (Sprout and Seedling)

**A1 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Pip joined two sticks like a V. Is it a triangle yet?
- A. Yes. It has a pointy corner already. *(tests: an open shape counts)*
- B. Not yet. Add a stick to close it. *(right)*
- C. No. A triangle needs one curved side. *(tests: curved sides)*
Hint: Run your finger round. Can you get back to the start?
Explanation: A triangle is closed: straight sides, every corner joined.
Feedback A: A triangle closes all the way round. Is this one closed?
Feedback B: Yes. Close the gap and every end is joined.
Feedback C: Triangle sides are straight. This one just has a gap.

**A2 (Sprout, 3-choice, pictures)**
Prompt: Which one is a triangle too?
- A. A long, thin triangle, lying tilted. *(right)*
- B. A three-sided shape with one bendy side. *(tests: curved sides)*
- C. Three sticks in a zigzag, ends apart. *(tests: an open shape counts)*
Hint: Look for straight sides that all join up.
Explanation: A triangle can be wide, thin or tilted. Straight sides, closed.
Feedback A: Yes. Long and thin, still straight sides, still closed.
Feedback B: One side bends. Triangle sides are straight.
Feedback C: The ends don't meet. It isn't closed.

**A3 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Dot turns the triangle stamp upside down. What is it now?
- A. Not a triangle. Triangles point up. *(tests: name tied to position)*
- B. A new shape, called an upside-down. *(tests: turning makes a new shape)*
- C. Still a triangle. Its sides didn't change. *(right)*
Hint: Touch each side before and after turning.
Explanation: Turning keeps every side and corner, so the name stays.
Feedback A: Turn it back. Did any side or corner change?
Feedback B: Turning moves it. It doesn't add or take away sides.
Feedback C: Yes. Same sides, same corners, same name.

**A4 (Seedling, number)**
Prompt: A pentagon stamp frame. How many sticks? How many clay balls?
Answer: 5 sticks and 5 balls.
Worked steps: 1. A pentagon has 5 sides. 2. One stick per side: 5. 3. One ball per corner: 5.
Tests: 6 balls (an extra ball to "close" it); 4 sticks (counting the gaps between balls in a line).
Hint: One stick for each side, one ball for each corner.
Explanation: A closed shape has as many corners as sides.

**A5 (Seedling, 3-choice)**

Feedback B: Yes. Raise four sticks, then add a top square.
Feedback C: Longer sticks make a bigger square. It's still flat.

**A6 (Seedling, draw on dot paper)**

### Set B. What does it have? (Seedling and Sapling)

**B5 (Sapling, 3-choice)**

**B6 (Sapling, property card)**

### Set E. Circles and drawing to order (Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**E1 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: A round stamp has a radius of 4.5 cm. What is its diameter?
Answer: 9 cm.

Tests: 2.25 (halving instead of doubling); 4.5 (thinking radius and diameter are equal).
Hint: Does a diameter cross the whole circle, or half of it?
Explanation: Diameter = 2 × radius.

**E2 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: The diameter is 10 cm. What is the radius?

Worked steps: 1. The radius is center to edge: half a diameter. 2. 10 ÷ 2 = 5. 3. 5 cm.
Tests: 20 (doubling when it should halve); 10 (thinking they're equal).
Hint: Is the radius longer or shorter than the diameter?
Explanation: Radius = diameter ÷ 2.

**E3 (Sapling, 3-choice)**

## Key terms (glossary)

- **side**: a straight edge of a flat shape.
- **vertex (corner)**: where two sides meet. More than one: vertices.
- **closed**: every corner is joined, with no gaps.
- **right angle**: a square corner, 90°.
- **parallel**: always the same distance apart; parallel lines never meet.
- **perpendicular**: meeting at a right angle.
- **line of symmetry**: a fold line where both halves match exactly.
- **equilateral triangle**: all three sides equal.
- **isosceles triangle**: at least two sides equal.
- **scalene triangle**: no two sides equal.
- **right triangle**: a triangle with one right angle.
- **regular polygon**: all sides equal and all angles equal.
- **irregular polygon**: a polygon that is not regular.
- **trapezoid**: four sides, at least one pair of parallel sides.
- **parallelogram**: four sides, two pairs of parallel sides.
- **rectangle**: a parallelogram with four right angles.
- **rhombus**: a parallelogram with four equal sides.
- **square**: a rectangle and a rhombus at once.
- **diagonal**: a line joining two opposite corners.
- **Venn diagram**: loops for sorting; the overlap holds things with both properties.
- **Carroll diagram**: a grid for sorting by two yes-or-no questions.
- **center**: the middle point of a circle.
- **radius**: a line from the center to the edge.
- **diameter**: a line from edge to edge through the center; two radii long.
- **chord**: a line from edge to edge that misses the center.
- **circumference**: the distance all the way round a circle.
- **AB**: the side or line from point A to point B.
- **∥ and ⊥**: short for "is parallel to" and "is perpendicular to".
- **turn symmetry (rotational symmetry)**: a shape fits its outline before a full turn.
- **order of turn symmetry**: how many times a shape fits its outline in one full turn.

## Off-screen invitation (optional, grown-up-led)

**Straws and dough** (Sprout and Seedling): build a triangle, a square and a tall thin rectangle from straws and small balls of play dough. Then try a cube frame. Count before you build. *Grown-up note: use soft dough and bendy straws; keep small balls away from children who still mouth things.*

**Fold a circle** (Sapling): trace round a plate on paper and cut it out. Fold it in half, then in half again. Where the folds cross is the center. Each fold is a diameter. *Grown-up note: an adult can do the cutting.*

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** building and drawing closed shapes from their parts; naming shapes by properties in any orientation; checking equal sides, right angles, parallel sides and lines of symmetry; triangle families; Venn and Carroll sorting; regular and irregular polygons; the quadrilateral family tree, including diagonals; the parts of a circle and diameter = 2 × radius; drawing shapes to order with a ruler and protractor; geometry marks and letter names (AB, ∥, ⊥); turn symmetry and its order.

**What it does not claim:** the stamps are perfect drawn shapes, and the tools snap to whole millimeters and degrees. It does not measure circumference or use pi (that is the waterwheel pack), add up angles inside shapes (the footbridge truss pack), or teach kites. A screen rehearses the reasoning; real straws, rulers and protractors teach the handling.

**Sources in brief:** the Cape of Good Hope's 1853 triangular stamps via postal-history reference pages; Euclid's definitions via Heath's translation and David Joyce's online *Elements*; Abu al-Wafa's book for craftspeople via the St Andrews MacTutor archive. Full list in the source audit.
