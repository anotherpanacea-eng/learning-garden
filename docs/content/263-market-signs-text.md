# Market Signs: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 263](../specs/263-market-signs.md). Use as written, after review. `{guide}` is never gendered. Pronouns: Pip, the baker he/him; Dot, the miller, Bramble and the Vadoalto cheese trader she/her. Sources: [audit](../source-audits/263-market-signs.md). Nothing is scored, timed or locked.

**Read-aloud rule:** at Sprout and Seedling a grown-up or the device reads every prompt, hook and card aloud. Spelling choices are shown, not read aloud, because they sound alike. No sound is taught on screen; the grown-up panel explains the sounds.

**Root tags:** where an item needs a root word, it appears on a little tag beside the prompt, never inside the prompt.

**Labels used throughout:**
- **A story from Willowbank** marks the town, its people, the signs and every item sentence (fiction).
- **A true thing about language** marks real facts about English spelling and words.
- **A made-up model** marks the Stall Dictionary, a tiny dictionary written for this market.

## Arriving

**Place title:** Market Signs

**First visit** (A story from Willowbank):
> The stalls are back on the green, and every stall wants a sign. A paint pot, a ladder, and a small fat dictionary wait on the sign bench.
>
> "Paint is easy," says Dot. "Spelling is the hard part. That's what the dictionary is for."

**What you already know (recall card, links to `#words`):** In the Word Garden, un- and re- made new words: unlock, rebuild. Here they come back, with more parts and more signs.

**Every bench screen (always shown):** Spelling rules usually work. A dictionary has the last word.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. Worm bread (Act II, the weekly market)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** The stalls are coming back. Pip offered to paint the signs. He finished every one before breakfast.

**Short:**
> Pip's signs say HUNNY, WOLK THIS WAY, PAY HEAR and WORM BREAD. Dot checks the dictionary. They become honey, walk, here and warm.

**Longer:**
> The baker reads his new sign. "Worm bread?"
>
> "Warm," says Pip. "Warm bread."
>
> "It says worm," says the baker. "Nobody wants worm bread."
>
> Dot walks along the row with the dictionary. *HUNNY. WOLK THIS WAY. PAY HEAR.*
>
> "Honey has an o, like Monday and money," she says. "Walk is spelled like talk. And here means this place. Hear means listening."
>
> "And warm?"
>
> "Warm has ar. Worm has or. Both are real words. Only one belongs on bread."
>
> Pip fetches the paint. "How did you know all that?"
>
> "I didn't," says Dot. "I looked them up."
>
> The baker props up the fixed sign: WARM BREAD. He keeps the worm one for the compost heap.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> English often spells one part of a word in more than one way. Writers learn words in families, like walk, talk and chalk, or Monday, money and honey. When they are unsure, they check a dictionary.

### Scene 2. Rapped in paper (Act II, the weekly market, at the bakery stall)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** The baker wants a second board, saying how his bread is sold. Pip paints it fast. Dot brings the dictionary again.

**Short:**
> Pip paints FRESHLEY BAKED and RAPPED IN PAPER. Fresh just adds -ly. And the baker wrapped his bread. That word starts with wr.

**Longer:**
> The baker wants a second board, to say how his bread is sold. Pip paints it in big letters.
>
> *FRESHLEY BAKED. RAPPED IN PAPER.*
>
> "Fresh, freshly," says {guide}. "Usually you just add -ly. No e."
>
> Pip fixes it. Then the baker frowns at the second line. "I didn't rap it. I don't knock on my bread. I wrapped it."
>
> "Same word," says Pip.
>
> "Different word," says the baker. "Look it up."
>
> Pip opens the dictionary at ra. *Rap: to knock quickly and sharply.* "That's not it."
>
> "Try wr," says Dot. "Like write."
>
> Pip turns to the w pages. *Wrap: to cover something with paper or cloth.*
>
> "It was hiding under w the whole time," he says.
>
> The board goes up: FRESHLY BAKED. WRAPPED IN PAPER. The baker hands Pip a roll. It is warm, not worm.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> Wrap, write, wren and wrong all start with wr. Knock, knot and knife start with kn. If a word isn't where you first look, try another way to spell its start, then look again.

### Scene 3. The unnamed pumpkin (Summer, Market Fair)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** Bramble has the biggest pumpkin at the fair. Guess its weight, and you get to name it. Her magpie guards the sign.

**Short:**
> Pip says UNNAMED has too many n's. Bramble says un plus named keeps both. The dictionary agrees with Bramble.

**Longer:**
> Bramble's pumpkin sits on a hay bale, round, orange and very large. Her sign reads:
>
> *GUESS ITS WEIGHT. NAME THE UNNAMED PUMPKIN.*
>
> "Unnamed has too many n's," says Pip. "Take one out."
>
> "Un, plus named," says Bramble. "Nothing falls off either part. So both n's stay."
>
> The magpie pecks at the second n, as if it agrees with Pip.
>
> "Let's check," says {guide}. Dot finds u, then un, then a long row of un- words. *Unnamed.* Two n's.
>
> "Told you," says Bramble.
>
> Pip thinks hard. "So misspell is mis plus spell? Two s's?"
>
> "Two s's," says Dot.
>
> "Then I've been misspelling misspell," says Pip.
>
> Bramble writes his guess on a card: *EIGHT POUNDS.* "Weight has eigh, like eight," she says. "And your guess is low."
>
> The pumpkin weighs forty-one pounds. Nobody guesses it. It stays unnamed for one more week.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> Most prefixes join a word without changing a letter in either part. When the prefix ends with the letter the word begins with, you get a double letter: unnamed, misspell, dissatisfied.

### Scene 4. Famoso, famous (Act III, the floating market)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** Boats from Vadoalto tie up below Willow Bridge. A cheese trader chalks her sign in Spanish and in English. She wants help with the English.

**Short:**
> The trader's English line says FAMOSO CHEESE. In English it's famous, ending -ous. Spanish información is English information, ending -tion.

**Longer:**
> The boats from Vadoalto tie up below Willow Bridge. A trader sets out wheels of cheese and chalks her sign.
>
> *QUESO FAMOSO*
> *FAMOSO CHEESE*
>
> "¿Está bien mi inglés?" she asks. *Is my English right?*
>
> "Almost," says Dot. "In English it's famous. F, a, m, o, u, s."
>
> "Famoso, famous," says the trader. She rubs out -oso and chalks -ous.
>
> Pip points to the next boat. Its board says *INFORMACIÓN*. Underneath, someone has chalked *INFORMACION*.
>
> "That one's the English, without the little mark," says Pip.
>
> "Not quite," says the trader. "English writes information. T, i, o, n."
>
> Dot checks the dictionary to be sure. *Information.* The trader nods.
>
> She sells Pip a piece of famous cheese. It is, he says, very famous indeed.

**Spanish lines, each beside its English (flagged for a fluent reader):** *QUESO FAMOSO* · FAMOUS CHEESE. *¿Está bien mi inglés?* · Is my English right? *INFORMACIÓN* · INFORMATION.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> Spanish información means information, and famoso means famous. Each language spells its own endings. English writes these two with -tion and -ous.

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer; worked-example steps appear one tap at a time. Every card is **A true thing about language**; the sign examples are **A story from Willowbank**.

**Card 1. A before l**
> Ball, small, walk and talk all have a before l. If a word is in this family, spell it with a.

Worked example: *SM_LL APPLES*
1. Small is in the ball family.
2. The family has a before l.
3. Small apples.

**Card 2. The Monday family, and -ey**
> Monday, money, honey and nothing are spelled with o. Key, valley and donkey end in -ey. Honey and money belong to both.

Worked example: *The h____ stall*
1. Honey is in the Monday family: o.
2. It ends like key: -ey.
3. h, o, n, e, y.

**Card 3. After w**
> After w, look out. Watch and want have a. Warm and war have ar. Word and work have or.

Worked example: *W__M BREAD*
1. The baker means warm.
2. Warm is in the war family: ar.
3. Warm, not worm.

**Card 4. A -tion chunk**
> Station, section, motion and fiction all end in -tion. Many words do. Learn those four letters as one chunk.

Worked example: *APPLES IN SEC____ B*
1. The word is section.
2. It ends with the -tion chunk.
3. Section.

**Card 5. Two words, two spellings**
> Here and hear are different words. Here means this place. Hear means to listen. Read the whole sign, then choose.

Worked example: *PAY H___*
1. The sign tells you where to pay.
2. Where means a place.
3. Here.

**Card 6. A prefix goes at the front**
> A prefix goes at the start of a word and changes its meaning. Re- often means again: reopen. Pre- usually means before: preheat.

Worked example: *The stalls ____open after the storm.*
1. They were open before the storm.
2. Now they open again.
3. Re- means again: reopen.

**Card 7. Not, and wrongly**
> Un- and dis- usually mean not: unsold, dislike. Mis- usually means wrongly: misprint, mislabel.

Worked example: *A jar with the wrong label*
1. The label is wrong.
2. Mis- means wrongly.
3. The jar is mislabeled.

**Card 8. Against, self, above**
> Anti- usually means against: antifreeze works against freezing. Auto- usually means self: an autograph is your own name, written by you. Super- often means above or more: superstar.

Worked example: *Bramble's star act*
1. A star is famous.
2. A superstar is more famous still.
3. Super- means more.

**Card 9. Nothing falls off**
> Adding a prefix usually changes no letters. If the prefix ends with the letter the word starts with, keep both: misspell, unnamed.

Worked example: *mis + spell*
1. Write the prefix: mis.
2. Write the word with no change: spell.
3. Misspell, with two s's.

**Card 10. -ly tells how**
> -ly turns a describing word into a word that tells how or how often: fresh, freshly; neat, neatly. It starts with a consonant, so usually just add it.

Worked example: *usual + -ly*
1. Usual ends in l.
2. -ly starts with l. Nothing changes.
3. Usually, with two l's.

**Card 11. When -ly changes the word**
> A word ending in a consonant and y usually changes y to i: happy, happily. A word ending in a consonant and le usually swaps le for ly: gentle, gently.

Worked example: *simple + -ly*
1. Simple ends in p, then le.
2. Swap le for ly.
3. Simply.

**Card 12. -ous**
> -ous makes a describing word: danger, dangerous. A final e usually drops: fame, famous. Y after a consonant usually becomes i: glory, glorious. Some have no root word.

Worked example: *glory + -ous*
1. Glory ends in r, then y.
2. Change y to i.
3. Glorious.

**Card 13. -ation**
> -ation turns a doing word into a naming word: inform, information. It starts with a vowel letter, so a final e usually drops first: invite, invitation.

Worked example: *prepare + -ation*
1. Prepare ends in r, then e.
2. -ation starts with a vowel letter. Drop the e.
3. Preparation.

**Card 14. Spellings to learn**
> Some spellings are just learned. Gym and myth have y. Young and touch have ou. Treasure and measure end -sure. Picture and nature end -ture.

Worked example: *BURIED TREA____*
1. Treasure is in the measure family.
2. That family ends -sure.
3. Treasure.

**Card 15. Word lists in families**
> A long word list is easier in families. Eight, weight and height share eigh. Enough, though and through share ough. Early, earth and learn share ear.

Worked example: *SOLD BY W_____*
1. Weight is in the eight family.
2. The family has eigh.
3. w, eigh, t: weight.

**Card 16. Find the tricky part**
> Find the part of a word you might get wrong, then find a helper. Separate has a rat in the middle. Business starts with bus.

Worked example: *different*
1. The tricky part is the double f.
2. Helper: three parts, dif, fer, ent.
3. Different.

**Card 17. ABC order**
> A dictionary lists words in ABC order. If the first letters match, look at the next letter: fig before flour; wall before warm.

Worked example: *flour or fig?*
1. Both start with f.
2. Next letters: l and i.
3. i comes first: fig, then flour.

**Card 18. Guide words**
> The two words at the top of a dictionary page are guide words: the first and last words on it. Your word fits between them, in ABC order.

Worked example: *Is honey on the page heard to honey?*
1. Honey starts with h, like both guide words.
2. Honey is the last guide word.
3. Yes, it is the last word on the page.

**Card 19. Check a spelling**
> Not sure how to spell a word? Find its first two or three letters. Then read down the page until you find it.

Worked example: *Pip wrote freind.*
1. Find f, then fr.
2. Read down: fresh, friend, fruit.
3. The page says friend.

**Card 20. Try another start**
> Can't find a word? Its start may be spelled another way. Knock, knot and knife start with kn. Write and wrap start with wr.

Worked example: *The word for covering bread in paper*
1. It isn't under ra.
2. Try wr, like write.
3. Wrap is on the w pages.

**Card 21. -ic words (Keeper's Desk)**
> Words ending in -ic usually add -ally, not just -ly: dramatic, dramatically; basic, basically. Publicly is the odd one out.

Worked example: *basic + -ly*
1. Basic ends in -ic.
2. Add -ally.
3. Basically.

### (b) The Prefix Signs (Seedling, Sapling; first slice)

**Header:** Pick a prefix. Pick a word. Watch them join.

**Prefixes:** re-, pre-, un-, dis-, mis-, anti-, auto-, super-.

**Joins on the sign board, with their meanings:** reopen, open again · reuse, use again · preheat, heat before · prepay, pay before · preview, a look before · unsold, not sold · unwrapped, not wrapped · unnamed, with no name yet · unnoticed, not noticed · dislike, not like · disobey, not obey · misprint, a mistake in printing · mislabel, label wrongly · misspell, spell wrongly · antifreeze, works against freezing · autograph, your own name, written by you · autopilot, flies a plane by itself · superstar, a very famous star · supermarket, a very big food store.

**The join:** each join shows prefix and word in two colors. Where a letter doubles, both letters glow: mi**s s**pell, u**n n**amed, u**n n**oticed.

**Any other pair:** A word to wonder about. What could these parts mean together? Check a dictionary to see if it's there.

### (c) The Ending Signs (Seedling, Sapling, Keeper's Desk)

**Header:** Pick a word and an ending. Then pick a tool: just add, drop the e, y to i, or le to ly.

**Words and joins:** fresh, freshly · neat, neatly · usual, usually · complete, completely · happy, happily · easy, easily · busy, busily · gentle, gently · simple, simply · danger, dangerous · poison, poisonous · mountain, mountainous · fame, famous · glory, glorious · vary, various · joy, joyous · inform, information · prepare, preparation · admire, admiration · invite, invitation · imagine, imagination · explore, exploration.

**Keeper's Desk tool:** ic to ically: dramatic, dramatically · basic, basically.

**The no-root shelf:** enormous, jealous. These -ous words have no root word to find.

**The odd-ones shelf (Keeper's Desk):** truly: true drops its e · publicly: just -ly · courageous: courage keeps its e.

**Hint feedback (the board waits):**
- Drop the e before -ly: -ly starts with a consonant. Keep the e: completely.
- Just add on happy: Happy ends in a consonant, then y. Try y to i.
- y to i on joy: A vowel comes before this y. Keep it: joyous.
- Just add on gentle: Gentle ends in a consonant, then le. Try le to ly.

### (d) The Word-List Crate (Seedling, Sapling)

**Header:** These are long-list words. Sort them into families, then find each tricky part.

**Family crates:** eigh: eight, eighth, weight, height · ough: enough, though, although, thought, through · ear: early, earth, heard, learn · double letters: address, appear, different, difficult, possible, opposite.

**Helper cards:** separate has a rat in the middle · business starts with bus · island is an is and a land · believe has a lie inside.

**Hint feedback:** Look for the letters the family shares. Do you see them in this word?

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Spelling family:** words that share a spelling pattern, like walk, talk and chalk.
- **Prefix:** letters added at the start of a word that change its meaning, like re- in reopen.
- **Suffix:** letters added at the end of a word, like -ly in freshly. Also called an ending.
- **Root word:** a word before any prefix or suffix is added, like spell in misspell.
- **Doing word:** a word for an action, like invite. Also called a verb.
- **Naming word:** a word for a thing or an idea, like invitation. Also called a noun.
- **Describing word:** a word that tells what something is like, like famous. Also called an adjective.
- **Vowels:** the letters a, e, i, o and u.
- **Consonants:** the other letters.
- **Dictionary:** a book or screen that lists words in ABC order and tells what they mean.
- **ABC order:** the order of the alphabet. Also called alphabetical order.
- **Guide words:** the first and last words on a dictionary page, shown at the top.
- **Autograph:** your own name, written by you, for someone to keep.
- **Antifreeze:** a liquid that keeps water from freezing.
- **Preheat:** to heat an oven before you bake in it.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** other spellings for sounds a child already knows (a before l, o in Monday, -ey, a, ar and or after w, the -tion ending, and four homophone pairs); the prefixes re-, pre-, un-, dis-, mis-, anti-, auto- and super-, their meanings and the rule that a prefix usually changes no letters; the suffixes -ly, -ous and -ation and the root changes they bring; less common spellings (y in gym, ou in young, -sure and -ture); words from the Years 3 and 4 list, chosen by pattern; and finding a word in a dictionary by its first two, three or four letters.

**About sounds:** the English national curriculum states these patterns by sound. The ball family is the "aw" sound of saw, spelled a before l (the curriculum, in a British accent, calls it "or"); the Monday family is the "u" sound in "cup" spelled o; -ey spells the "ee" sound at the end of key; after w, a spells the vowel of "hot" (watch), ar the "or" sound of "for" (warm), and or the vowel of "her" (word). -sure ends measure and -ture ends picture: the endings sound different, and the sound tells you which, usually. Why kn and wr are spelled that way belongs to The Old Keeper's Spellings; here they are only places to look in a dictionary. Here and hear, two, too and to, bee and be, blue and blew sound the same: they are homophones, and meaning decides. On screen, this pack shows only spelling families and meanings. Say the words aloud together if that helps. Sound teaching and recorded audio are for the separate phonics review.

**About the word list:** the Years 3 and 4 list in England is statutory there. Here it is a reference only: words are chosen by pattern, and British spellings on it (centre, favourite) are not used. Families and helpers (a rat in separate) make long lists easier than drilling them one by one.

**About US and British spelling:** this pack uses US forms (mislabeled, humorous). British spelling doubles the l (mislabelled) and writes humour, which drops its u before -ous: humorous.

**About the Spanish signs:** the trader's lines sit beside English and await a fluent reader. The pack shows only two word pairs. It does not teach a rule for turning Spanish words into English ones.

**What it does not claim:** these are usual patterns, not laws, and a dictionary settles any doubt. Word Garden owns un- and re- on its own words; The Grafting Bench owns what verb prefixes mean and Latin and Greek roots; One Seed, Many Words owns -s, -ed, -ing, -er and -est and the root changes; The Old Keeper's Spellings owns silent letters, later homophones and -able and -ible for older explorers. Willowbank, its signs, the pumpkin and the Stall Dictionary are made up.
