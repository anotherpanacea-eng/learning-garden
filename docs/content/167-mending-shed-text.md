## Story scenes

### Scene 1. The right stuff

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** The barn roof drips. The grain sacks are rotten. The well bucket split. Hob needs help, and Pip has an idea involving bread.

**Short:**
> Hob needs a roof, sacks and a bucket mended. Pip offers bread. Bread squashes and soaks. You start sorting the heap.

**Longer:**
> Rain drips through Hob's roof into a pail. The pail is full.
>
> "Three jobs before supper," says Hob. "Patch the roof. New grain sacks. And the well bucket split down one side."
>
> Pip lifts a loaf from his bag. "Bread fills holes. I have filled many holes with bread."
>
> "Those holes were in you," says {guide}.
>
> Hob holds up two buckets from the heap. One is wood. One is metal. "Which one's the bucket?"
>
> "Both," you say.
>
> "Right. Same job, different stuff." He taps the wood. "This is the object. That's the material it's made of."
>
> Pip squeezes his loaf. It squashes and stays squashed. "Bread is a material too."
>
> "It is," says Hob. "For eating. Not for buckets."
>
> {guide} pulls the first thing off the heap. "Then let's find out what everything is made of."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):**
> In science, a material is whatever an object is made of: wood, metal, glass, plastic, cloth, stone. The same kind of object can be made of different materials, and one object can use several. A lantern might have a metal frame, glass panes and a cotton wick. To name a material, look, touch and test. The object's name never tells you.

### Scene 2. Bramble's glass hammer

**Hook:** Bramble arrives to help with the gate. She has brought a hammer made of glass. It is very beautiful. Hob looks worried.

**Short:**
> Bramble wants a glass hammer. It looks wonderful. Glass is hard but snaps when struck. A hammer needs a tough material, so Hob uses iron.

**Longer:**
> Bramble holds up a hammer that sparkles in the doorway light.
>
> "Glass," she says. "Clear as a pond. Every mender should have one."
>
> Her magpie hops behind a barrel.
>
> "What does a hammer have to do?" asks {guide}.
>
> "Hit nails," says Pip. "Hard. Many times."
>
> Hob sets a slate on the bench and gives it a gentle bend. It snaps in two. "Slate's hard. Great for a roof. But it won't bend, and it breaks if you knock it wrong. Glass is the same."
>
> Bramble looks at her hammer, then at the slate. "So it's a hammer for looking at."
>
> "A lovely one," says Hob, and hands her an iron hammer. "This one's for nails."
>
> Bramble takes it. "Fine. But my hammer is still the best-looking hammer in the valley."
>
> Nobody argues with that.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):**
> Choosing a material starts with the job. Looks come last. A hammer must take hard knocks again and again without breaking. A window must let light through and keep rain out. Most jobs need several properties at once, so the best material is the best for that job. Slate makes a good roof because it is waterproof and lasts through frost, even though it snaps if bent.

### Scene 3. Fair or not?

**Hook:** Dot needs a mailbag that keeps letters dry. Bramble says she already tested the cloths. Her test has a problem.

**Short:**
> Bramble poured more water on one cloth than the other. That isn't fair. You test again: same squares, same water, same time.

**Longer:**
> Dot sets four cloths on the bench. "The mailbag must keep letters dry in a downpour."
>
> "Done," says Bramble. "I poured water on the sacking and on the waxed cloth. The waxed cloth let less through. Obviously."
>
> "How much water did you pour?" asks {guide}.
>
> "A full jug on the sacking. Half a jug on the waxed one. I got tired."
>
> Dot's hat tilts. "Then we don't know if it was the cloth or the amount."
>
> You cut four squares, all the same size. You pour the same water, from the same height, for the same time. You do it three times for each cloth and write every number down.
>
> The waxed cloth still lets the least through.
>
> "I was right," says Bramble.
>
> "You were," says Dot. "Now we can show it."

**Keeper's note (A true thing from history):**
> In 1823 Charles Macintosh, a Scottish chemist, patented a waterproof cloth: a layer of dissolved rubber between two layers of fabric. Early coats went stiff in cold weather and sticky in hot weather, and the seams could leak. One good property came with some bad ones. The raincoat called a "mackintosh" is named after him.

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer; worked-example steps appear one tap at a time.

**Card 1. What is it? What is it made of? (A true thing about science)**
> An object is a thing with a job. A material is the stuff it is made of. Ask two questions.

Worked example: *Three buckets from the heap.*
1. What is each one? A bucket, a bucket, a bucket.
2. What is it made of? Wood. Metal. Plastic.
3. Same object. Three different materials.

**Card 2. Same pieces, new thing**
> Take an object apart. Build the pieces into something new. Count them. The pieces are still there.

Worked example: *Hob's broken gate.*
1. The gate has 7 planks and 2 crossbars.
2. Take it apart: 9 pieces in a line.
3. Build a bench with all of them.
4. Count again: 9. Nothing new. Nothing lost.

**Card 3. Squash, bend, twist, stretch**
> Pushing and pulling can change a solid's shape. Some materials spring back. Some keep the new shape. Some hardly change, or snap.

Worked example: *Clay and a rubber strip.*
1. Squash the clay. Let go. It stays flat.
2. Stretch the rubber. Let go. It springs back.
3. Both are still clay and rubber.

**Card 4. What does the job need?**
> First ask what the job needs. Then pick a material with that property. Looks don't count.

Worked example: *The grain sacks.*
1. Job: keep the grain dry on the cart.
2. It needs to be waterproof.
3. Paper soaks through. Waxed cloth doesn't.
4. Choose waxed cloth.

**Card 5. Make it fair**
> Change one thing: the material. Measure one thing. Keep everything else the same.

Worked example: *Two cloths for Dot's mailbag.*
1. Change: the cloth.
2. Measure: water that drips through.
3. Keep the same: square size, water, height, time.
4. Now the cloth is the only difference.

| Sample | Result pictures (A simplified picture) |

### (e) The Test Table

**Prompt:** Choose one test. Do it the same way to every sample.
**Tests:** Magnet · Bend · Water drop · Light through
**Rule line:** Write each result first. Sort after.
**Samples:** iron nail · iron hinge · copper kettle · brass bell · aluminum cup · wooden peg · glass jar · rubber strip

| Test | Group names | Results |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Magnet | Magnet pulls / No pull | Pulls: iron nail, iron hinge. No pull: the other six. |
| Bend | Bends / Stays stiff | Bends: rubber strip. Stiff: the rest, in this bench. |

**Regroup line:** Same samples, new test, new groups.

### (g) The Silt Jar (revisit)

**Prompt:** Hob's field is covered in flood mud. Get the parts back.
**Steps:** Sieve out the gravel · Shake the rest in water · Wait

### (h) The Scrap Box (Keeper's Desk)

**Arrival (A story from Willowbank):** Wren Halloway sets a box on the table. "Before anything goes to the tip, we decide: mend it, reuse it, or recycle it."

**Bins:** Mend · Reuse · Recycle · Not sure yet
**Cards and feedback:**
- Bucket with a cracked hoop: Mend. A new hoop keeps the whole bucket working.
- Torn sack: Mend or reuse. A patch, or a cleaning rag.
- Glass jar: Reuse first. Clean, sorted glass can be melted and remade many times.
- Aluminum can: Recycle. Remaking it takes about 5% of the energy of new metal.
- Steel can: Recycle. A magnet pulls it out of the mix.
- Newspaper: Recycle, for a while. Paper fibers get shorter each time.
- Bent hinge: Mend. Straighten it. It's still iron.
**Not sure yet:** That's allowed. What would help you decide?
**Magnet sorter line:** The magnet lifts the steel. The aluminum stays on the belt.
**Energy strip label:** A rounded comparison from a real figure: 100 from ore, about 5 recycled.

## Problem sets

Each item lists prompt, choices (right answer marked ✔), misconception tags for wrong choices, hint and explanation. Feedback for a wrong choice is the explanation's first sentence unless given.

### Set A. What is it made of? (Sprout, Seedling)

**A1 (Sprout, guided).** Steps shown first: 1. What is it? 2. What is it made of?
- Prompt: Hob holds a wooden bucket. What is it made of?
- Choices: A bucket (`object-is-material`) · Wood ✔ · Cloth (`material-means-cloth`)
- Feedback, "A bucket": That's what it is. Now, what stuff is it made of?
- Hint: Touch it in your mind. Is it soft, cold or woody?
- Explanation: The bucket is the object. Wood is its material.

**A2 (Sprout).**
- Prompt: Wood, metal and plastic buckets. What is the same about them?
- Choices: They are all buckets ✔ · They are all made of metal (`always-same-material`) · They are all cloth (`material-means-cloth`)
- Hint: Ask "What is it?" for each one.
- Explanation: They do the same job, so they're all buckets. Their materials differ.

**A3 (Seedling).**
- Prompt: Dot's lantern. Which materials is it made of?
- Choices: Only metal, all the way through (`one-object-one-material`) · Metal and glass (underclaim: the wick is easy to miss) · Metal, glass, cotton ✔
- Feedback, "Metal and glass": Good spotting. Look at what burns inside.
- Hint: Find the frame, the windows, and the wick.
- Explanation: Many objects use several materials. The wick is cotton.

**A4 (Sprout, number).**
- Prompt: The gate has 7 planks and 2 crossbars. How many pieces?
- Answer: 9. Follow-up: Hob builds a bench from all of them. How many pieces now? **9**.
- Steps: 7 + 2 = 9. Nothing was added or lost, so still 9.
- Misconception watched: `pieces-used-up`.
- Hint: Count planks, then add the crossbars.
- Explanation: Taking apart and rebuilding moves the pieces. It doesn't make or lose any.

**A5 (Seedling).**
- Prompt: Hob builds a shelf from 6 of the 9 pieces. Where are the rest?
- Choices: Used up by the flood (`pieces-used-up`) · On the floor: 3 pieces ✔ · Hidden inside the new shelf (`rebuilt-is-new-stuff`)
- Hint: 9 pieces, 6 used. Count what's left.
- Explanation: 9 − 6 = 3. The other three pieces are still there, waiting.

### Set B. Squash, bend, twist, stretch (Sprout, Seedling)

**B1 (Sprout, guided).** Steps shown first: 1. Squash the clay. 2. Let go. 3. Look.
- Prompt: You squash a ball of clay and let go. What happens?
- Choices: It stays flat ✔ · It springs back round (`everything-springs-back`) · It turns into rubber (`shape-changes-material`)
- Hint: Think of pressing a thumb into clay.
- Explanation: Clay keeps its new shape. It is still clay.

**B2 (Sprout).**
- Prompt: You stretch the rubber strip and let go. What happens?
- Choices: It stays long (`one-rule-for-all`) · It snaps at once (`rigid-just-push-harder`) · It springs back ✔
- Hint: Rubber is like a hair tie.
- Explanation: Rubber is elastic. It springs back to its first shape.

**B3 (Seedling).**
- Prompt: Hob bends a slate and it snaps. What is it now?
- Choices: Two pieces of slate ✔ · A new material (`shape-changes-material`) · Bendy, if pushed harder (`rigid-just-push-harder`)
- Hint: Did the stuff change, or only the shape?
- Explanation: Slate is rigid. It breaks instead of bending, and both pieces are still slate.

**B4 (Seedling, number).**
- Prompt: A 10 cm rubber strip stretches to 16 cm. How much longer?
- Answer: **6 cm**. Follow-up: You let go. How long is it? **10 cm**.
- Steps: 16 − 10 = 6. Rubber springs back, so 10 cm again.
- Misconception watched: `one-rule-for-all`.
- Hint: Take the first length from the stretched length.
- Explanation: It was 6 cm longer while stretched, and back to 10 cm after.

### Set C. The right stuff for the job (Seedling, Sapling)

**C1 (Seedling, guided).** Steps shown first: 1. What is the job? 2. What property does it need? 3. Which material has it?
- Prompt: The grain must stay dry on the cart. Which sack?
- Choices: Paper, because it's nice and light (`single-property`) · Lace, because it's so pretty (`choose-by-looks`) · Waxed cloth, as it's waterproof ✔
- Hint: What will rain do to each one?
- Explanation: The job needs waterproof. Waxed cloth keeps water out.

**C2 (Seedling).**
- Prompt: Why is the mill window made of glass?
- Choices: Glass lets light through ✔ · Windows are always glass (`always-same-material`) · Glass bends easily (`property-mixup`)
- Hint: What does a window need to let in?
- Explanation: Glass is transparent and keeps rain out. That's what a window needs.

**C3 (Sapling).**
- Prompt: Macintosh's cloth kept rain out but stiffened in cold. What does that show?
- Choices: Rubber is the best material for everything (`one-best-material`) · A good property can come with bad ones ✔ · The rain made it stiff (`misread-source`)
- Hint: Read the history card again. What did cold weather do?
- Explanation: Most materials have strengths and weaknesses. Choosing means weighing them.

**C4 (Sapling).**
- Prompt: Waxed paper lets light through, but not a clear view. It is...
- Choices: transparent (`see-through-binary`) · opaque (`see-through-binary`) · translucent ✔
- Hint: Some light passes, but can you see shapes clearly?
- Explanation: Translucent means some light gets through, without a clear view.

**C5 (Sapling).**
- Prompt: A pan's wooden handle stays cool. Which property is that?
- Choices: Wood lets heat through slowly ✔ · Wood blocks electricity, so it's cool (`heat-equals-electric`) · Wood is opaque (`property-mixup`)
- Hint: Heat and electricity are two different tests.
- Explanation: Wood is a poor heat conductor. Its electricity result is a separate test.

### Set D. The test table and the sack test (Sapling, Keeper's Desk)

**D1 (Sapling, guided).** Steps shown first: 1. What changed? 2. Was anything else different? 3. Can we tell which caused it?
- Prompt: Bramble poured 100 mL on sacking, 50 mL on waxed cloth. Fair?
- Choices: Yes, she tried both (`unfair-test-ok`) · No, two things changed ✔ · Yes, if she does it twice (`unfair-test-ok`)

**D2 (Sapling, number).**

- Misconception watched: `conclude-from-belief` (guessing instead of using the numbers).
- Hint: Take what came through from what was poured.
- Explanation: 38 mL stayed in the cloth or ran into the side tray.

**D3 (Sapling).**
- Prompt: Sacking 62, 66, 64. Waxed cloth 2, 4, 3. Which claim fits?

- Explanation: Every sacking trial (62 to 66) was above every waxed trial (2 to 4).

**D4 (Sapling).**
- Prompt: Which samples did the magnet pull?

- Hint: Look at your table for copper, brass and aluminum.

**D5 (Keeper's Desk, order and number; revisit of spec 145).**
- Prompt: Put the settling layers in order, first to last.
- Cards: clay · sand · silt. Answer: sand, silt, clay.
- Follow-up: Sand 5 cm, silt 3 cm, clay 2 cm. What part is sand? **Half** (5 of 10 cm).

### Set E. The scrap box (Keeper's Desk)

**E1 (Keeper's Desk, guided).** Steps shown first: 1. How long does iron ore take to form? 2. How fast do we use it?
- Prompt: Why do people call iron ore a finite material?
- Choices: There's none left anywhere on Earth (`already-gone`) · It forms far slower than we use it ✔ · There's too much to ever run out (`never-run-out`)
- Hint: Compare millions of years with a few years.
- Explanation: New ore forms over millions of years. We use it much faster than that.

**E2 (Keeper's Desk, number).**
- Prompt: A can from ore takes 100 energy units; recycled, about 5. How many times less?
- Answer: **about 20 times**. Steps: 100 ÷ 5 = 20.
- Misconception watched: `recycling-free` (recycling takes no energy).
- Hint: How many 5s make 100?
- Explanation: Recycling aluminum still uses energy, about 5% of making it new. A rounded figure.

**E3 (Keeper's Desk).**
- Prompt: Which material wears out a little each time it's recycled?
- Choices: Glass (`same-recycling-for-all`) · Paper ✔ · Steel (`same-recycling-for-all`)
- Feedback, Glass or Steel: Clean glass and steel can be remade many times. Which one has fibers?
- Hint: Think about fibers getting shorter.
- Explanation: Paper fibers shorten each time, so paper recycles only a few times, often said to be 5 to 7.

**E4 (Keeper's Desk, say why; a grown-up reads it).**
- Prompt: The bucket's hoop cracked. Mend it or recycle it? Tell a grown-up why.
- Grown-up note: Any answer that weighs a cost against a saving is a good answer. Mending keeps the whole bucket; recycling is useful when it can't be fixed. Nothing is saved or graded.

## "I'm stuck" worked examples

Shown at the second rung, with "how to start this one."

- **Object or material (A1-A3):** *A metal spoon.* What is it? A spoon. Made of? Metal. Start: say "It is a ... made of ...".
- **Counting pieces (A4-A5):** *4 bricks become a wall.* Take it apart: 4. Build a step: still 4. Start: count first, then build.
- **Shape changes (B1-B4):** *A sponge.* Squeeze, let go, it springs back. Start: think of letting go.
- **Choosing (C1-C3):** *An umbrella.* Job: keep rain off. Needs: waterproof. Start: name the job first.
- **Property words (C4-C5):** *Frosted glass in a bath-house door.* Light comes through, no clear view: translucent. Start: ask "how much light?"
- **Fair tests (D1-D3):** *Two cups of seeds, one watered, one not, one in sun, one in shade.* Two things changed. Start: list what's different.
- **Magnets (D4):** *An iron key and a copper coin.* The magnet pulls the key only. Start: name each metal.
- **Settling (D5):** *Pebbles and flour shaken in water.* Pebbles sink first. Start: biggest first.
- **Scrap box (E1-E3):** *A tree versus a mine.* A tree regrows in years; ore takes millions. Start: compare how fast each comes back.

## Key terms

- **object:** a thing with a job, like a bucket or a gate.
- **material:** the stuff an object is made of, like wood or metal.
- **property:** how a material behaves when you test it.
- **squash, bend, twist, stretch:** press, curve, turn the ends opposite ways, pull.
- **elastic:** springs back after squashing or stretching.
- **flexible / rigid:** bends without breaking / doesn't bend.
- **brittle:** snaps instead of bending.
- **absorbent / waterproof:** soaks up water / doesn't let water through.
- **transparent / translucent / opaque:** clear view through / some light, no clear view / no light through.
- **magnetic:** pulled by a magnet. Only some metals are.
- **conductor of heat:** lets heat through easily. Metal pans do. A separate test from electricity.
- **fair test:** change one thing, measure one thing, keep the rest the same.
- **finite:** there is only so much, and it doesn't come back fast.
- **recycle:** break down a used material to make new things.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** the difference between an object and its material; that pieces taken apart and rebuilt persist; how squashing, bending, twisting and stretching affect different materials; choosing a material by the property a job needs; classifying by one consistent test; reading a fair test with repeats; precise property words; and, at Keeper's Desk, finite raw materials and the trade-offs of mending, reusing and recycling.

**What it does not claim:** the bench results and the sack-test numbers are authored, simplified pictures, not measurements. It does not explain properties with particles, teach rusting or other chemical reactions, or teach electrical circuits (spec 163). Recycling figures are rounded. "Mend before you recycle" is a rule of thumb to discuss.

**Sources in brief:** US EIA on aluminum recycling energy; USGS on mineral deposits as finite; US EPA on paper fiber limits; industry and university sources on magnetic metals, recycling separators, glass and steel; encyclopedia and history sources on Charles Macintosh's 1823 patent; extension-service soil jar guides.
