# The Grafting Bench: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 266](../specs/266-grafting-bench.md). Use as written, after review. `{guide}` is never gendered. Pronouns: Pip he/him; Dot, the orchard keeper, Nuria she/her; Léon he/him; the librarian they/them. Sources: [audit](../source-audits/266-grafting-bench.md). Nothing is scored, timed or locked.

**Language rule:** every Spanish or French line sits beside an English line and is marked *(fluent reader)*. A fluent Spanish reader and a fluent French reader check each one before build.

**Labels used throughout:**
- **A story from Willowbank** marks the town, its people and their letters (fiction).
- **A true thing about language** marks real word meanings and word histories, each checked in a dictionary.

**Word parts and word history are kept apart.** "Grow and growth are family" is a fact about English today. "Graft comes from a Greek word for writing" is a word history, and every word history here was checked in a dictionary (see the audit).

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. The grafting bench (Act II, chapter 2, the garden)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** The orchard keeper joins a twig to a young tree's roots. Dot is writing the garden record. Pip wants to know why graft sounds like graph.

**Short:**
> The orchard keeper grafts a twig onto roots. Dot says words work like that: parts join onto a root. And graft and graph turn out to be family.

**Longer:**
> The orchard keeper fits a thin twig into a cut on a young tree and binds it tight.
>
> "The roots hold," she says. "The twig joins on. Next spring it grows."
>
> Dot is drawing the garden record on squared paper. "Words do that," she says. "Grow is the root. Join on parts and you get grower. Growth. Regrow."
>
> "Overgrow," says Pip, looking at the brambles.
>
> Then Pip frowns. "Graft. Graph. Graft. Graph. Are they the same word?"
>
> Nobody knows. So {guide} fetches the librarian, who brings the big dictionary.
>
> The librarian reads, and smiles. "Very old cousins. Graft was probably named after a pointed writing stick."
>
> The orchard keeper holds up her twig. It is thin and pointed, like a pencil.
>
> "Huh," she says. "Thirty years I've been holding a pencil."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> Dictionaries trace *graft* through old French to a Latin word for a writing stick, and back to the Greek *graphein*, "to write." A grafting twig probably got the name because it looked like that stick. *Autograph* and *photograph* come from the same Greek word.

### Scene 2. Letters up and down the river (Act III, chapter 4)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** Two letters arrive. Nuria writes from upstream in Spanish and English. Léon writes from downstream in French and English. Some words look like cousins.

**Short:**
> Nuria writes *escribo*. Léon writes *écris*. Both mean "I write." You spot the shared root, like *describe*. Then Pip trips on a word that only looks friendly.

**Longer:**
> Nuria's letter begins: *Te escribo esta mañana.* (fluent reader) I'm writing to you this morning.
>
> Léon's begins: *Je t'écris ce matin.* (fluent reader) I'm writing to you this morning.
>
> "Escribo. Écris," says Dot. "They look alike."
>
> "Like describe," says {guide}. "And script."
>
> "Latin again," says the librarian. "Scribere meant to write. All three languages have a piece of it."
>
> Pip reads on. *Compré un libro en la librería.* (fluent reader) I bought a book at the bookshop.
>
> "Librería!" says Pip. "Library! But you can't buy library books."
>
> "Read her English line," says the librarian.
>
> "Bookshop," Pip reads. "Oh."
>
> "In Spanish a library is a *biblioteca*," says the librarian. "Librería and library share an old root. Their meanings drifted apart."
>
> Pip writes in his notebook: ROOTS ARE CLUES. CHECK THEM.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> Latin *liber* meant book, and first meant the inner bark of a tree, once used for writing. It gave English *library* and Spanish *librería*, which means bookshop. A different Latin *liber* meant free. That one gave *liberty*. Same letters, two roots.

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer; worked-example steps appear one tap at a time. Cards 1 and 6 to 10 are **A true thing about language**.

**Card 1. A word family**
> Words that share a root are a word family. They share part of their spelling and part of their meaning.

Worked example: *The grow family.*
1. Root: grow.
2. Join on parts: grower, growth, regrow, overgrow.
3. Every one is about growing.

**Card 2. Same letters, not always family**
> Some words hide the same letters but mean something else. Check the meaning, not just the letters.

Worked example: *Growl.*
1. Growl has g-r-o-w inside.
2. A growl is a deep, angry sound.
3. It isn't about growing. Not family.

**Card 3. Prefixes that change actions: re- and over-**
> A prefix joins the front of a verb and changes the action. Re- can mean again or back. Over- often means too much.

Worked example: *Plant and water.*
1. Replant: plant again.
2. Overwater: water too much.
3. The action stays. The prefix changes how it's done.

**Card 4. Prefixes that change actions: dis-, de- and mis-**
> Dis- often means the opposite. De- can mean undo or take away. Mis- means wrongly.

Worked example: *Three actions.*
1. Disappear: the opposite of appear.
2. Defrost: take the frost away.
3. Misplace: put in the wrong place.

**Card 5. Endings that make verbs**
> The endings -ate, -ify and -ize can turn a naming or describing word into a verb, an action word. The spelling sometimes shifts.

Worked example: *Three new verbs.*
1. pollen → pollinate (bees pollinate flowers)
2. simple → simplify (simpl + ify)
3. memory → memorize (the y drops off)

**Card 6. Roots from Latin**
> Many English words have Latin roots. Port means carry. Scrib and script mean write. Vox and voc mean voice. Dict means say.

Worked example: *Transport.*
1. Trans- means across.
2. Port means carry.
3. Transport: carry across.

**Card 7. Roots from Greek**
> Some roots come from Greek. Graph means write. Tele means far. Phon means sound. Auto means self.

Worked example: *Autograph.*
1. Auto: self.
2. Graph: write.
3. An autograph: your name, written by yourself.

**Card 8. Cousins in other languages**
> Spanish and French grew from Latin. English took many words from Latin and French. So cousins can help you guess a meaning.

Worked example: *Write.*
1. Latin: scribere.
2. Nuria's Spanish: escribir. Léon's French: écrire. (fluent reader)
3. English: describe, script.

**Card 9. Cousins can drift**
> A shared root is a clue, not a promise. Over hundreds of years, a word's meaning can drift.

Worked example: *Librería.*
1. Spanish librería looks like library.
2. It means bookshop.
3. Check the sentence or a dictionary before you decide.

**Card 10. A plan for an unknown word (Keeper's Desk)**
> Read around it for clues. Look inside it for parts. Make a guess. Check the guess in a dictionary, then try it in the sentence.

Worked example: *Contradict.*
1. Around it: "Whatever Pip says, Bramble likes to contradict him."
2. Inside it: contra (against) + dict (say).
3. Guess: say the opposite. The dictionary agrees.

**Card 11. Words with more than one meaning (Keeper's Desk)**
> Many words have several meanings. A dictionary numbers them. Choose the one that fits the sentence.

Worked example: *Bank.*
1. "Otters slid down the muddy bank."
2. Senses: ground by a river; a place for money.
3. Muddy, otters: the river one.

## Benches

### (a) One Root, Many Shoots (Sprout, Seedling)

**Header:** Tap a shoot to join it to the root. Only family words grow here.

**Roots and shoots:**
- grow: grower · growth · regrow · overgrow · outgrow · *growl (not family)*
- plant: planter · replant · transplant · *planet (not family)*
- act: actor · action · active · react · *cactus (not family)*

**Feedback when a shoot joins:** grower: someone who grows things. · growth: how much something grows. · regrow: grow again. · overgrow: grow too much, over everything. · outgrow: grow too big for. · planter: someone or something that plants. · replant: plant again. · transplant: move a plant to a new place. · actor: someone who acts. · action: something done. · active: busy doing things. · react: act back, in answer.

**"Not this family" pot (a shoot that won't join):**
- growl: Growl is a sound, not growing.
- planet: Planet has p-l-a-n, but no plant in it.
- cactus: Cactus hides a-c-t, but it's a plant name.

**Card after a full root:** Same root, same meaning, many words.

### (b) Prefix Cuttings (Seedling, Sapling)

**Header:** Pick a verb. Then pick a prefix cutting. What happens to the action?

**Verbs and prefixed forms (only these grow; each shows its meaning):**
- appear: disappear (the opposite of appear) · reappear (appear again)
- agree: disagree (not agree)
- connect: disconnect (the opposite of connect) · reconnect (connect again)
- frost: defrost (take the frost away)
- code: decode (undo the code to find the message)
- place: misplace (put in the wrong place) · replace (put back, or swap)
- count: miscount (count wrongly) · recount (count again)
- cook: overcook (cook too long)
- water: overwater (water too much)
- fill: overfill (fill too full) · refill (fill again)
- tell: retell (tell again)
- write: rewrite (write again)

**Any other pair (a word to wonder about):** That one isn't on our list. Is it a real word? Check a dictionary, or try another cutting.

**Prefix meanings card:**
- re-: again, or back
- over-: often, too much
- dis-: the opposite of, or not
- de-: undo, or take away
- mis-: wrongly

**Not every over- means too much:** An overcoat goes over your other clothes. To overhear is to hear by chance.

### (c) Verb Makers (Sapling)

**Header:** Pick a word. Pick an ending. Make an action word.

**Words and verbs:**
- pollen + -ate → pollinate: Bees pollinate the apple blossom.
- pollen + -ize → pollenize: a rare word that also means pollinate. Pollinate is the usual one.
- active + -ate → activate: Press the lever to activate the pump.
- simple + -ify → simplify: The librarian will simplify the map.
- pure + -ify → purify: A filter can purify the water.
- solid + -ify → solidify: The wax will solidify as it cools.
- beauty + -ify → beautify: Dot wants to beautify the post office.
- memory + -ize → memorize: Pip tries to memorize the river words.
- fossil + -ize → fossilize: Bones can fossilize over a very long time.
- magnet + -ize → magnetize: Rub the pin on a magnet to magnetize it.

**Spelling shift cards:** simple: the le becomes l. · beauty, memory: the y changes or drops. · pollen: the e becomes i. The dictionary shows the spelling.

**Wrong ending (any):** That ending doesn't usually go on this word. Try another.

**Word job card:** Before: pollen names a thing. After: pollinate is an action. Now it can be the verb in a sentence.

### (d) The Root Family Bench (Sapling) *(suggested first slice)*

**Header:** One Latin root on the rootstock. Join English words to it. Then hang Nuria's and Léon's cousins beside them.

**The port root (A true thing about language):** In Latin, *portare* meant to carry.

**English twigs:**
- transport: carry across, from place to place
- portable: able to be carried
- export: carry out, to another country
- import: carry in, from another country
- report: carry news back

**Cousin tags (fluent reader):**
- Nuria's Spanish: *transportar* (to transport) · *exportar* (to export) · *portátil* (portable)
- Léon's French: *transporter* (to transport) · *exporter* (to export) · *porter* (to carry)

**Drifted far:** important is family too. Its meaning drifted a long way from carry.

**Card after the bench (A true thing about language):** Three languages, one Latin root. Spanish and French grew from Latin. English borrowed many Latin words.

**More rootstocks (after the first slice):**
- scrib, script (write): describe (tell in words) · script (the written lines of a play) · manuscript (written by hand). Cousins: *escribir* (to write), *écrire* (to write). (fluent reader)
- vox, voc (voice): vocal (using the voice) · voice. Cousins: *voz* (voice), *voix* (voice). (fluent reader)
- dict (say): predict (say before it happens) · contradict (say the opposite). Cousins: *decir* (to say), *dire* (to say). (fluent reader)

**Greek corner:** graph (write): autograph (your name, written by you), photograph (light + write). tele (far), phon (sound): telephone. Cousins: *teléfono*, *téléphone*. (fluent reader)

### (f) The Unknown Word (Keeper's Desk)

**Header:** A word you don't know. Four tools: read around it, look inside it, look it up, check it fits.

**Léon's note (A story from Willowbank):**
> Below the falls the river is too wild for my ferry. So we portage. We lift the boat out and carry it along the bank to calm water. *Nous portons le bateau.* (fluent reader) We carry the boat.

**Tool 1, read around it:** Which words tell you what they do? *(lift the boat out, carry it along the bank)*

**Tool 2, look inside it:** port (carry) + -age (the act of).

**Tool 3, look it up (dictionary card, A true thing about language):**
> **portage** 1. (noun) carrying boats or goods over land, from one water to another, or around rapids. 2. (noun) the place where this is done. 3. (verb) to carry a boat over land this way.

**Tool 4, check it fits:** Use sense 3, the verb: "So we carry the boat over land." It fits.

**Two meanings shelf:** current (moving water; happening now) · bank (ground by a river; a place for money) · stock (the rooted part of a grafted tree; goods in a shop; a cooking broth).

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Root:** the main part of a word, which carries its meaning. Grow is the root of growth.
- **Word family:** words that share a root and part of its meaning.
- **Prefix:** a part joined to the front of a word, like re- or dis-.
- **Suffix:** a part joined to the end of a word, like -ify or -er.
- **Verb:** an action word, like grow, carry or write.
- **Noun:** a naming word, like pollen or memory.
- **Adjective:** a describing word, like simple or pure.
- **Latin:** the language of ancient Rome. Spanish and French grew from it.
- **Greek:** the language of ancient Greece. English borrowed many Greek roots.
- **Cousin words:** words in different languages that grew from the same root.
- **Context:** the words and sentences around a word.
- **Rapids:** a part of a river where the water runs fast and rough.
- **Dictionary entry:** what a dictionary says about one word, with each meaning numbered.
- **Sense:** one meaning of a word that has several.
- **Rootstock:** the rooted part of a grafted tree. Also called the stock.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** word families built on a shared root; verb prefixes (dis-, de-, mis-, over-, re-) and how each changes an action; the verb-making suffixes -ate, -ify and -ize; Latin and Greek roots as clues, shown beside Spanish and French cousins; and, at Keeper's Desk, a plan for an unknown word: context, word parts, a dictionary, then a check, including words with several meanings.

**Word parts and word history:** the pack teaches how words are built in English today ("simple and simplify are family") separately from where words came from. Every word history in the pack (graft, liber, scribere, portare, vox, dicere, portage, benevolent, photograph, autograph) was checked in a dictionary, and the pack avoids popular stories about word origins that dictionaries don't support. One well-known teaching example, advert to advertise, is left out: dictionaries trace *advertise* to French, not to *advert*.

**Spelling: -ize and -ise.** The child text uses US spelling: memorize, fossilize, magnetize. In British English these are often spelled -ise (memorise); Oxford spelling keeps -ize. Some verbs end in -ise in every kind of English (advertise, surprise, exercise, supervise), because they were never made with the suffix. The English national curriculum calls these suffixes "-ate, -ise, -ify". The ending -en also makes verbs (sharpen, darken); it isn't taught here.

**Spanish and French:** the cousin words and letter lines are short and always shown with English. A fluent reader must check them before release. Cousin words are often called cognates. Words that look alike but mean different things, like *librería* (bookshop), are often called false friends.

**What it does not claim:** a shared root is a clue, not proof of a meaning. The pack shows a small, checked set of roots, not every Latin or Greek root. Real grafting science is taught at the Orchard Year (`#orchard/year`), not here. The *bank* card is simplified: many dictionaries give the river bank and the money bank separate entries, because they are two different words that happen to match.
