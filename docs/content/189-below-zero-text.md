# Below Zero: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 189](../specs/189-below-zero.md). Use as written, after independent spec review and a math check. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion and is never gendered; `{courier}` is the explorer's courier. Pip is he/him, Dot she/her, Bramble she/her (with her magpie), the miller she/her (with her cat), Nuria she/her, Léon he/him, Wren Halloway she/her, the baker he/him. Before the end of Act III the old Keeper is never named; only the Act IV scene says "Hollis". Real-world facts are in the [source audit](../source-audits/189-below-zero.md); items still marked `[check]` stay off screen until cleared. Nothing here is scored or timed.

Negative numbers are written with a true minus sign (−3), read aloud as "negative three". On screen, "three below zero" may follow in small type at Sprout and Seedling.

## Labels used on every card

- **A story from Willowbank**: the town, its brook, its flood post, its weather and every character are made up.
- **A true thing about math**: checked mathematics, explained simply.
- **A true thing from history**: something people really did or wrote.
- **A made-up model**: a simple game rule, shown on the card, that is not a real measurement.

>

## Teach cards

Each card is under 30 words. Worked steps appear one tap at a time.

**T1. Zero is a mark you choose** (A true thing about math)
> The post's zero is the brook's usual summer level. Above zero: higher than usual. Below zero: lower than usual. Below zero is not empty.
Worked example: 1. The water is 2 marks under zero. 2. Each mark is 10 cm. 3. The post reads −20 cm: 20 cm lower than usual.

**T2. Counting through zero** (A true thing about math)
> Count down past zero: 2, 1, 0, −1, −2. The numbers keep going. Say −2 as "negative two". It means two below zero.
Worked example: 1. Start at 2. 2. Count down 4: 1, 0, −1, −2. 3. Zero is one step, counted once.

**T3. Reading below zero** (A true thing about math)
> Water freezes at 0 °C. Colder readings have a minus sign. Count the marks down from zero: −1, −2, −3.
Worked example: 1. The liquid stops below zero. 2. Count marks down from 0: four marks. 3. The reading is −4 °C.

**T4. Lower is less** (A true thing about math)
> On a number line, numbers get bigger to the right and smaller to the left. −7 is left of −2, so −7 is less.
Worked example: 1. Find −2 and −7. 2. −7 is farther left, and lower on the thermometer. 3. −7 < −2, so −7 is colder.

**T5. Distance from zero** (A true thing about math)
> How far a number is from zero is its distance from zero. We write |−8| = 8. A distance is never negative.
Worked example: 1. −9 is 9 steps from zero. −2 is 2 steps. 2. |−9| = 9 and |−2| = 2. 3. −9 is farther from zero. −2 is greater.

**T6. Gaps across zero** (A true thing about math)
> To find a gap that crosses zero, split it at zero. Add the two parts. Count steps, not marks.
Worked example: 1. From −4 to 9. 2. −4 up to zero: 4. 3. Zero up to 9: 9. 4. 4 + 9 = 13.

**T7. Gaps on one side** (A true thing about math)
> If both numbers are below zero, the gap does not cross zero. Take the smaller distance from the bigger one.
Worked example: 1. −2 °C falls to −8 °C. 2. Distances from zero: 2 and 8. 3. 8 − 2 = 6 degrees colder.

**T8. Red rods and black rods** (A true thing about math)
> A red upright rod is one above zero. A black slanted rod is one below. One of each makes zero: a zero pair.
Worked example: 1. 3 red, 3 black. 2. Pair them off. 3. Three zero pairs: the tray shows 0.

**T9. Adding moves along the line** (A true thing about math)
> Adding a positive moves up or right. Adding a negative moves down or left. You can pass straight through zero.
Worked example: 1. 4 + (−7). 2. From 4, move 7 steps down. 3. 4 steps reach zero, 3 more reach −3.

**T10. Taking away what you don't have** (A true thing about math)
> Subtracting takes rods away. If the rods you need aren't there, add zero pairs first. Zero pairs don't change the total.
Worked example: 1. 1 − (−3). 2. One red rod; no black rods. 3. Add 3 zero pairs. 4. Take away 3 black. 5. 4 red: 4.

**T11. Two jobs for one sign** (A true thing about math)
> A minus sign can mean "below zero", as in −3. It can also mean "take away". Brackets keep them apart: 5 − (−3).
Worked example: 1. −5 + (−3): five below, then three more below. 2. −8. 3. Adding two negatives goes more negative. Multiplying them is different (T12).

**T12. Follow the pattern** (A true thing about math)
> −4 × 3 = −12. −4 × 2 = −8. −4 × 1 = −4. −4 × 0 = 0. Each step adds 4. What comes next?
Worked example: 1. Keep adding 4. 2. −4 × (−1) = 4. 3. −4 × (−2) = 8. 4. The pattern makes negative × negative positive.

**T13. Size first, then sign** (A true thing about math)
> Multiply or divide the sizes. Then the sign: same signs give positive, different signs give negative. Division uses the same rule.
Worked example: 1. −18 ÷ 3. 2. Size: 18 ÷ 3 = 6. 3. Different signs: −6.

**T14. Running time backward** (A true thing about math)
> The brook falls 4 cm a day: −4 each day. Two days ago is −2 days. (−2) × (−4) = +8.
Worked example: 1. Today: −12 cm. 2. Change since two days ago: +8 going back. 3. −12 + 8 = −4 cm two days ago.

## Real-world and history cards

**H1. Red rods and black rods** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> In China, an old book called *The Nine Chapters* gave rules for adding and taking away gains and losses. It is nearly 2,000 years old.
Keeper's note:
> In the year 263, a mathematician named Liu Hui wrote notes on the book. He said: use red counting rods for gains and black rods for losses. Rods could also be set slanted or upright to tell them apart. The Rod Tray uses both ideas.

**H2. Fortunes and debts** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> In India in 628, Brahmagupta wrote a book of rules for "fortunes" and "debts". One rule: a debt minus zero is a debt.
Keeper's note:
> His book is called the *Brahmasphutasiddhanta*. It is the oldest known book with rules for calculating with zero as a number, and it has rules for negatives too. Another rule: the product of two debts is a fortune. He wrote the rule; the Dry-Spell Log shows one reason it works.

**H3. The upside-down thermometer** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> In Sweden in 1742, Anders Celsius made a temperature scale. Water boiled at 0. Water froze at 100. Soon after, the scale was turned the other way up.
Keeper's note:
> On his first scale, a cold winter morning would read more than 100, with no minus sign at all. Where zero sits is a choice people make. In the United States, Fahrenheit puts freezing at 32, so −5 °C is 23 °F.

## Bench (a): The Flood Post

**Buttons:** Warmer by 1 · Colder by 1 · Count with me · Show the number line

## Bench (c): The Gap Line

**Buttons:** Turn the line · Mark two numbers · Split at zero · Distance from zero
**Split at zero** draws two arcs that meet at zero and labels each one.
**Distance from zero** draws one arc from zero and reads "|−8| = 8".

**Guided item:** How far is it from −4 to 9?
- Step 1: Mark −4 and 9.
- Step 2: −4 up to zero: 4 steps.
- Step 3: Zero up to 9: 9 steps.

## Bench (d): The Rod Tray

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> Red upright rods count one above zero. Black slanted rods count one below. A red and a black together make zero.

**Opening:** Build the number. Then add or take away rods.
**Buttons:** Add red · Add black · Take away red · Take away black · Add a zero pair · Pair them off · Show on the line

**Guided item:** 4 + (−7). Build it with rods.
- Step 1: Put out 4 red rods.
- Step 2: Add 7 black rods.

## Bench (e): The Dry-Spell Log (Keeper's Desk)

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> In this dry spell the brook falls 4 cm each day. Today the post reads −12 cm. Days ahead count +; days ago count −.

**Opening:** Fill the pattern. Then read the brook backward and forward.
**Buttons:** Next row · Day ahead · Day back · Show the pattern

**Pattern table:**

| Days from today | Change (cm) | Post reads (cm) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3 | 3 × (−4) = −12 | −24 |
| 2 | 2 × (−4) = −8 | −20 |
| 1 | 1 × (−4) = −4 | −16 |
| 0 | 0 × (−4) = 0 | −12 |
| −1 | (−1) × (−4) = 4 | −8 |
| −2 | (−2) × (−4) = 8 | −4 |
| −3 | (−3) × (−4) = 12 | 0 |

**Guided item:** −4 × 2 = −8. −4 × 1 = −4. −4 × 0 = 0. What is −4 × (−1)?
- Step 1: How much does each answer go up? *(4.)*
- Step 2: 0 + 4 = 4.
- Step 3: −4 × (−1) = 4.

Answer: +$4 (−2 − (−6) = 4).

Tests: −8 (adding the charge again); −4 (removing it but staying below zero).
Hint: Taking away a debt moves the account up.
Explanation: Removing a $6 debt from −$2 leaves $4 above zero.

**D6 (Keeper's Desk, say why; spoken to a grown-up, not stored)**
Prompt: Bramble says, "Two minuses make a plus." Is −5 + (−3) a plus? Why?
Model answer for the grown-up: No. −5 + (−3) = −8. Adding a negative moves down, so it gets more negative. The saying does not work for adding. For multiplying, the pattern table gives the real reason.
Tests: the slogan used for adding.
Hint: Start at −5 on the line. Which way does adding −3 move?
Explanation: Adding a negative moves down. Look at what each sign is doing.

### Set E. Signs when you multiply and divide (Keeper's Desk)

**E1 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Setup: −3 × 1 = −3 and −3 × 0 = 0.
Prompt: Find −3 × (−1) and −3 × (−2).
Answer: 3 and 6.
Worked steps: 1. Each answer goes up by 3. 2. 0 + 3 = 3. 3. 3 + 3 = 6.
Tests: −3 and −6 (two negatives staying negative).
Hint: How much does each answer change from one row to the next?
Explanation: The pattern keeps adding 3, so the answers become positive.

**E2 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Falling 4 cm a day. Today: −12 cm. What did it read two days ago?
- A. −20 cm. *(tests: running time forward instead of back)*
- B. −4 cm. *(right)*
- C. 8 cm. *(tests: forgetting to start from today's reading)*
Hint: Going back undoes the fall. How much does two days undo?
Explanation: (−2) × (−4) = +8. −12 + 8 = −4 cm.
Feedback A: −20 is two days ahead. Go the other way.
Feedback B: Yes. Eight centimeters higher than today.
Feedback C: +8 is the change. Add it to today's −12.

**E3 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: −3 × (−5) = ?
- A. −15. *(tests: two negatives staying negative)*
- B. 15. *(right)*
- C. −8. *(tests: adding instead of multiplying)*
Hint: Size first: 3 × 5. Then: same signs or different?
Explanation: 3 × 5 = 15. Same signs give a positive: 15.
Feedback A: Try the pattern: −3 × 1, −3 × 0, −3 × (−1)…
Feedback B: Yes. Same signs, positive.
Feedback C: −8 is −3 + (−5). This one multiplies.

**E4 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: The brook fell 20 cm in all, 4 cm a day. How many days?
Answer: 5 days (−20 ÷ (−4) = 5).
Worked steps: 1. Total change: −20. 2. Each day: −4. 3. −20 ÷ (−4) = 5.
Tests: −5 (different-sign rule used for same signs).
Hint: How many −4s make −20?
Explanation: Five days of −4 make −20, so −20 ÷ (−4) = 5.

**E5 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**

## Key terms (glossary)

- **zero mark**: the line a scale counts from. On the post, the usual summer level.
- **positive number**: a number above zero, like 3.
- **negative number**: a number below zero, like −3, said "negative three".
- **number line**: numbers in order along a line, smallest on the left.
- **less than (<)**: farther left on the number line.
- **greater than (>)**: farther right on the number line.
- **distance from zero (| |)**: how many steps a number is from zero; never negative.
- **gap**: how far apart two numbers are on the line.
- **zero pair**: one red rod and one black rod, which together make zero.
- **°C (degrees Celsius)**: a temperature scale where water freezes at 0.
- **debt**: money owed; an account below zero.
- **sign**: plus or minus, telling which side of zero.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** reading scales above and below a chosen zero (a flood post in centimeters, a thermometer in °C); counting through zero; ordering negative numbers; distance from zero (absolute value) as different from size order; gaps across zero; adding and subtracting negative numbers with rods and the number line; multiplying and dividing with signs, with a pattern that shows why a negative times a negative is positive.

**What it does not claim:** the brook, its readings and the weather are made up. It does not teach Fahrenheit conversion beyond one example, fractions or decimals below zero, or negative coordinates. It does not use "two negatives make a positive" as a rule for adding.
