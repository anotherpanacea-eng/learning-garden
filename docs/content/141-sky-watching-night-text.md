# The Sky-Watching Night: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 141](../specs/141-sky-watching-night.md). Use as written, after independent review and a subject check. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion and is never gendered. Pip is a fox (he/him), Dot a ladybug (she/her), Bramble a girl witch (she/her) with a magpie, the librarian is they/them, the baker he/him, Ember a pocket-dragon. Facts were source-checked on 2026-10-25 (see the audit). Nothing here is scored.

**Labels used on every card:**
- **A true thing about space** (real science)
- **A true thing from history** (real history, with a date)
- **A story from Willowbank** (the fictional town and its people)
- **A story people told** (star stories and myths)
- **A made-up model** or **A simplified picture** (benches and the walk)

## Arriving

**Place title:** The Library Roof

**Hook (first visit, 30 words max):**
> Up the library's back stairs is a flat roof with a rail, a lamp bench, a telescope and a star chart. Someone has left a page tucked inside the chart.

**Later visits:** The lamp bench is ready. The star chart is where you left it.

**Safety card (first visit, and on every off-screen card):**
> Never look at the Sun. Never point binoculars or a telescope at it. A real sky night needs a grown-up, a safe spot on the ground and warm clothes.

## The story

### Scene 1. The page in the star chart *(A story from Willowbank)*

**Short:**
> The librarian unrolls Hollis's star chart. A blank page falls out: *For the next Keeper*. "A sky night," says {guide}. "On Lantern Night. Let's plan it."

**Longer:**
> The librarian unrolls Hollis's old star chart on the roof table and holds the corners down with books. A page slides out. It is headed *For the next Keeper* and nothing else.
>
> "Hollis used to bring the whole town up here in winter," the librarian says. "They'd point at things and argue."
>
> "About what?" asks Pip.
>
> "Whether the Sun goes round us. Why the Moon changes. Which shiny dot is a planet." The librarian smiles. "Hollis liked arguments with evidence in them."
>
> Dot reads the empty page twice, as if words might appear. "Lantern Night is in three weeks," she says. "We could do it again."
>
> {guide} looks at the lamp bench and the old globe. "Then we'd better know how the sky works first," says {guide}. "Before anyone asks us."

**Keeper's note:**
> Act V's projects start from Hollis's blank pages. A sky night is a real thing people run: a few telescopes, a dark place, a grown-up in charge. It needs no special equipment. Eyes, a star chart and patience do most of the work.

### Scene 2. Ember's glow *(A story from Willowbank)*

**Short:**
> Ember can't see a single star. "Too bright," says {guide}. "You glow." Dot lends Ember her hat. Under the brim, stars appear.

**Longer:**
> On the first practice night, Ember climbs onto the rail and stares up. "There aren't any," Ember says. "Everyone says there are millions."
>
> Pip looks up. "I can see loads."
>
> "Then why can't I?"
>
> {guide} points at Ember's tail. It glows orange, like a small lantern. "Your light is in your eyes," says {guide}. "Faint stars get lost next to it. It's the same reason the town dims the bridge lanterns on Lantern Night."
>
> Dot takes off her hat. This is a serious moment, because Dot does not take off her hat. She sets it on Ember like a little roof. "Wait under there," she says. "Eyes need time to get used to the dark."
>
> Ember waits. One star. Then three. Then far too many to count.
>
> "They were there the whole time," says Ember.

**Keeper's note:**
> Stars don't switch off in daytime or near lights. The Sun or a lamp nearby is so bright that faint stars are lost next to it. Our eyes also take about half an hour to adjust fully to the dark, which is why sky-watchers avoid bright screens.

### Scene 3. The Scale Walk *(A story from Willowbank)*

**Short:**
> The baker lends a ball for the Sun. Planets become tiny beads along the river path. Earth is barely bigger than a crumb. Neptune is near the bridge.

**Longer:**
> The baker brings out a round ball about 20 centimeters across and sets it on his doorstep. "The Sun," he says. "Mind the bread."
>
> Bramble's magpie lands on it at once.
>
> "Now the planets," says {guide}. "Same scale for everything."
>
> Pip holds up a bead. "Is this Earth? It's tiny."
>
> "About two millimeters," says {guide}. "And it goes twenty-one and a half meters away."
>
> They pace it out along the river path. Mercury and Venus stay near the bakery. Mars is a speck by the café. Jupiter, the size of a big grape, is past the orchard gate.
>
> Then the path goes on, and on. Saturn. Uranus at the mill. Neptune, at last, near the bridge.
>
> Bramble looks back at the bakery. The Sun is a dot. "Most of it is nothing," she says. "Just path."
>
> "Most of space is space," says {guide}.

**Keeper's note:**
> With a 20 cm Sun, Earth is 1.8 mm wide and 21.5 m away, and Neptune is 649 m away (computed from NASA data). On this scale the nearest other star, Proxima Centauri, would be about 5,800 km away. Posters show the planets close together and big; that is a choice to fit the page.

### Scene 4. Lantern Night *(A story from Willowbank)*

**Short:**
> The bridge lanterns dim for one hour. The town looks up. Orion is over the hills. The magpie wants Jupiter. Nobody can reach it.

**Longer:**
> On Lantern Night the bridge lanterns go down to a glow, one by one. The town stands on the riverbank in coats and scarves.
>
> "Three stars in a row," says {guide}. "Orion's belt. You can find it on winter evenings."
>
> Bramble points the other way. "That's the Plough. My grandmother called it that."
>
> "Nuria calls it *el Carro*, the cart," says Dot, who has been reading letters. "Léon says *la Grande Casserole*. A big saucepan."
>
> Pip says it looks like a ladle full of soup.
>
> The magpie has been staring at one bright, steady dot for an hour. "That one's Jupiter," says {guide}. "It's shiny because sunlight bounces off it. It doesn't make its own light. The stars do."
>
> The magpie never gives up on anything shiny.
>
> When the lanterns come back on, the librarian hands you the page. It isn't blank any more.

**Keeper's note:**
> Constellation names are patterns people chose; the stars in one pattern can be at very different distances. Different places tell different stories about the same stars. Planets look steady and move against the stars from week to week; the word *planet* comes from a Greek word for "wanderer".

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer. Worked examples reveal one step per tap.

### Spin and orbit bench

**Model line (always shown):** A simplified picture: the lamp is much closer and smaller than the real Sun, and the globe is not to scale. It shows which side faces the light.

**Ask first:** What makes night? Tap your idea. *(The Sun goes behind the hills · The Sun goes around Earth · Earth turns away from the Sun · I'm not sure.)* No feedback on this one; it is kept for later.

**Teach 1 (A true thing about space):** Earth spins. The side facing the Sun has day. The side facing away has night. It is always day somewhere.

**Worked example (four taps):**
1. The sticker is Willowbank. It faces the lamp. Noon.
2. A quarter turn. Willowbank is at the edge of the light. Evening.
3. Another quarter. Willowbank faces away. Night. The far side has day.
4. Another quarter. Morning. One whole turn is about 24 hours.

**Teach 2:** The Sun looks like it moves across the sky. That's because we turn. "Sunrise" is a name for how it looks.

**What if the Sun went round?** In this toggle, the lamp circles the still globe. "From Willowbank, this looks the same. So how do we know which is true?" (Answer card: This ground view alone cannot decide. Other evidence is needed. Jupiter's moons show that not everything circles Earth; the January distance helps test the seasons explanation, not Earth's daily spin.)

**Teach 3:** Earth also travels all the way round the Sun. One trip takes about 365 days. That's a year. Spin makes the day. Orbit makes the year.

### Seasons panel

**Teach 4:** Earth's axis is tilted, and it always points the same way. On one side of the orbit, the north leans toward the Sun. That's northern summer.

**Teach 5:** Leaning toward the Sun means the Sun climbs higher. The same light lands on a smaller patch. Days are longer too. So it's warmer.

**Worked example (torch on squares, three taps):**
1. The torch shines straight down, like a high summer Sun. It lights 4 squares.
2. Now tilt it low, like a winter noon Sun. Same torch, same distance.
3. The same light spreads over about 10 squares. Each square gets less.

**Teach 6 (the surprise):** Earth is closest to the Sun in early January. That's northern winter. So distance can't be what makes seasons.

**Model line:** A simplified picture. The torch stands in for the Sun. Real sunlight comes from much farther away, but the spreading works the same way.

### Phase bench

**Model line:** A simplified picture: real sizes and distances are far bigger, and the Moon's path is tilted a little, so most full moons miss Earth's shadow.

**Teach 7:** The Sun always lights half the Moon. As the Moon goes round Earth, we see different amounts of that lit half.

**Worked example (four taps):**
1. Moon between Earth and Sun. The lit half faces away. We see almost nothing: new moon.
2. A quarter of the way round. We see half the lit half. A half moon.
3. Opposite the Sun. The whole lit half faces us. Full moon.
4. Back round again. It shrinks. About 29 and a half days in all.

**Teach 8:** The dark part is not Earth's shadow. Look: in a crescent, Earth's shadow points away from the Sun, nowhere near the Moon.

**Teach 9 (Line them up):** Sometimes Sun, Earth and Moon line up exactly at full moon. Earth's shadow falls on the Moon. That's a lunar eclipse. Most full moons miss.

**Link card:** Want to keep notes on the real Moon? Dot's Field Guide has a Sky page. *(Shown only when spec 116 exists.)*

### Planets and stars

**Teach 10:** Eight planets go around the Sun. In order: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune. Mercury is closest.

**Teach 11:** The four nearest are small and rocky. The four far ones are giants made of gas and ice. You couldn't stand on them.

**Teach 12:** The Sun is a star. It's our nearest one. Other stars are suns too, so far away they look like dots.

**Lamp card (worked, three taps):**
1. A lamp close to you looks big and dazzling.
2. The same lamp across the valley is a small dot.
3. The Sun is close. Stars are far. Same kind of thing.

**Teach 13:** Stars make their own light. Planets and the Moon shine because sunlight bounces off them. That's why Jupiter is shiny.

**Teach 14:** Light is fast, but not instant. Sunlight takes about 8 minutes 20 seconds to reach Earth.

**Pluto card (A true thing about space):** In 2006, astronomers agreed what counts as a planet. Pluto went into a new group: dwarf planets. Ceres and Eris are dwarf planets too.

### Scale Walk

**Model line:** A scale model of sizes and distances only, with one ratio. Planets are not lined up like this in real life; they are spread around their orbits.

**Teach 15:** In a scale model, you shrink everything by the same amount. Sizes and distances. Then the gaps look real.

**Worked example (Sun-widths, three taps):**
1. Earth is about 107 and a half Sun-widths from the Sun.
2. Our Sun is 20 cm wide. So 107.5 × 20 cm.
3. That's 2,150 cm, which is 21.5 meters. Put Earth there.

### Galileo's notebook

**Label (every page):** Our reconstruction from a modern computer model, not Galileo's own drawings. Real nights were spread over a week; this page squeezes them together.

**History card (A true thing from history):** In January 1610, Galileo pointed his telescope at Jupiter. He saw small lights beside it. Night by night, they moved.

**Teach 16:** Follow one dot. Tap Next night. Watch where it goes.

**After nine nights:** It went out, slowed, then came back across Jupiter. That's how a circle looks from the side.

**Coin card:** Turn a coin until you see only its edge. Something going round the rim would look like it slides side to side.

**History card 2:** Galileo did not invent the telescope. He built better ones in 1609. Late that year he saw mountains on the Moon.

**History card 3:** He wrote it all in a short book, *The Starry Messenger*, printed in Venice in 1610.

**History card 4 (why it mattered):** Most people thought everything circled Earth. Moons circling Jupiter showed that isn't true for everything. Ideas changed slowly, over many years.

### Exploration card *(A true thing from history)*

**Timeline (tap each):**
- 1957: Sputnik 1, the first satellite, launched by the Soviet Union.
- 1961: Yuri Gagarin is the first person in space.
- 1969: Apollo 11. Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin walk on the Moon.
- 2000: People start living on the International Space Station, and still do.
- 2021: The Perseverance rover lands on Mars. No person has been there.

**Teach 17:** Robots go where people can't yet. Mars is too far, too cold, with no air to breathe.

**Teach 18:** Messages to Mars take minutes. So a rover can't be steered live like a toy car.

### Constellations *(A true thing about space, with stories people told)*

- **The Big Dipper / the Plough:** seven bright stars, part of the Great Bear, Ursa Major. In French, *la Grande Casserole* (the big saucepan). In Spanish, *el Carro* (the cart).
- **Cassiopeia:** a W shape, across the Pole Star from the Big Dipper. In a Greek story, a queen on a chair. *(A story people told.)*
- **Orion:** three stars in a row, the belt. Easy to see on winter evenings. In a Greek story, a hunter. *(A story people told.)*

**Card line:** A constellation is a pattern people see. The stars in it can be very different distances away.

## Problem sets

Each item: an optional Given line, a prompt of 15 words or fewer, answer (✓), misconception tags, hint, explanation, and feedback for each choice (20 words or fewer).

### Set A. Day, night and seasons

**A1 · Sprout · 3-choice**
When is there more daylight?
- In winter. *Tag: `seasons-reversed`.* Feedback: In winter it gets dark early. Summer evenings stay light.
- In summer. ✓ Feedback: Yes. Summer days are long and winter days are short.
- Both the same. *Tag: `no-change`.* Feedback: Look at the two bars. One is much longer.
Hint: Picture a summer evening, then a winter evening. Explanation: There is more daylight in summer and less in winter. The pattern comes back every year.

**A2 · Seedling · 3-choice**
Why does Willowbank have night?
- Willowbank turns away from the Sun. ✓ Feedback: Yes. Earth spins, and Willowbank faces away for a while.
- The Sun goes behind the hills. *Tag: `behind-hills`.* Feedback: The hills hide the Sun for a bit. But the far side of Earth has day.
- The Sun goes around Earth each day. *Tag: `sun-moves`.* Feedback: It looks that way. But Earth is the one turning.
Hint: Turn the globe on the bench. What happens to the sticker? Explanation: Earth spins once in about 24 hours. The half facing away from the Sun has night.

**A3 · Seedling · number with steps**
Willowbank faces the lamp at noon. Half a turn later, what time is it?
Answer: midnight (12 hours later).
Steps: 1. One whole turn is about 24 hours. 2. Half of 24 is 12. 3. Noon plus 12 hours is midnight.
Tags tested by common wrong answers: 6 o'clock (`quarter-half`, counted a quarter turn); noon again (`spin-orbit`).
Hint: How long is one whole turn? Explanation: Half a turn takes about 12 hours, so noon becomes midnight.

**A4 · Seedling · 3-choice**
How long does Earth's trip around the Sun take?
- About one year. ✓ Feedback: Yes. One trip round the Sun is one year.
- About one day. *Tag: `orbit-day`.* Feedback: A day is one spin. The trip round the Sun is much slower.
- About one month. *Tag: `moon-month`.* Feedback: That's about how long the Moon's phases take. Earth's trip is longer.
Hint: Spin makes the day. What does the orbit make? Explanation: Earth goes round the Sun in about 365 days. In that time it spins about 365 times.

**A5 · Sapling · 3-choice**
Earth is closest to the Sun in early January. What does that tell you?
- January must be summer everywhere. *Tag: `whole-earth-summer`.* Feedback: Willowbank is cold in January. Closer didn't make it summer here.
- The measurement must be wrong. *Tag: `distance-seasons`.* Feedback: Astronomers measure this very carefully. The data is solid.
- Distance can't explain northern seasons. ✓ Feedback: Yes. Closest in our winter means distance isn't the cause.
Hint: What season is January in Willowbank? Explanation: Earth is about 147 million km away in January and 152 million in July. Northern winter happens when we're closest.

**A6 · Sapling · 3-choice**
Why is northern summer warmer?
- Earth is nearer the Sun then. *Tag: `distance-seasons`.* Feedback: Earth is actually a little farther away in July.
- The north leans toward the Sun. ✓ Feedback: Yes. More direct light and longer days.
- Earth's axis swings toward the Sun. *Tag: `axis-swivels`.* Feedback: The axis keeps pointing the same way. Earth's place in its orbit changes.
Hint: Watch the axis on the orbit bench. Does it ever turn? Explanation: The tilt stays the same. Where Earth is in its orbit decides which half leans sunward.

**A7 · Sapling · number with steps**
Given: the same torch lights 4 squares from high up and 10 squares from low down.
How many times more spread out is the low light?
Answer: 2.5 times (two and a half).
Steps: 1. Low: 10 squares. High: 4 squares. 2. 10 ÷ 4 = 2.5. 3. Each square gets less light from the low torch.
Tags: answer 6 (`subtract-not-divide`).
Hint: How many 4s make 10? Explanation: The low beam spreads the same light over 2.5 times as many squares. A low winter Sun warms the ground less.

**A8 · Keeper's Desk · say why (a grown-up reads)**
When Willowbank has winter, Australia has summer. Use this to argue against the distance idea.
Model answer: At any moment both halves of Earth are the same distance from the Sun. If distance made seasons, they would have the same season. They don't, so tilt must matter.
Hint: Are the two halves of Earth different distances from the Sun? Explanation: Opposite seasons at the same moment rule out distance as the cause.

### Set B. The Moon's faces

**B1 · Seedling · order the cards**
Start at new moon. Put the Moon's shapes in order.
Cards: full · thin growing · half growing · fat growing
Answer: thin growing → half growing → fat growing → full.
Tag: putting full second (`random-phases`).
Hint: The lit part grows a little each night. Explanation: After new moon, the lit part grows from a thin curve to a half, to more than half, to full.

**B2 · Seedling · 3-choice**
How much of the Moon does the Sun light up?
- Half of it, always. ✓ Feedback: Yes. We just see different amounts of that half.
- Only the part we can see. *Tag: `phase-property`.* Feedback: The Sun lights half. Sometimes most of that half faces away from us.
- All of it, at full moon. *Tag: `full-all-lit`.* Feedback: Even at full moon, the far side is dark. We see the whole lit half.
Hint: On the bench, look at the Moon from above. Explanation: The Sun always lights the half of the Moon facing it. The phase is how much of that half faces Earth.

**B3 · Sapling · 3-choice**
We see a thin crescent Moon. Where is Earth's shadow?
- On the dark part of the Moon. *Tag: `shadow-phases`.* Feedback: Look from above. In a crescent, the shadow points nowhere near the Moon.
- Pointing away from the Sun. ✓ Feedback: Yes. So the dark part can't be Earth's shadow.
- Earth doesn't make a shadow. *Tag: `no-shadow`.* Feedback: Earth blocks sunlight like any ball. The shadow points away from the Sun.
Hint: Shadows point away from the light. Where is the Moon in a crescent? Explanation: A crescent Moon is near the Sun's direction. Earth's shadow points the other way, so it doesn't cause the dark part.

**B4 · Sapling · 3-choice**
Why isn't there a lunar eclipse every month?
- Earth's shadow only appears in winter. *Tag: `shadow-seasonal`.* Feedback: Earth always has a shadow. It's about lining up.
- There is one every month. *Tag: `eclipse-monthly`.* Feedback: Full moon comes every month. Lunar eclipses are much rarer.
- The Moon's tilted path usually misses it. ✓ Feedback: Yes. It usually passes just above or below the shadow.
Hint: On Line them up, try a full moon a little high. Explanation: The Moon's path is tilted about 5 degrees. Only sometimes does a full moon pass right through Earth's shadow.

### Set C. Planets and stars

Where do you see each one? Drag to Day, Night, or Both.

**C2 · Seedling · order the cards (no line shown)**
Put the planets in order, starting nearest the Sun.

Tags: Earth first (`earth-first`); giants near the Sun (`giants-inner`).

**C3 · Seedling · match**
Match each planet to its clue.

Hint: Start with the one you're surest of. Explanation: Each planet has a feature that helps you remember it.

**C4 · Seedling · Which is a star? (3-choice)**
Bramble's magpie wants the shiniest thing. Which one is a star?
- The Moon. *Tag: `moon-makes-light`.* Feedback: The Moon shines because sunlight bounces off it.
- Jupiter. *Tag: `planets-make-light`.* Feedback: Jupiter is bright, but it shines by reflected sunlight.
- The Sun. ✓ Feedback: Yes. The Sun makes its own light. It's our nearest star.
Hint: Which one makes its own light? Explanation: Stars make light. Planets and moons shine because the Sun lights them.

**C5 · Sapling · 3-choice, then say why**
Why does the Sun look so much bigger than other stars?
- It's the biggest star there is. *Tag: `sun-biggest`.* Feedback: Many stars are bigger. The Sun is just much closer.
- It's much closer to us. ✓ Feedback: Yes. Close things look big. Far things look like dots.
- It isn't really a star. *Tag: `sun-not-star`.* Feedback: The Sun is a star. It's the one we live next to.
Then (grown-up reads): Use the lamp to explain why. Model answer: A lamp nearby looks big and bright; far away it's a dot. Stars are so far away they look tiny.
Hint: Remember the lamp across the valley. Explanation: The Sun is our nearest star. Distance makes other stars look like points.

**C6 · Sapling · 3-choice**
What happened to Pluto in 2006?
- It moved into a new group. ✓ Feedback: Yes. Astronomers agreed a new rule, and Pluto became a dwarf planet.
- It broke apart in space. *Tag: `pluto-destroyed`.* Feedback: Pluto is still there. Only its group changed.
- It turned out to be a mistake. *Tag: `pluto-mistake`.* Feedback: Nobody got Pluto wrong. The meaning of "planet" got sharper.
Hint: Did Pluto change, or did the word change? Explanation: In 2006 a new definition of planet put Pluto with the dwarf planets. Science updates its groups when it learns more.

### Set D. Scale and light

**D1 · Seedling · 3-choice**
Given: your Sun is 20 cm wide. The Sun is about 109 times wider than Earth.
How wide is your Earth bead?
- About 2 mm. ✓ Feedback: Yes. 200 mm ÷ 109 is about 1.8 mm. Tiny.
- About 2 cm. *Tag: `planets-big`.* Feedback: That's ten times too big. Try 200 mm ÷ 109.
- About 20 cm. *Tag: `same-size`.* Feedback: That's the same as the Sun. Earth is much smaller.
Hint: 20 cm is 200 mm. Divide by about 100 to estimate. Explanation: 200 ÷ 109 ≈ 1.8, so Earth is a bead about 2 mm across.

**D2 · Sapling · number with steps**
Given: Earth is about 107.5 Sun-widths from the Sun. Your Sun is 20 cm.
How far from the Sun does Earth go, in meters?
Answer: 21.5 m.
Steps: 1. 107.5 × 20 cm. 2. = 2,150 cm. 3. 100 cm is 1 m, so 2,150 cm = 21.5 m.
Tags: 2.15 m or 215 m (`unit-slip`).
Hint: Multiply first. Then change cm to m. Explanation: Using Sun-widths keeps one ratio for everything. Earth goes 21.5 m from the Sun.

**D3 · Sapling · 3-choice**
Given: Neptune is about 30 times as far from the Sun as Earth. Earth is at 21.5 m.
About how far from the Sun does Neptune go?
- About 60 m. *Tag: `even-spacing`.* Feedback: That's if planets were evenly spaced. The gaps get much bigger farther out.
- About 6.5 km. *Tag: `overclaim-scale`.* Feedback: That's ten times too far. Try 30 × 21.5.
- About 650 m. ✓ Feedback: Yes. 30 × 21.5 is about 645, and the exact figure is 649 m.
Hint: Multiply 21.5 by 30. Explanation: Neptune is far out. On a 20 cm Sun walk it sits almost at the bridge.

**D4 · Sapling · 3-choice**
Given: your Sun is 30 cm. Neptune lands at 973 m. The bridge is at 700 m.
What should you do?
- Put Neptune at the bridge anyway. *Tag: `mixed-scale`.* Feedback: Then Neptune is on a different scale from the rest.
- Choose a smaller Sun or a longer path. ✓ Feedback: Yes. Change the whole scale, or find more room.
- Move only the far planets closer. *Tag: `mixed-scale`.* Feedback: Then the gaps aren't to scale. One ratio for everything.
Hint: What does "to scale" mean? Explanation: A scale model shrinks everything by the same amount. If it doesn't fit, pick a new scale for all of it.

**D5 · Seedling · number with steps**
Given: light goes about 300,000 km each second. The Sun is about 150 million km away.
How long does sunlight take to reach us?
Answer: 500 seconds, which is 8 minutes 20 seconds.

## Key terms

- **Axis:** the imaginary line Earth spins around.
- **Spin:** turning on the spot. Earth spins once in about a day.
- **Orbit:** a path around something. Earth orbits the Sun once in a year.
- **Tilt:** a lean. Earth's axis leans the same way all year.
- **Season:** part of the year with its own weather and daylight.
- **Phase:** the Moon's shape as we see it from Earth.
- **Eclipse:** when a shadow falls on the Moon, or the Moon hides the Sun.
- **Planet:** a big round world that goes around the Sun.
- **Dwarf planet:** a smaller round world, like Pluto.
- **Star:** a huge ball of hot gas that makes its own light.
- **Scale model:** a model with everything shrunk by the same amount.
- **Telescope:** a tube with lenses that makes faraway things look bigger.
- **Constellation:** a pattern of stars people have named.
- **Rover:** a robot car that explores another world.
- **Reconstruction:** a picture made later to show what something was probably like.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** day and night from Earth's spin; the year from its orbit; seasons from the tilt of Earth's axis (and why distance is the wrong explanation); Moon phases from a half-lit Moon, with eclipses as the rare different case; the eight planets in order; the scale of the solar system; the Sun as our nearest star; light travel time; and how Galileo's telescope observations changed ideas about the sky.

**What it does not claim:** benches and the walk are simplified models, labeled on screen. The Galileo notebook is a modern computer reconstruction, not his drawings. Star stories are stories. Nothing here tells you what the sky will show on a given night, and nothing is a test.

**Sources in brief:** NASA's planetary fact sheets for sizes and distances; the PyEphem astronomy library for dates and positions; Marble's teaching research for the teaching approach. History facts come from museum, NASA and encyclopedia sources. Full list in the source audit.

**Off-screen activity (adult-led):** a sky night on a clear evening. Choose a safe, dark spot on the ground away from roads and water: a garden, a park or a field you know, never a roof. Dress warmly. Let eyes adjust for a while without screens. Look for the Moon (Dot's Field Guide can keep notes), Orion on winter evenings, the Big Dipper, and a bright steady dot that might be a planet.

**Off-screen Scale Walk (adult-led):** use a ball for the Sun and beads or seeds for planets, and pace out the 10 cm Sun column of the scale table along a quiet path or field (about 325 m to Neptune). Keep away from roads and water.

**Sun safety:** never look at the Sun, and never point binoculars, a telescope or a camera viewfinder at it. Sunglasses are not safe for looking at the Sun.
