### Teach cards (A true thing about language)

Cards 10, 11, 12, 13, 14 and 21 exactly as 263, with their worked examples revealed one step per tap:

**Card 10. -ly tells how**
> -ly turns a describing word into a word that tells how or how often: fresh, freshly; neat, neatly. It starts with a consonant, so usually just add it.

Worked example: *usual + -ly*. 1. Usual ends in l. 2. -ly starts with l. Nothing changes. 3. Usually, with two l's.

**Card 11. When -ly changes the word**
> A word ending in a consonant and y usually changes y to i: happy, happily. A word ending in a consonant and le usually swaps le for ly: gentle, gently.

Worked example: *simple + -ly*. 1. Simple ends in p, then le. 2. Swap le for ly. 3. Simply.

**Card 12. -ous**
> -ous makes a describing word: danger, dangerous. A final e usually drops: fame, famous. Y after a consonant usually becomes i: glory, glorious. Some have no obvious root word here.

Worked example: *glory + -ous*. 1. Glory ends in r, then y. 2. Change y to i. 3. Glorious.

**Card 13. -ation**
> -ation turns a doing word into a naming word: inform, information. It starts with a vowel letter, so a final e usually drops first: invite, invitation.

Worked example: *prepare + -ation*. 1. Prepare ends in r, then e. 2. -ation starts with a vowel letter. Drop the e. 3. Preparation.

**Card 14. Spellings to learn**
> Some spellings are just learned. Gym and myth have y. Young and touch have ou. Treasure and measure end -sure. Picture and nature end -ture.

Worked example: *BURIED TREA____*. 1. Treasure is in the measure family. 2. That family ends -sure. 3. Treasure.

**Card 21. -ic words (A harder tool)**
> Words ending in -ic usually add -ally, not just -ly: dramatic, dramatically; basic, basically. Publicly is the odd one out.

Worked example: *basic + -ly*. 1. Basic ends in -ic. 2. Add -ally. 3. Basically.

Grown-up text: **This bench explores three endings, -ly, -ous and -ation, and the small changes a root word can make when one is added: dropping an e before a vowel letter, changing y to i after a consonant, swapping le for ly, and -ic adding -ally. It also shows a few spellings that are learned in families (-sure, -ture, ou, y). These are usual patterns, not laws, and the odd-ones shelf shows words that keep their own way. All checks are guided practice with supplied words, not independent spelling assessment. An unexpected combination may still be a real word; check a bigger dictionary together. No sound samples play here. The English curriculum also names -sion (television, division); it is told apart from -tion by sound, so it is not taught on screen here. British spelling writes humour, which drops its u before -ous (humorous); US humor needs no change. No story chapter is unlocked.**
