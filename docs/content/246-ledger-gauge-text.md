**Copied source note:**

> “Years not written had no flood worth the ink.”

**What to notice:**

> This is a selected record, not a list of every flood. Hollis does not say how a flood became “worth the ink” or whether that choice stayed the same. An unwritten year is not a height of zero. The extract also does not tell us whether the post, measuring method, or meaning of ordinary level stayed unchanged throughout the record.

### Source Y: Hollis's year 22 note

**Label:** Two changes in one year · fictional source

**Copied source sentence:**

> “Year 22. The Sponge is drained and the brook cut straight past the new fields.”

**Original English summary:**

> Hollis places two changes in year 22: draining the Sponge and straightening the brook. The Sponge was a marsh where some orchard fields now stand. This note records when the changes happened. It does not measure the separate effect of either change on later floods.

**Limit to carry with it:**

> A comparison across year 22 includes both changes, along with anything else that differed between those years. It cannot separate drainage from straightening by itself.

## Shared teaching prose

### Reading the ledger plot

> The bottom line marks Hollis's years. A gap of ten years takes the same space wherever it appears. Each bar is one recorded flood, placed at its year. The height scale is in centimeters above the stated ordinary level. Read the labelled scale to compare bar heights.
>
> The year 22 marker shows when the two changes were recorded. A year without a bar means no flood was entered here. It does not mean the water height was zero.
>
> The later 160 cm storm has its own labelled place beside the plot. It has no supplied Hollis year, so it does not sit on the year line.

### Mean and range

> A **mean** shares a total equally among its values. Add the values, then divide by how many values there are. A repeated value still counts each time it appears.
>
> Imagine three marker strips with heights 30, 50 and 70. Moving pieces between the strips until they are level gives three strips of 50. This models the arithmetic of heights. It does not model moving real floodwater.
>
> The same calculation is (30 + 50 + 70) ÷ 3 = 150 ÷ 3 = 50.
>
> The **range** tells us the distance from the lowest recorded value to the highest: highest minus lowest. It is one way to describe the spread. It does not describe every detail of the group.

**Applied comparison:**

> Before year 22, the ledger has two heights: 40 and 60 cm. Their total is 100 cm. Their mean is exactly 100 ÷ 2 = 50 cm. The lowest is 40 cm, the highest is 60 cm, and the range is 20 cm.
>
> From year 22 through year 40, the ledger has five heights: 120, 80, 120, 140 and 100 cm. Count both entries of 120. Their total is 560 cm. Their mean is exactly 560 ÷ 5 = 112 cm. The lowest is 80 cm, the highest is 140 cm, and the range is 60 cm.
>
> The later group's recorded mean and maximum are higher. The difference between the recorded means is 112 − 50 = 62 cm. These calculations describe the entries we have. They do not estimate how much a particular change caused the water to rise.

### Exact calculation and uncertain reach

> We can be certain that 40 and 60 have a mean of 50. A different question is whether those two selected entries describe all earlier floods well. Hollis may have left smaller floods unwritten, and the recording rule is unknown. One more entry could change the mean. More entries alone would not fix an unknown selection rule.

### Counts and lengths of time

> Before year 22 means years 1 through 21: 21 years. The later period means years 22 through 40: 19 years. There are two recorded floods in the first period and five in the second.
>
> The recorded count more than doubled. Because the periods have different lengths, give both counts and both lengths when you compare them: “2 recorded floods in 21 years, then 5 in 19 years.” That lets a reader see what was counted.

### Ten-year groups

> A ten-year group includes both of its named end years. Years 1 to 10 includes year 4. Years 11 to 20 includes year 15. Years 21 to 30 includes year 27. Years 31 to 40 includes years 31, 34, 36 and 38.
>
> To find a group's highest recorded flood, compare only the ledger entries inside that group. Do not enter zero for an unwritten year or add the separate later storm.

| Years | 1 to 10 | 11 to 20 | 21 to 30 | 31 to 40 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Recorded big-rain events | 6 | 5 | 6 | 8 |
| Recorded floods | 1 | 1 | 1 | 4 |
| Highest recorded flood (cm) | 40 | 60 | 120 | 140 |

### What an aligned table can do

> Years 1 to 10 and years 21 to 30 each have six recorded big-rain events and one recorded flood. The recorded flood heights are 40 and 120 cm. The second recorded rise above ordinary level is three times the first. That does not mean three times the water, damage, or danger.
>
> The count of six does not hold rainfall constant. We do not know the amounts, durations, or threshold, and the tally comes from Vadoalto. Years 21 to 30 also straddles year 22. It is not a group made entirely of years after the changes.
>
> Years 31 to 40 has the largest recorded big-rain count, eight, and the largest recorded flood count, four. These quantities occur together in this table. The table does not show that one caused the other.

### Weighing claims

> A claim is a sentence someone asks us to accept. Look for a number or a source phrase that bears on it.
>
> **The records support this:** the record gives evidence for the sentence as written.
>
> **The records count against this:** an entry or source phrase gives evidence against the sentence as written.
>
> **The records cannot tell:** the needed information is missing, or more than one explanation still fits.
>
> You can try any tray and read the reply. There is no score. The replies do not make every reading equally supported: they show what evidence fits, what conflicts, and what remains open.
>
> A pattern after year 22 can suggest a question about causes. It cannot separate two changes made in the same year. Rainfall, recording choices, and other differences may matter too. Uncertainty about cause is different from having no useful observations.

## Nine claim cards and all 27 tray replies

The direction notes below are for authors and reviewers, not learner badges or scores. Replies are learner-facing. Card 1 keeps the existing higher-after claim but narrows its displayed wording to recorded mean and maximum.

### C1. “The recorded mean and maximum flood heights were higher after year 22.”

Author direction: records support.

**Records support:**

> Hollis's two earlier entries have mean 50 cm and maximum 60 cm. The five later entries have mean 112 cm and maximum 140 cm. Both summaries are higher. This describes selected entries, not the cause of the difference or every flood that happened.

**Records count against:**

> Compare the summaries in the ledger: 50 to 112 cm for the means, and 60 to 140 cm for the maxima. Those numbers support this sentence. Hollis's missing entries limit what we can say about all floods, but they do not reverse this comparison of the recorded groups.

**Records cannot tell:**

> We can calculate this comparison from the entries: mean 50 cm and maximum 60 cm before, mean 112 cm and maximum 140 cm after. What we cannot tell is how well the selected record describes all floods or what caused the difference.

### C2. “The last ten years had more recorded big-rain events at Vadoalto.”

Author direction: records support.

**Records support:**

> Nuria's copied tally gives eight events in years 31 to 40, compared with six, five and six in the other ten-year groups. It supports a larger recorded event count, not a larger measured rainfall total.

**Records count against:**

> The last tally is eight; the earlier tallies are six, five and six. Those counts support the sentence. The missing big-rain threshold limits what the counts mean, but there is no larger count elsewhere in this extract.

**Records cannot tell:**

> The tally does let us compare its recorded counts: eight is greater than six, five and six. It cannot tell us the rainfall amounts or whether the same threshold was used throughout. Those are different claims from this one.

### C3. “Rain alone explains the higher floods.”

Author direction: records cannot tell.

**Records support:**

> Years 31 to 40 has eight big-rain events and four recorded floods. That gives a pattern to investigate, but it does not establish “rain alone.” The copy lacks rainfall amounts and durations, and Hollis records drainage and straightening in year 22.

**Records count against:**

> The two groups with six big-rain events have recorded flood heights of 40 and 120 cm. That challenges an argument based only on event counts. It does not rule out rain alone, because six events could contain different amounts or durations of rain. We cannot settle the claim here.

**Records cannot tell:**

> The tally has counts of six, five, six and eight, but no amounts or durations. Hollis also records two changes in year 22. These sources do not establish or rule out rain as the sole explanation of the higher recorded mean and maximum.

### C4. “Draining the Sponge was the only cause.”

Author direction: records cannot tell.

**Records support:**

> The ledger's mean rises from 50 to 112 cm across year 22, when the Sponge was drained. But Hollis's note also says the brook was cut straight that year, and the rain copy lacks amounts. The timing does not establish drainage as the only cause.

**Records count against:**

> Hollis names straightening as well as drainage in year 22. That gives us another change to investigate, not proof that straightening caused the difference. These records cannot confirm “only,” and they cannot rule it out by measuring separate effects either.

**Records cannot tell:**

> Hollis's year 22 note places drainage and straightening together. Neither source separates their effects, and Nuria's event counts do not hold rainfall constant. “Only cause” goes beyond what the records can decide.

### C5. “Draining the Sponge made floods worse.”

Author direction: records cannot tell.

**Records support:**

> The recorded mean is 50 cm before year 22 and 112 cm after. That makes drainage worth investigating. It does not show that drainage caused the rise: straightening happened in the same year, rainfall details are missing, and Hollis selected which floods to record.

**Records count against:**

> The ledger does not show lower later summaries: its mean rises from 50 to 112 cm and its maximum from 60 to 140 cm. But those rises do not measure drainage's effect. The records leave this causal claim open rather than showing it false.

**Records cannot tell:**

> Hollis records higher later mean and maximum heights, and drainage in year 22. Straightening happened then too, and the rainfall and recording details are incomplete. We can describe the pattern without deciding whether drainage made the floods worse.

### C6. “Straightening the brook made floods worse.”

Author direction: records cannot tell.

**Records support:**

> Hollis's note says the brook was cut straight in year 22, before the five later recorded floods. Their mean is 112 cm against 50 cm earlier. The same note records drainage too. The sources do not isolate straightening's effect.

**Records count against:**

> The ledger's later mean and maximum are higher, so it does not supply a simple lower-after pattern against this claim. Yet the numbers do not measure the effect of straightening. With drainage in the same year and rain details missing, the claim remains unresolved.

**Records cannot tell:**

> Straightening and drainage share year 22 in Hollis's note. We have no separate comparison for one change without the other, and the rain tally gives no amounts. These records cannot identify whether straightening made floods worse.

### C7. “There were no small floods before year 22.”

Author direction: records cannot tell.

**Records support:**

> Hollis lists 40 and 60 cm before year 22, but also writes “no flood worth the ink” about unwritten years. There is no definition of “small” or a complete list of smaller events. Two entries cannot establish that no small floods happened.

**Records count against:**

> Hollis's phrase “worth the ink” allows for floods left unwritten, but it does not identify any small flood that happened. We should not invent one. Without a definition of “small” or more entries, the record neither establishes this absence nor directly disproves it.

**Records cannot tell:**

> The earlier recorded heights are 40 and 60 cm. Hollis gives no small-flood definition and says some years had no flood “worth the ink.” Small events could have gone unwritten. We cannot turn that possibility into either a known event or a known absence.

### C8. “The storm will happen again next year.”

Author direction: records cannot tell.

**Records support:**

> The separate town note records a later storm at 160 cm. One recorded storm is evidence that it happened, not a schedule for another. The note supplies no Hollis-year coordinate or forecast for next year.

**Records count against:**

> Neither Hollis's seven entries nor the separate 160 cm note says what next year will bring. They do not promise a repeat, but they do not show a repeat is impossible. This prediction remains beyond the supplied records.

**Records cannot tell:**

> The later note gives 160 cm for a storm that happened. The ledger gives seven earlier dated entries. Neither source predicts next year, so these records cannot decide whether a storm like it will happen then.

### C9. “No recorded flood after year 22 exceeded 60 cm.”

Author direction: records count against.

**Records support:**

> Look at year 27: Hollis records 120 cm, which exceeds 60 cm. The other later entries are 80, 120, 140 and 100 cm, also above 60. These entries count against the sentence even without the separate later storm.

**Records count against:**

> Year 27 records 120 cm, already enough to contradict “no recorded flood.” The five later ledger heights are 120, 80, 120, 140 and 100 cm. All exceed 60 cm. This is about the recorded entries, not a claim that the ledger contains every flood.

**Records cannot tell:**

> We do have the entries needed for this claim: 120 cm in year 27 is greater than 60 cm. One such recorded flood contradicts “no recorded flood.” Unknown unrecorded floods do not remove the contradiction in the record we can read.

### Claim-page optional help

**Hint:**

## Five-part evidence note

### Writing invitation

> Write a short note for Wren about what these records show and what you would investigate next. You may include a provisional plan. It stays in this case and can be revised. It does not put a plan on the town's meeting board.

### Writing teaching card

> Give a reader a route from your claim to your evidence. Name the source and point to a number or phrase. Then explain why it matters.
>
> A plan recommendation also contains a judgement about what to value. The records can inform that judgement without proving a plan will work. Call it provisional if you may revise it.
>
> An objection is a serious reason someone could give against your recommendation. Answer it fairly. You can agree with part of it, say what is still missing, or change your recommendation.
>
> State an uncertainty precisely. “The rain amounts are missing” tells us more than “we know nothing.” Propose a new record that addresses that gap, and say what it would still leave open.

### Choice-and-adapt introduction

> Write your own words, or choose a complete sentence and adapt it. Supplied words are help you used, even after you edit them. There is no writing score.

### Part 3: An objection and your answer

Prompt:

> What is a serious objection to your provisional choice? Give the objection and an answer that takes it seriously.

These are nine complete supplied objection-and-answer options, three for each plan. Each is stored and shown with its named plan. The quoted downstream question is an existing fictional line; other objections are original authored case questions, not attributed source quotations.

**Wall objection A: where the wall ends**

> Objection: “And where does the water go when it reaches the end of your wall?” My answer: This is a fair question. Hollis's ledger measures water at one post, not the effect of a proposed wall elsewhere. I would keep the Wall provisional and ask for evidence about the rest of the brook before settling it.

**Wall objection B: choosing a height**

> Objection: The records do not tell us how high to build the wall. My answer: I agree. The later recorded maximum of 140 cm is a past entry, not a guaranteed future limit or a design instruction. I can prefer the Wall's aim while asking for the missing evidence about the proposal.

**Wall objection C: choosing before counting costs**

> Objection: Keeping the orchard does not tell us what building this wall would cost. My answer: That cost is missing from this case. I would request a clear account of the work and resources before settling the choice. Preserving the orchard is my reason for considering the Wall, not a reason to ignore its cost.

**Sponge objection A: losing trees**

> Objection: The proposal removes some trees, but these records do not prove the benefit. My answer: I agree that the tree loss is a serious cost. The later heights do not isolate drainage's effect. I would keep the Sponge provisional, investigate its proposed water effects, and ask how the loss would be addressed before settling it.

**Sponge objection B: the reed trade**

> Objection: A proposed reed trade may not replace what the lost orchard provides. My answer: The ledger and rain tally cannot answer that. I would ask for a separate account of the orchard's contribution and what the trade could provide. I should not treat a named trade as proof that nobody bears a cost.

**Sponge objection C: two simultaneous changes**

> Objection: The brook was straightened in year 22 too, so why focus on the Sponge? My answer: That is a limit on my evidence. I prefer investigating the Sponge's aim, but I cannot claim the ledger proved drainage caused the higher heights. I would compare evidence about both changes and revise the recommendation if it counted against my reason.

**Some of Each objection A: enough**

> Objection: Will a lower wall and smaller wet meadow be enough? My answer: These records cannot tell us. I would keep the combination provisional and ask for a direct comparison of the three proposals. A compromise between two ideas is not evidence that the combined plan meets its aim.

**Some of Each objection B: paying both costs**

> Objection: This choice still asks for wall building and the loss of some orchard land. My answer: I accept that it carries both kinds of cost. I would ask for a clear account of each rather than call the combination a free middle choice. My reason is to investigate the two aims together, not to pretend their costs disappear.

**Some of Each objection C: splitting effort**

> Objection: Making each part smaller could leave us with two measures whose effects we do not understand. My answer: That is a reason to investigate the combination itself. Neither Hollis's heights nor Nuria's counts test the proposed sizes or their joint effect. I would compare the proposals before settling this preference, and change it if the evidence counted against it.

Optional help:

## Fifteen checked questions: exact choices and replies

The specification supplies the numeric fields, expected values, hint and different example for each ID. The order below is the authored order, frozen with the source. Numeric units are given in the labels. These are supported comparisons with the supplied records, not independent-performance or reading-level judgments.

### Numeric feedback

| ID | Match reply | Different reply |
| --- | --- | --- |
| entry-counts | Two entries are in years 1–21: 4 and 15. Five are in years 22–40: 27, 31, 34, 36, 38. These are recorded entries, not a complete flood count. | Count the written years on each side of 22. Years 4 and 15 are before; 27, 31, 34, 36, 38 are in the later period. An unwritten year is not another flood entry. |
| mean-before | 40 + 60 = 100 cm in total; 100 ÷ 2 = 50 cm. That is the exact mean of these two entries. | Add the two written heights, 40 and 60, then divide their 100 cm total by 2 entries. Dividing by 21 years answers a different question. |
| mean-after | 120 + 80 + 120 + 140 + 100 = 560 cm; 560 ÷ 5 = 112 cm. Both 120 cm entries count. The separate storm is excluded. | Use the five dated later entries. Add both entries of 120 cm, then 80, 140 and 100 cm, then divide their 560 cm total by 5 entries. The later storm is a separate note. |
| ranges | The earlier range is 60 − 40 = 20 cm. The later range is 140 − 80 = 60 cm. These describe the two recorded groups. | Use highest minus lowest within each group. Earlier: 60 − 40. Later: 140 − 80. A maximum alone is not a range. |
| mean-difference | The two recorded means are 112 and 50 cm. Their difference is 112 − 50 = 62 cm; this is not a measured causal effect. | Subtract the earlier mean 50 from the later mean 112. Reporting either mean alone does not give the difference. |
| period-one | Year 4 is the only entry in years 1–10. This group has 1 recorded entry and maximum 40 cm. | Use only years 1–10. The ledger entry at year 4 has height 40 cm. Unwritten years add no known heights. |
| period-two | Year 15 is the only entry in years 11–20. This group has 1 recorded entry and maximum 60 cm. | Use only years 11–20. Year 15 belongs here; year 4 and year 27 do not. The one supplied height is 60 cm. |
| period-three | Year 27 is the only entry in years 21–30. This group has 1 recorded entry and maximum 120 cm. The interval straddles year 22. | Use all of 21–30, not just the years before 22. The only written flood here is year 27 at 120 cm. |
| period-four | Years 31, 34, 36, 38 give 4 entries. Their heights 80, 120, 140, 100 cm have maximum 140 cm. | Count 31, 34, 36, 38 and compare 80, 120, 140, 100 cm. Year 27 belongs to the preceding interval, and the separate storm has no supplied Hollis year. |

For after-mean 120, an additional reply may say: “120 is the middle and most frequent later height. Including the separate 160 cm storm would also give a different six-entry mean of 120. We cannot tell how you found it. The question uses five dated ledger entries.” For 140: “140 is the largest later height. The mean shares the total across all five entries.” For 560: “560 is the total of the five heights. Divide that total by five to find their mean.” These are possible numerical explanations, not diagnoses of the learner's method.

### count-periods

Prompt: **Which sentence keeps the counts and time periods clear?**

1. “Comparing 5 with 2 alone gives the change in floods per year.” Reply: “5 and 2 are counts. Their periods contain 19 and 21 years. A count-per-year comparison must also use those lengths.”
2. “There are 2 recorded entries in 21 years, then 5 in 19 years.” Reply: “This gives both counts and both period lengths. A reader can see the comparison without pretending every flood was recorded.” **Fits.**
3. “Different period lengths prevent any useful count comparison.” Reply: “We can still report 2 in 21 years and 5 in 19 years. Naming the lengths makes the limitation visible instead of discarding the counts.”

### mean-limit

Prompt: **What does the earlier 50 cm mean establish?**

1. “It is the mean of every flood in those 21 years.” Reply: “Hollis did not record every flood. 50 cm is the mean of 40 and 60, not a known mean of all earlier floods.”
2. “Nothing numerical can be said about just two recorded values.” Reply: “We can calculate the mean of these values: 100 ÷ 2 = 50 cm. The limit concerns generalising beyond them.”
3. “It is the exact mean of the two written heights.” Reply: “40 and 60 have total 100; 100 ÷ 2 = 50 cm. The arithmetic is exact for those entries, while unwritten floods remain unknown.” **Fits.**

### unwritten-years

Prompt: **What height belongs to an unwritten year?**

1. “The height is not known from this ledger.” Reply: “The note says no flood was worth the ink. It gives no fixed threshold or height for an unwritten year.” **Fits.**
2. “Use 0 cm because there is no entry.” Reply: “No entry is missing information, not a measurement of 0 cm. Hollis's selection note does not give that height.”
3. “Use the nearest written year's height.” Reply: “A neighbouring entry does not measure this year. No interpolation rule or observation supplies its height.”

### equal-tallies

Prompt: **Which periods have the same recorded big-rain count?**

1. “Years 11–20 and 31–40.” Reply: “Those counts are 5 and 8. They differ; compare the event-count row rather than the flood-height row.”
2. “Years 1–10 and 21–30.” Reply: “Both copied tallies are 6. Equal counts do not establish equal rainfall amounts or duration.” **Fits.**
3. “Years 11–20 and 21–30.” Reply: “Those counts are 5 and 6. The two periods with 6 are 1–10 and 21–30.”

### recorded-changes

Prompt: **Which changes does Hollis explicitly record at year 22?**

1. “The two periods had equal rainfall totals.” Reply: “The tally gives event counts, not rainfall totals. Neither record says those totals were equal.”
2. “The Sponge was drained; the brook otherwise stayed unchanged.” Reply: “The same sentence records that the brook was cut straight. Omitting it hides a simultaneous change.”
3. “The Sponge was drained and the brook was straightened.” Reply: “The year 22 sentence records both changes. It does not separate either one's effect on the later heights.” **Fits.**

### straddling-period

Prompt: **Where does period 21–30 sit beside the change at 22?**

1. “It includes years on both sides of 22.” Reply: “Year 21 is before 22; 22 through 30 belong to the later period. The whole interval cannot be called wholly before or wholly after.” **Fits.**
2. “Every year in it is after the change.” Reply: “Year 21 is in the interval and comes before 22. The decade total cannot be treated as an entirely later rain total.”
3. “Every year in it is before the change.” Reply: “Years 22 through 30 are also in this interval. It spans the boundary rather than ending before it.”

The six fitting positions are 2, 3, 1, 2, 3, 1. Do not shuffle them on render or resume. Each source may order the complete choices differently when authored; its original order remains authoritative.
