## Story scenes

### Scene 1. The show book (Act II, ch. 4, the first show)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** The Story Loft's first show for the town is tomorrow. Dot copied the whole script into a show book. She left out every mark.

**Short:**
> Dot's show book has no marks. Pip reads it aloud and muddles everything. Quotation marks and commas show who says what.

**Longer:**
> The show book is lovely. Dot copied every line in her neatest hand.
>
> Pip reads the first page aloud. "Let's eat Pip said Dot."
>
> He stops. "Who is eating me?"
>
> "Nobody," says Dot. "I'm telling you it's lunchtime."
>
> "Then the book needs a comma," says {guide}. "And marks around the words each puppet says."
>
> Pip turns the page. "Before the show Pip eats a sandwich," he reads. "That part is true."
>
> "Where do my words start?" asks Dot. "And where do they stop?"
>
> You look at the page. The words are all there. The marks are not.
>
> "Marks are like stage directions for a reader's voice," says {guide}.
>
> Pip sits down beside you. "Then let's fix it. After lunch."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> A play script puts the speaker's name first and needs no quotation marks. A story needs them, because the speaker's name can come anywhere. Dot's show book is written like a story.

### Scene 2. The curtain call (Act II, ch. 4)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** The show is over. Everyone claps. At the curtain call, {guide} asks Dot a question. It belongs on the show book's last page.

**Short:**
> At the curtain call, {guide} asks, "Dot, will you stay?" Dot says she already unpacked. You write it on the last page.

**Longer:**
> The puppets bow. Puff claps so hard that a cushion falls off the bench.
>
> Dot steps out from behind the curtain. Her hat is on sideways.
>
> "Dot, will you stay?" asks {guide}. "In Willowbank, I mean. For good."
>
> Dot straightens her hat. "I already unpacked," she says.
>
> Pip cheers louder than anyone. Then he cheers again, in case anyone missed it.
>
> "That goes on the last page," says Dot. "With all the right marks."
>
> She hands you the pen.

### Scene 3. Letters up and down the river (Act III, ch. 4)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** Two letters arrive, one from Nuria upstream and one from Léon downstream. The town's reply will quote their exact words.

**Short:**
> To copy someone's exact words, you use quotation marks. To tell what they said in your own words, you don't.

**Longer:**
> Two letters arrive on the same day. One is from Nuria in Vadoalto. One is from Léon in Les Saules, and it is slightly damp.
>
> Nuria's letter quotes her grandmother: *Mi abuela dice: «Las cañas crecen bien en el barro».* Underneath, in English: *My grandmother says, "Reeds grow well in mud."*
>
> Léon's letter quotes the whole town: *Ici, tout le monde me dit : « Léon, tu es encore mouillé ! »* Underneath, in English: *Everyone here tells me, "Léon, you are still wet!"*
>
> "Their marks look like little arrows," says Pip.
>
> "Lots of Spanish and French writers use those," says {guide}. "Same job. Exact words go inside."
>
> Dot starts the town's reply. "Nuria's grandmother says reeds like mud," she says. "That's telling it my way. No marks."
>
> "And if we copy the exact words?" asks Pip.
>
> "Then they go inside quotation marks," says Dot. "Every word, just as it was written."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> Spanish and French printers often use angle marks, « », for quoted words. French puts a small space inside them. Spanish puts the period after the closing mark; American English puts it inside. The job is the same: someone's exact words.

*(Fluent-reader flag: the Spanish and French lines above need a fluent reader before build.)*

### Scene 4. The summer program (Summer, Market Fair and the Show)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** The summer show needs a program for the market board. The librarian wants it clear and impossible to misread.

**Short:**
> The program has extra facts in brackets. One missing hyphen makes a sock chase the miller's cat. Bramble spots it.

**Longer:**
> The librarian pins the program to the market board.
>
> THE SUMMER SHOW (with a break for cake). Starring the miller's sock chasing cat.
>
> Bramble reads it over your shoulder. Her magpie tilts its head.
>
> "A sock chasing a cat?" says Bramble. "I'd pay to see that."
>
> "It's the cat that chases socks," says the miller.
>
> "Then it needs a hyphen," says Bramble. "Sock-chasing. Two words doing one job."
>
> The librarian sighs and fetches a pen. "Anything else?"
>
> Bramble studies the brackets. "Those are fine," she says. "Cake is very important extra information."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> Twenty-odd puppets means a few more than twenty. Twenty odd puppets means twenty strange ones. The Story Loft has both.

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer; worked-example steps appear one tap at a time. Every card is **A true thing about language**.

**Card 1. Exact words go inside**
> Quotation marks, also called speech marks, go around the exact words someone says. The words outside tell who said them.

Worked example: *Pip is hungry.*
1. What did Pip say? I'm hungry.
2. Put marks around just those words.
3. Pip said, "I'm hungry."

**Card 2. A mark before the closing marks**
> When the speaker comes after the words, end the spoken part with a comma, a question mark or an exclamation mark. It goes inside the closing quotation marks.

Worked example: *Dot starts the show.*
1. Spoken words: Let's begin.
2. Then the speaker: said Dot.
3. "Let's begin," said Dot.

**Card 3. Speaker first**
> When the speaker comes first, put a comma after the speaker. The spoken words start with a capital letter.

Worked example: *Dot calls the cast.*
1. Speaker: Dot called
2. Comma, then the marks open.
3. Dot called, "Places, everyone!"

**Card 4. Words split in two**
> Sometimes the speaker sits in the middle. Close the marks, name the speaker, then open them again. If the sentence goes on, the second part starts with a small letter.

Worked example: *Dot gives a cue.*
1. First part: When the bell rings
2. Speaker: said Dot
3. "When the bell rings," said Dot, "open the curtain."

**Card 5. New speaker, new line**
> Each time a new puppet speaks, start a new line. Readers can then tell who is talking, even without names.

Worked example: *Pip and Dot.*
1. "Is it lunchtime?" asked Pip.
2. New speaker, new line.
3. "Not yet," said Dot.

**Card 6. Names, yes, no and tags**
> Use a comma to set off the name of someone you talk to. Use one after yes or no, and before a tag like isn't it.

Worked example: *Three short lines.*
1. Let's eat, Pip.
2. Yes, the show starts at six.
3. It's a good show, isn't it?

**Card 7. Openers take a comma**
> An opener tells when, where or how. Move it to the front of a sentence, and usually put a comma after it. A front opener is called a fronted adverbial.

Worked example: *Pip ate a sandwich before the show.*
1. Find the when part: before the show.
2. Move it to the front.
3. Before the show, Pip ate a sandwich.

**Card 8. Commas stop misreading**
> Sometimes a reader joins words that should stay apart. A comma tells the reader to pause there, so the sentence means what you meant.

Worked example: *While Dot painted the puppet watched.*
1. A reader thinks Dot painted the puppet.
2. But the puppet was watching.
3. While Dot painted, the puppet watched.

**Card 9. Extra information**
> Extra information can sit inside a sentence between brackets, dashes or commas. Take it out, and the sentence still works.

Worked example: *Ember (the café's dragon) sat on a cushion.*
1. Extra part: the café's dragon.
2. Take it out: Ember sat on a cushion.
3. Still a whole sentence. The brackets were right.

**Card 10. Loud, quiet or in between**
> Dashes usually make extra information stand out. Brackets make it quiet, like a whisper. Commas sit in between.

Worked example: *The magpie – a terrible actor – stole the show.*
1. The writer wants the joke to stand out.
2. Dashes do that.
3. Brackets would make it a whisper.

**Card 11. Hyphens join words**
> A hyphen can join two words into one describing word before a noun. It can also join a prefix to a word, so it doesn't become another word.

Worked example: *Two pairs.*
1. A two-hour show: two and hour work together.
2. Recover means get better. Re-cover means cover again.
3. The hyphen changes the meaning.

## Benches

### (a) The Show Book (Sprout, Seedling, Sapling)

**Header:** Drag marks onto the show book's lines. Tap Read it to hear Pip read your line.

**Tile tray:** quotation marks · comma · period · question mark · exclamation mark

**Read it (Pip's voice):** Pip reads exactly the marks placed. With no marks, he reads every word in one flat line.

**Lines (authored, in order):**
1. Let's begin said Dot
2. Where is my hat asked Dot
3. Dot called Places everyone
4. When the bell rings said Dot open the curtain
5. Is it lunchtime asked Pip / Not yet said Dot
6. Dot will you stay asked {guide} / I already unpacked said Dot

**Sprout job:** Tap the words each puppet says. They light up.

**Feedback for common placements:**
- Comma placed after the closing marks: Tuck the comma inside the closing marks, next to the last spoken word.
- Period before said: A period ends the whole sentence. Use a comma, because said Dot still follows.
- Marks around said Dot: Said Dot is not spoken. Only the spoken words go inside.
- Line 5 joined on one line: Pip and Dot both speak. Give Dot her own line.
- Any placement not listed: Pip reads it just as it stands. Does it sound like the show?

### (b) Pip Reads It Wrong (Seedling, Sapling)

**Header:** Pip reads every line exactly as written. Add a comma so he reads it right.

**Lines and Pip's misreadings (A story from Willowbank):**
- Let's eat Pip. Pip's misreading: "Eat me? I'm a fox, not a sandwich!"
- Thank you Dot for the hat. Pip's misreading: "Thank you Dot? Is that her whole name?"
- Yes I already unpacked. Pip's misreading: "Yes I? Is that a new word?"
- It's a good show isn't it. Pip's misreading: "Is it, or isn't it? I can't tell."
- While Dot painted the puppet watched. Pip's misreading: "Dot painted the puppet? It looks the same."
- Inside the box was full of hats. Pip's misreading: "Inside the box was what?"

**After the right comma (Pip rereads):** Oh! Now I hear it. One little comma.

**Wrong spot (any):** Pip reads it again. Does that pause land in the right place?

### (c) Opener Cards (Seedling, Sapling)

**Header:** Drag the opener card to the front. Then add its comma.

**Cards:**
- Pip ate a sandwich [before the show].
- Dot opened the curtain [quietly].
- The puppets wake up [when the lights go down].
- [However] the show went on. (already at the front; add the comma)

**Feedback:**
- Opener moved, no comma: Good move. Now add a comma where the opener ends.
- Comma after the next word instead: Put the comma right where the opener ends.
- Opener left at the end: That works too. Openers at the end usually need no comma.

### (d) Whisper or Shout (Sapling, Keeper's Desk)

**Header:** Find the extra information. Put a pair of marks around it.

**Lines:**
- Ember the café's dragon sat on a cushion.
- Puff the café's quietest guest clapped the loudest.
- The magpie a terrible actor stole the show.

**Take it out:** Tap the extra part to hide it. Is the sentence still whole?

**Choose the volume:** Try brackets, dashes and commas on one line. Which fits what the writer wants?

**Feedback:**
- One mark only: Extra information needs a pair, one on each side.
- Marks swallow a needed word: Take it out. Is the sentence still whole?

### (e) The Hyphen Hook (Sapling, Keeper's Desk)

**Header:** Hook two words together, or leave them apart. Watch the picture change.

**Pairs (each picture changes as the hyphen goes on or off):**
- a sock-chasing cat · a sock chasing cat
- re-cover the chair · recover the chair
- re-sort the props · resort
- a small-puppet show · a small puppet show
- twenty-odd puppets · twenty odd puppets

**Picture captions:**
- Sock-chasing cat: The miller's cat chases a sock.
- Sock chasing cat: A sock chases the miller's cat. The cat looks surprised.
- Re-cover: Dot puts new cloth on the old chair.
- Recover: Dot gets the chair back. Someone had borrowed it.
- Re-sort: Pip sorts the props again.
- Resort: A place for a holiday. Not a pile of props.
- Small-puppet show: A show starring very small puppets.
- Small puppet show: A small show. The puppets can be any size.
- Twenty-odd puppets: A few more than twenty puppets.
- Twenty odd puppets: Twenty strange puppets.

## Problem sets

### Set A. Spoken words (Sprout, Seedling, Sapling)

- Hint: Which words did Pip actually say out loud?

**A3 (Seedling, tile).**

- Hint: Look at card 2.
- Explanation: Spoken words, comma, closing marks, then said Dot and a period.

**A4 (Seedling).**

- Hint: Which words make the question?

**A5 (Sapling, tile, guided).** Steps shown first: 1. Find the first spoken part. 2. Find said Dot. 3. Find the second spoken part.

- Line: When the bell rings said Dot open the curtain

- Hint: You need two pairs of quotation marks.
- Explanation: Close, name the speaker, comma, then open the marks again.

**A6 (Sapling).**
- Prompt: Pip speaks, then Dot answers. What does the writer do?
- Choices: Keep both speakers on one line (`one-paragraph`) · Leave out quotation marks for Dot (`answer-unmarked`) · Start a new line for Dot ✔
- Feedback: One line: Readers lose track of who is talking. · No marks: Dot's words are spoken too. They need marks.
- Hint: Look at card 5.
- Explanation: New speaker, new line. Readers can tell who speaks.

**A7 (Sapling).**
- Prompt: Which line gives Dot's exact words?
- Choices: Dot said the show was ready. (`reported-is-direct`) · Dot said, "The show is ready." ✔ · Dot said that the show was ready. (`reported-is-direct`)
- Feedback: Said the show was: That tells what Dot said in other words. · Said that: That is telling, not Dot's exact words.
- Hint: Which line could Dot say out loud, word for word?
- Explanation: Exact words get quotation marks. Telling in your own words does not.

## "I'm stuck" ladder

**Step 1, hint:** the item's own hint.

**Step 2, an easier case and how to start:**
- Spoken-word items: Try this one first: Pip said, "Hi." Hi is what he said. Now find the spoken words in yours.
- Name items: "Try this one first: Hi, Dot. Dot is being spoken to, so a comma sets her off. Now find the name in yours."
- Opener items: "Try this one first: Later, Pip slept. Later is the opener. Now find where yours ends."
- Misreading items: "Try this one first: read the line aloud with no pauses. Where does it go wrong? Pause there."
- Extra-information items: "Try this one first: Pip (a fox) yawned. Take out a fox. Pip yawned still works. Now find yours."
- Hyphen items: "Try this one first: a red-nosed puppet. Red and nosed describe it together. Now find your pair."

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Quotation marks:** marks that go around someone's exact words. Also called speech marks.
- **Speaker:** the one who says the words.
- **Direct speech:** someone's exact words, inside quotation marks.
- **Opener:** words at the front of a sentence, before the main part. Many tell when, where or how.
- **Fronted adverbial:** an opener moved to the front of a sentence.
- **Tag question:** a short question added at the end, like isn't it.
- **Extra information:** words you can take out and still have a whole sentence.
- **Brackets:** a pair of curved marks, ( ), around extra information.
- **Dash:** a short line between words. Here, dashes come in pairs around extra information.
- **Hyphen:** a very short line that joins words or word parts.
- **Prefix:** a word part added to the front of a word, like re-.
- **Misread:** to read something in a way the writer didn't mean.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** punctuating direct speech (Year 3 to 4 in England's national curriculum, grades 3 to 4 in the Common Core); commas after fronted adverbials and other introductory elements; commas with yes, no, tag questions and names (direct address); commas that prevent misreading; brackets, dashes and commas for parenthesis; and hyphens that avoid ambiguity.

**Terms:** in England, quotation marks are called inverted commas and often printed single (' '); a period is a full stop; the child text uses American terms and double marks with the comma and period inside. Spanish (per the Real Academia Española) puts the period after the closing « », and Spanish and French novels often mark dialogue with a dash instead of quotation marks; the pack shows « » only for quoted words in letters. "Fronted adverbial" is England's classroom term, taught here beside the plainer "opener." This pack says "brackets" for ( ), the word used across Learning Garden's packs; in the United States these are usually called parentheses.

**What it does not claim:** style guides differ at the edges. Many adult writers leave out the comma after a very short opener (the *Chicago Manual of Style* calls it optional unless misreading is likely), and American books often print a long dash with no spaces. The pack teaches the classroom convention: a comma after an opener is usual and never a mistake in these sentences, and items that need it say so. Colons and semicolons are taught in a later pack. Nothing the child places is graded or recorded beyond the saved bench choices.
