# Mills, Bridges and Pilgrims: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 137](../specs/137-mills-bridges-pilgrims.md). Use as written, after independent spec review and a subject check by a medieval historian. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion and is never gendered. `{courier}` is the explorer's courier. The miller is she/her; Bramble is a girl witch (she/her) with a magpie; the librarian is they/them; Pip is a fox (he/him). All real-history lines were source-checked on 2026-10-25 (see the [source audit](../source-audits/137-mills-bridges-pilgrims.md)); a subject reviewer still reads them before build. Nothing here is scored.

## Labels used everywhere

Every card carries one of these two labels, in the same place, in the same words:

- **A true thing from history.** (real places, people, records and objects, with a source line)
- **A story from Willowbank.** (the village, its people, the shell in the wall, the ribbons)

A third label is used only in the "Legend or history?" sort:

- **A legend.** (a story people told and still tell, which nobody has shown really happened)

Model cards carry: **A simplified picture.** One line says what it leaves out.

## The story scenes

### Scene 1. The shell in the mill wall (Spring Flood Boards; Act II or III)

**Label:** A story from Willowbank.

**Hook (30 words max):**
> The miller is fitting the spring flood boards. A loose stone comes out of the mill wall. Behind it: a small shell, carved in stone.

**Short (30 words max):**
> Behind a loose stone in the mill wall, the miller finds a carved stone shell. "Old," she says. "Older than me." She takes it to the library.

**Longer (150 words max):**
> It's Flood Boards morning. The miller is sliding the spring boards into the millrace when one of the old wall stones wobbles and slides out into her hands.
>
> Behind it is a hollow, and in the hollow is a shell carved from gray stone, ridge by ridge, like a fan.
>
> "Well," says the miller. Her cat sniffs it and is not impressed.
>
> Pip wants to know if it's for eating. It isn't.
>
> Bramble leans over the wall. "My grandmother said people walked the old ways with shells on their hats," she says. "Going somewhere far. They tied ribbons where the path turned, so the next walker wouldn't get lost."
>
> "Is that true?" asks {guide}.
>
> Bramble shrugs. "It's true that she said it."
>
> The miller wraps the shell in a flour sack. "The librarian will know where to look."

**Keeper's note (80 words max):**
> Bramble's grandmother's ribbons are a Willowbank story. Nobody has a record of them. The scallop shell is different: in real medieval Europe, the scallop was the badge of pilgrims going to the shrine of Saint James at Santiago de Compostela, in Spain. A carved shell in a wall proves nothing about who passed by. It is a clue that sends you to the real records.

### Scene 2. The librarian's shelf (any time after Scene 1)

**Label:** A story from Willowbank, with true things from history on the shelf.

**Short:**
> The librarian sets the shell beside a map. "Our valley is made up," they say. "But valleys like it were real. Let's read about them."

**Longer:**
> The librarian turns the stone shell over and over. "We can't know who carved this," they say. "Willowbank is our own place. No old book mentions it."
>
> They unroll a map of real mountains, the Pyrenees, with a road climbing over them.
>
> "But hundreds of years ago, real people walked over mountains like ours. Real millers ground real grain. A real king had every mill in his kingdom counted. Those things left records."
>
> Dot adjusts her hat. "So the shell is a story, and the shelf is history?"
>
> "The shell is a real thing in our story," says the librarian. "What it *means* is the question. The shelf can't tell us who put it in our wall. It can tell us what a shell like it meant to people long ago."

### Scene 3. The high ford (Act III, chapter 3, "The route by the high ford")

**Label:** A story from Willowbank.

**Short:**
> On the old path to Vadoalto, {courier} finds faded ribbons on a thorn tree, and a scratched shell shape on a stone.

**Longer:**
> The old path to Vadoalto climbs past the high ford, where the brook is wide and shallow enough to cross.
>
> On a thorn tree by the water there are scraps of faded ribbon. On a flat stone beside the path, someone long ago scratched a shell shape.
>
> {courier} counts the days on the map. "The pilgrims in the library book walked about a day's walk at a time," says {guide}. "Then they needed somewhere to sleep."
>
> "Like us," says Pip. "And somewhere to eat."
>
> Bramble ties a new ribbon beside the old ones, neatly, and doesn't say anything.
>
> Nobody knows if pilgrims ever came this way. The path is still a Way, and someone kept it open.

**Keeper's note:**
> A real pilgrim road crossed the Pyrenees at Roncesvalles (Roncevaux in French). Willowbank's high ford is fictional. The scene borrows a real idea: travelers on foot need stages, crossings and shelter.

## Reading 1. The mill and the village

All cards in this reading: **A true thing from history.** Each teach card is 30 words or fewer.

**1.1 When was this?**
> People in Europe call about 500 to 1500 CE the "Middle Ages." It's a European name. Other places count their history differently.

**1.2 The manor**
> Much farmland belonged to lords. A village and its fields, run for a lord, is called a manor. Peasants farmed strips of land there.

**1.3 Who owed what**
> Many peasants owed their lord work on his land, and payments. Some were not free to leave. In return they farmed land for themselves.

**1.4 Everyday food**
> Most people ate bread and pottage, a thick stew of grain, peas or vegetables. They drank ale. Grain was the heart of every meal.

**1.5 The lord's mill**
> Grain must be ground into flour. A watermill used the brook's push to turn heavy millstones. Grinding by water was much faster than grinding by hand.

*Mechanism note on this card:* How the wheel and gears turn the stones is in the mill's own workshop. This reading is about who used the mill and who paid.

**1.6 Suit of mill**
> On many manors, tenants had to grind their grain at the lord's mill. Grinding at home could mean a fine. This rule is called "suit of mill."

**1.7 The miller's share (multure)**
> The miller kept part of your grain as payment. This share was called multure. It changed with place and time, from about 1/30 to 1/10.

*Worked example (one step per tap):*
1. You bring 16 measures of grain.
2. The share here is 1/16. That's 1 measure in every 16.
3. 16 ÷ 16 = 1. The miller keeps 1 measure.
4. 16 − 1 = 15 measures go home with you, as flour.

**1.8 A king's book of lists**
> In 1085, King William I ordered a survey of England. His officials wrote down who held land and what it was worth. We call it Domesday Book.

**1.9 What they asked**
> Each place was asked: who holds it? How many plows, people, mills and fisheries? What is it worth? They asked about three different times.

*Details:* The three times were: in King Edward's days, when King William gave out the land, and now (1086). This is how we know the questions: a record from the Ely area lists them.

**1.10 Counting mills**
> Domesday Book records more than 5,600 mills, and by some counts over 6,000. Historians count differently, because some "mills" were shares of a mill.

**1.11 Why count mills?**
> A mill earned money for its owner. Counting mills helped the king know what each estate was worth, and who should pay.

**Guided item (steps shown before answering):**
- Prompt: The share is 1/16. You bring 48 measures. How many go home?
- Steps shown: "48 ÷ 16 = ?" · "48 − that = ?"
- Answer: 45.
- Feedback (right): Yes. The miller keeps 3, and 45 go home as flour.
- Feedback (wrong): Try the first step: 48 ÷ 16. That's the miller's share.

## Reading 2. The road over the mountains

All cards: **A true thing from history.**

**2.1 What a pilgrimage is**
> A pilgrimage is a journey to a holy place, made for religious reasons. Many medieval Christians walked to shrines far from home.

**2.2 Santiago de Compostela**
> Many pilgrims walked to Santiago de Compostela, in northwest Spain. They believed Saint James the apostle was buried there. Several routes led there.

**2.3 Over the Pyrenees**
> One famous route crossed the Pyrenees mountains from France into Spain, by the pass above Roncesvalles. The French name is Roncevaux. It is a steep climb.

*Keeper's note on this card:* Walkers today climb from about 200 meters to about 1,430 meters on the way to Roncesvalles. The twelfth-century guide names this crossing too: the pass of Cize.

**2.4 The scallop shell**
> The scallop shell became the badge of Saint James's pilgrims. Nobody is quite sure why. Pilgrims wore shells, and later metal shell badges, as souvenirs.

**2.5 Places to sleep**
> Along the road, hospitals and hospices gave pilgrims a bed, food and care. At Roncesvalles, a bishop of Pamplona set up a pilgrim hospital around 1127.

*Details:* Sources disagree a little: some say the work started in 1127 and others give 1132. An older hospice stood higher up, at the pass.

**2.6 Bridges for travelers**
> Rivers were dangerous to wade. In Navarre, a stone bridge over the River Arga was built in the 1000s for pilgrims. The town is called Puente la Reina.

*Details:* "Puente la Reina" means "the Queen's Bridge." Historians are not sure which queen ordered it. You can see six arches. Some guides say a seventh is buried under the town end.

**2.7 A guidebook for pilgrims**
> A book of about 1140 includes a guide for pilgrims. It lists the route in thirteen stages, from the mountain pass to Santiago.

*Details:* The big book is called the Codex Calixtinus. The guide is its fifth part, Book V. Many experts think a French monk named Aymeric Picaud wrote it, but nobody can be sure. The guide says some stages were ridden on horseback. The oldest copy that survives was made in 1173.

**2.8 A guide has opinions**
> The guide also says rude things about some people along the road. That tells you what its writer thought. It doesn't tell you what those people were like.

**2.9 How far in a day?**
> Walkers today often go 20 to 25 km in a day. People who tried medieval gear guessed 25 to 30 km. Both are estimates.

**Guided item:**
- Prompt: You walk 25 km a day. How many days for 75 km?
- Steps shown: "75 ÷ 25 = ?"
- Answer: 3 days.
- Feedback (right): Yes. Three days of 25 km each.
- Feedback (wrong): How many 25s fit in 75? Count 25, 50, 75.

## Reading 3. Towns, trade and who did the work

**3.1 Markets**
> A market was a weekly selling day. In England, the king granted the right to hold one. The lord who held it collected stall rents and tolls.

**3.2 Fairs**
> A fair was a bigger market held once a year, often for several days. Traders came from far away.

**3.3 Guilds**
> In towns, people in one trade often formed a guild. A guild set rules about prices and quality, and trained young apprentices.

**3.4 What was a castle for?**
> A castle was a strong home for a lord. It was also a center for running the lord's lands, holding courts, and defense.

**3.5 The pyramid picture**
> People often draw medieval society as a pyramid: a king, then lords, then knights, then peasants. Each owed something to the one above.

*Simplified picture:* Many historians say this pyramid is too neat. Real life was messier. Churches and monasteries held land too. Towns had merchants who didn't fit the pyramid. Some peasants were free.

**3.6 Who's who in the pyramid** (one card, three short lines)
> A **king** or queen ruled a kingdom. **Lords** held land from the king. **Knights** were trained fighters on horseback who served a lord, often in return for land.

**3.7 Alewives**
> In many English villages, women brewed ale and sold it. They were called brewsters or alewives. Records survive because ale prices were checked.

*Details:* Historians, especially Judith Bennett, found that women did much of England's brewing before about 1350. Over the next 250 years, men took over most of the trade.

**3.8 Hildegard of Bingen**
> Hildegard (about 1098 to 1179) was a German abbess, the leader of a community of nuns. She wrote music, books about plants and healing, and letters.

*Details:* She founded a convent at Rupertsberg, by the Rhine, around 1150, and another across the river in 1165. Nearly 400 of her letters survive.

**3.9 Women's work**
> Peasant women reaped, made hay, spun, milked and brewed. Some landowning women ran whole estates while their husbands were away.

**3.10 The Church in everyday life**
> The Church owned land, ran monasteries and kept many records. Monasteries and convents also cared for travelers and the sick.

**3.11 Feasts**
> Lords held feasts in the great hall, with many courses. Most people ate simply. Some lords fed their servants and tenants at Christmas.

## Job 1. The year of work

**Card:** Put each job in its season. Real months changed with the place and the weather.

**Teach card:** Farm work followed the year. Seeds go in, crops grow, harvest comes, then work moves indoors.

**Worked example (tap by tap):** "Haymaking" · Grass grows tall in early summer · Hay is cut and dried in the sun · So haymaking goes in **summer**.

## Job 3. Plan a pilgrim's days (problem set B)

**Card:** Plan where a walker sleeps each night. Aim for 20 to 25 km a day.

**Model line (always shown):** A simplified picture. Distances are rounded modern walking distances. Medieval paths, weather and walkers were all different.

**Map (schematic):** five dots on a line climbing over a mountain shape. Labels and distances between them:

Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port **25 km** Roncesvalles **22 km** Zubiri **21 km** Pamplona **24 km** Puente la Reina

**How to play:** Tap a place to stop for the night. The day's total shows above each stretch. A day over 30 km shows a tired-feet line: "That's a very long day on foot." Nothing is wrong; the walker just gets tired. **Rest day** adds a day with 0 km.

**Teach card:** Add the stretches you walk in one day. That sum is the day's distance.

**Worked example:** Roncesvalles to Zubiri is 22 km · Zubiri to Pamplona is 21 km · Both in one day: 22 + 21 = 43 km · That's a very long day. Stop at Zubiri instead.

## Job 6. Legend or history?

**Card:** Some stories really happened. Some are legends. Sort the cards.

**Teach card:** History has evidence, like records or buildings. A legend is a story people told, and still tell, which nobody has shown really happened.

**Worked example:** "Domesday Book" · Is there evidence? · The book still exists, in an archive · So: **history**.

| # | Card | Answer | Hint | Explanation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| L1 | Robin Hood robbed the rich to help the poor. | Legend | Is there a record of a real Robin Hood? | Poems about Robin Hood were known by about 1377. No real person has been proved to be him. |
| L2 | King Arthur pulled a sword from a stone. | Legend | Who told this story? | Arthur's stories grew over centuries. A popular book about him was written around 1136. There is no good evidence he was a real king. |

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Middle Ages:** a European name for about 500 to 1500 CE.
- **Manor:** a lord's estate, with a village and its fields.
- **Peasant:** a person who farmed the land, often for a lord.
- **Lord:** a person who held land and had power over the people on it.
- **Mill:** a building where grain is ground into flour.
- **Multure:** the share of grain the miller kept as payment.
- **Suit of mill:** a rule that tenants had to use the lord's mill.
- **Domesday Book:** the record of King William I's survey of England, 1086.
- **Survey:** asking everyone the same questions and writing down the answers.
- **Pilgrim:** someone on a journey to a holy place.
- **Pilgrimage:** that journey.
- **Hospice:** a place that gave travelers a bed, food and care.
- **Stage:** a section of a journey between stopping places.
- **Guild:** a group of people in one trade who set rules together.
- **Fair:** a big market held once or twice a year.
- **Abbess:** the woman who leads a community of nuns.
- **Alewife, brewster:** a woman who brewed and sold ale.
- **Legend:** a story people told, which nobody has shown really happened.
- **Source:** anything that gives us evidence about the past.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches.** Everyday life in medieval Europe through three linked threads: a village and its mill (manors, obligations, food, the miller's share, the Domesday survey of 1086); the pilgrim road to Santiago de Compostela over the Pyrenees (pilgrimage, the scallop badge, hospices, the bridge at Puente la Reina, a twelfth-century guide); and towns and work (markets, fairs, guilds, what castles were for, women's work including alewives and the abbess Hildegard of Bingen). It practices fractions of a quantity, addition of distances, division, counting and tables, and source reading: what a record says and what it can't say, and the difference between legend and history.

**What it does not claim.** Willowbank, its mill, the carved shell, the ribbons and the high ford are fiction. The Domesday-style entry for Willowbank is made up in the pattern of real entries. The 1/16 share is an example from inside the real range (about 1/30 to 1/10), not a rule from one real mill. Route distances are rounded modern walking figures; medieval paths differed. The "pyramid" is a simplified picture of a messier society. The dragon in the sort was made up to test the idea of a legend.

**Gentle topics.** The pack mentions that some peasants were not free to leave. It does not cover war, plague or punishment.
