## Story scenes

### Scene 1. One long row (Act I, chapter 3: investigate and plant)

**Hook:** The storm flattened the orchard's young trees. The orchard keeper has 24 seedlings to plant again, in a rectangle.

**Short:**
> Twenty-four seedlings wait in the barrow. Pip wants one long row. The orchard keeper wants a rectangle. "How many ways?" asks {guide}.

**Longer:**
> The storm has laid the young trees flat, roots and all. The orchard keeper has saved 24 seedlings in a barrow. "Rows," she says. "Straight rows, the same in each, so the cart fits between."
>
> "One row of 24!" says Pip. "Very long. Very impressive."
>
> "Very far to walk," says the orchard keeper.
>
> Dot pegs out 4 rows of 6. Bramble pegs out 6 rows of 4, on the other side of the path.
>
> "Mine's better," says Bramble. "Mine has more rows."
>
> "Mine has longer rows," says Dot.
>
> {guide} walks round Dot's plan and looks at it from the side. "It's the same plan, turned. Same 24 trees."
>
> Bramble's magpie lands on a peg and counts, very loudly, to 24. Twice.
>
> "Then let's find every way," says the orchard keeper. "I like to choose."

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> Twenty-four makes four rectangles: 1 by 24, 2 by 12, 3 by 8 and 4 by 6. Turning one round does not make a new one. Each side length is a factor of 24.

### Scene 2. Twenty-three won't (Act II, chapter 2: a garden across the seasons)

**Hook:** Some old trees survived. The orchard keeper counts 23 that can be moved, and nothing makes them into a rectangle.

**Short:**
> Twenty-three old trees to move. Bramble tries 2 rows, 3 rows, 4 rows. There's always one left over. "Twenty-three is being difficult," she says.

**Longer:**
> Not every tree fell. Twenty-three old ones can be dug up and moved to higher ground. The orchard keeper wants them in a neat rectangle, like the seedlings.
>
> Bramble tries 2 rows. One tree is left over. She tries 3 rows: two left over. Four rows, five rows. Something always sticks out.
>
> "Twenty-three is being difficult," says Bramble, and taps it with her wand. Nothing happens.
>
> "Twenty-three is prime," says {guide}. "Its only rectangle is one long row."
>
> Pip looks very pleased. "So I was right about long rows."
>
> "For twenty-three," says the orchard keeper, "you were." She plants them in one long row along the top wall, where they will shelter the seedlings from the wind.

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> A prime number has exactly two factors: 1 and itself. Its only rectangle is one row. Twenty-three is prime. The number 1 is not prime: it has just one factor.

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer. Worked steps appear one tap at a time.

**T1. Pairs and the odd one out** (A true thing about math)
> Put things in pairs. If every one has a partner, the number is even. If one is left over, it is odd.
Worked example: 1. Nine seedlings. 2. Make pairs: 4 pairs. 3. One is left over. 4. Nine is odd.

**T2. Two equal rows** (A true thing about math)
> Even numbers split into two equal rows. 8 = 4 + 4. Splitting 7 into 3 and 4 does not count: the rows must match.
Worked example: 1. Fourteen seedlings. 2. Two equal rows: 7 and 7. 3. 14 = 7 + 7, so 14 is even.

**T3. Why the last digit tells you** (A true thing about math)
> Every ten makes 5 pairs, with none left. So only the ones digit can leave one over. Even numbers end in 0, 2, 4, 6 or 8.
Worked example: 1. 37 is 3 tens and 7. 2. The tens pair up. 3. 7 leaves one. 4. 37 is odd.

**T4. Rows and columns** (A true thing about math)
> A plot of same-size squares, no gaps. Count the squares in one row. Count the rows. Rows × squares in a row = all the squares.
Worked example: 1. 4 rows. 2. 5 in each row. 3. 5, 10, 15, 20. 4. 4 × 5 = 20.

**T5. Turn the plan** (A true thing about math)
> Turn a plan a quarter turn. 4 rows of 6 become 6 rows of 4. Same trees, so 4 × 6 = 6 × 4.
Worked example: 1. 2 rows of 3: 6 trees. 2. Turn it. 3. 3 rows of 2: still 6.

**T6. Sharing doesn't turn** (A true thing about math)
> Sharing is different. 12 seedlings in 3 rows: 4 each. 3 seedlings in 12 rows: not even one each. So 12 ÷ 3 is not 3 ÷ 12.
Worked example: 1. 12 ÷ 3 = 4. 2. 3 ÷ 12: three rows get one, nine get none. 3. Not the same.

**T7. Counting in 6s** (A true thing about math)
> Count in 3s, then double each one. 3, 6, 9, 12 doubled is 6, 12, 18, 24. Sevens are fives and twos together.
Worked example: 1. 3 × 4 = 12. 2. Double it: 24. 3. 6 × 4 = 24.

**T8. A pattern needs a reason** (A true thing about math)
> Noticing a pattern is a start. Then ask why it happens. A reason tells you the pattern will keep going.
Worked example: 1. 5, 10, 15, 20. 2. Each step adds 5. 3. The ones digit swaps: 5, 0, 5, 0. 4. It always will.

**T9. Splitting a rectangle** (A true thing about math)
> Don't know a fact? Split the rectangle into facts you know. Then add the parts.
Worked example: 1. 6 rows of 7. 2. Split the 7: 5 and 2. 3. 6 × 5 = 30 and 6 × 2 = 12. 4. 30 + 12 = 42.

**T10. Every rectangle** (A true thing about math)
> Try 1 row, 2 rows, 3 rows, in order. Each one that fits is a factor pair. Stop when the pairs meet in the middle.
Worked example: 1. 12 trees. 2. 1 × 12, 2 × 6, 3 × 4. 3. 4 × 3 is 3 × 4 again. 4. Stop: three pairs.

**T11. Factors and multiples** (A true thing about math)
> Factors go into a number: they are its rectangle sides. Multiples are in its times table, hop after hop. Multiples never run out.
Worked example: 1. Factors of 6: 1, 2, 3, 6. 2. Multiples of 6: 6, 12, 18, 24, and on.

**T12. Common means both** (A true thing about math)
> A common factor goes into both numbers. A common multiple is on both hop lines. Look for numbers on both lists.
Worked example: 1. Rows of 4: 4, 8, 12. 2. Rows of 6: 6, 12. 3. 12 is on both.

**T13. Prime: exactly one rectangle** (A true thing about math)
> A prime number has exactly two factors, 1 and itself. Its only rectangle is one row. 1 is not prime. 2 is the only even prime.
Worked example: 1. 7 trees. 2. 2 rows? 3 rows? One left over each time. 3. Only 1 × 7. 4. 7 is prime.

**T14. Square and cube numbers** (A true thing about math)
> Square: equal rows and columns, so 7² = 7 × 7 = 49. Cube of crates: equal three ways, so 4³ = 4 × 4 × 4 = 64.
Worked example: 1. 7² is not 7 × 2. 2. Seven rows of seven. 3. 49 trees.

**T15. Undoing a square** (A true thing about math)
> A square root undoes squaring. 49 trees in a square: √49 = 7 on each side. Some roots never come out exact.
Worked example: 1. 64 crates in a cube. 2. What times itself, times itself, is 64? 3. 4. So ∛64 = 4.

**T16. Factor trees** (A true thing about math)
> Split a number into two factors. Keep splitting until every branch ends in a prime. Every way of splitting gives the same primes.
Worked example: 1. 12 = 2 × 6. 2. 6 = 2 × 3. 3. 12 = 2 × 2 × 3.

## Real-world and history cards

**H1. The sieve** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> A Greek scholar named Eratosthenes is said to have found primes by crossing out multiples. Whatever is never crossed out is prime.
Keeper's note:
> Eratosthenes lived in Egypt more than 2,000 years ago. His own books are lost. A later writer, Nicomachus, described the "sieve of Eratosthenes" in a math book written about 1,900 years ago. We use his method in the Sieve Bed.

**H2. More primes than any list** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> In the *Elements*, written in Greek over 2,000 years ago, Euclid showed that no list of primes can hold them all.
Keeper's note:
> Euclid did not say "infinitely many". He said the primes are more than any number of them you name. His idea: multiply the primes on your list and add one. That number leaves one over in every row size on your list.

**H3. Sunzi's puzzle** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> A Chinese book called the *Sunzi Suanjing* asks: count things by 3s, 2 are left; by 5s, 3; by 7s, 2. How many?
Keeper's note:
> The book was written between about 1,500 and 1,800 years ago; nobody knows exactly when, or by whom. It gives the answer 23 and a way to reach it. It does not prove that the way always works.

## Bench (e): The Sieve Bed

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> A nursery bed of 100 numbered plots, 10 by 10.

**Opening:** Cross out the multiples. What's left is prime.
**Steps shown one at a time:**
1. Cross out 1. It is not prime.
2. Circle 2. Cross out every multiple of 2 after it.
3. Circle the next plot not crossed out: 3. Cross out its multiples.
4. Do the same for 5, then 7.
5. Every plot left is prime.
**After 7:** Stop here. 11 × 11 is 121, past the bed.
**Count line:** [count] primes from 1 to 100.

**Guided item:** Is 15 crossed out? Why?
- Step 1: 15 = 3 × 5.
- Step 2: It was crossed out with the 3s.
- Step 3: 15 makes a rectangle, so it is not prime.

## Problem sets

Twenty-two three-choice items put the right answer at A seven times, B seven times and C eight times.

### Set A. Pairs and rows (Sprout and Seedling)

**A1 (Sprout, 3-choice; picture of seedlings in pairs, one alone)**
Prompt: Can every seedling have a partner?
- A. Yes. There are lots of seedlings. *(tests: parity judged by size)*
- B. No. One seedling has no partner. *(right)*
- C. Yes, if one pair takes three. *(tests: unequal groups count as pairs)*
Hint: Look for a seedling standing alone.
Explanation: One is left over, so they can't all pair up. That makes it odd.
Feedback A: Lots or few doesn't matter. Look for one left alone.
Feedback B: Yes. One left over means odd.
Feedback C: A pair is two. Three is not a pair.

**A2 (Sprout, 3-choice; picture of two rows paired one for one, exactly one extra seedling)**
Prompt: Pip pairs two rows. One seedling has no partner. Odd or even?
- A. Odd. One seedling has no partner. *(right)*
- B. Even. Two rows always means even. *(tests: any two groups make even)*
- C. Even. The rows are nearly the same. *(tests: near-equal is good enough)*
Hint: Do the two rows match, one for one?
Explanation: After pairing every seedling you can, exactly one is left over. That makes the number odd.
Feedback A: Yes. One left over after pairing means odd.
Feedback B: Two rows can hold odd or even totals. Pair the seedlings.
Feedback C: Nearly isn't enough. One seedling has no partner.

**A3 (Sprout, 3-choice; picture of a plan, then the same plan turned)**
Prompt: The keeper turns her plan. Are there more trees now?
- A. More. The rows are longer now. *(tests: turning changes the total)*
- B. Fewer. There are fewer rows now. *(tests: turning changes the total)*
- C. The same trees, just turned. *(right)*
Hint: Did any tree get added or taken away?
Explanation: Turning moves no trees. Same trees, looked at another way.
Feedback A: Longer rows, but fewer of them. No tree was added.
Feedback B: Fewer rows, but longer ones. No tree was taken.
Feedback C: Yes. Turning doesn't add or take away.

**A4 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: No seedlings at all. Is zero odd or even?
- A. Even. None is left without a partner. *(right)*
- B. Neither. Zero isn't a number of things. *(tests: zero left out of parity)*
- C. Odd. Zero is too small to be even. *(tests: parity judged by size)*
Hint: Is anything left over?
Explanation: Nothing is left over, so zero is even.
Feedback A: Yes. Zero leaves nothing over.
Feedback B: Zero is a number. Does it leave one over?
Feedback C: Small numbers can be even. 2 is.

**A5 (Seedling, number)**
Prompt: 3 rows of same-size squares, 5 in each row. How many squares?
Answer: 15.
Worked steps: 1. One row: 5. 2. Three rows: 5, 10, 15. 3. 3 × 5 = 15.
Tests: 8 (adding the rows and the row length); 12 (counting only the outside edge).
Hint: How many in one row? How many rows?
Explanation: Rows times squares in a row: 3 × 5 = 15.

**A6 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: 12 seedlings in 3 rows is 4 each. Is 3 in 12 rows the same?
- A. Yes. Swapping works for sharing too. *(tests: division is commutative)*
- B. Yes. Both use 12 and 3. *(tests: same numbers, same answer)*
- C. No. 3 seedlings can't fill 12 rows. *(right)*
Hint: Try sharing 3 seedlings into 12 rows.
Explanation: Turning works for rows of trees. It doesn't work for sharing.
Feedback A: Turning a plan works. Sharing is different. Try it.
Feedback B: Same numbers, different job. Which is being shared?
Feedback C: Yes. 12 ÷ 3 and 3 ÷ 12 are different.

**A7 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which shows that 14 is even?
- A. 14 = 7 + 7 *(right)*
- B. 14 = 6 + 8 *(tests: any two groups make even)*
- C. 14 is bigger than 10 *(tests: parity judged by size)*
Hint: Even splits into two equal rows.
Explanation: Seven and seven match, so 14 is even.
Feedback A: Yes. Two equal rows, none left over.
Feedback B: 6 and 8 don't match. Can you make two that do?
Feedback C: Size doesn't decide. 11 is bigger than 10, and odd.

### Set B. Counting and patterns (Seedling and Sapling)

**B1 (Seedling, number)**
Prompt: Posts go every 6 steps. Where is the 8th post?
Answer: 48.
Worked steps: 1. Count in 3s to the 8th: 24. 2. Double it. 3. 48.
Tests: 46 (a slip near the end); 54 (one post too far).
Hint: Count in 3s, then double.
Explanation: Eight 6s is double eight 3s: 24 doubled is 48.

**B2 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: 9, 18, 27, 36. What comes next?
- A. 46 *(tests: adding 10 and forgetting to take 1)*
- B. 45 *(right)*
- C. 44 *(tests: ones digit drops two)*
Hint: Tens go up one. Ones go down one.
Explanation: Adding 9 is adding 10 and taking 1: 45.
Feedback A: That's ten more. Nine more is one less than that.
Feedback B: Yes. Tens up, ones down.
Feedback C: Ones go down by one each time, not two.

**B3 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Every 5s answer ends in 0 or 5. Why?
- A. Because that's just how the 5s go. *(tests: restating the pattern as the reason)*
- B. Adding 5 flips the ones between 5 and 0. *(right)*
- C. It's chance. Big ones might end in 3. *(tests: pattern as coincidence)*
Hint: What happens to the ones digit when you add 5?
Explanation: 5 + 5 makes a new ten. So the ones go 5, 0, 5, 0 forever.
Feedback A: That says what happens. Why does it happen?
Feedback B: Yes. The step never changes, so neither does the pattern.
Feedback C: Each step adds 5, every time. Can a 3 ever appear?

**B4 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: The times table matches across its diagonal. Why?
- A. A 3-by-4 plot turned is a 4-by-3 plot. *(right)*
- B. Every number in the table shows up twice. *(tests: over-generalizing the pattern)*
- C. It was drawn that way to look neat. *(tests: pattern as coincidence)*
Hint: Think about turning a plan.
Explanation: Turning a rectangle keeps the total, so 3 × 4 = 4 × 3 everywhere.
Feedback A: Yes. Turning is the reason.
Feedback B: Not always. 49 shows up once; 12 shows up six times.
Feedback C: Turning a plot explains it, every time.

**B5 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: 3 rows of 4 is 12. What is 3 rows of 400?
Answer: 1,200.
Worked steps: 1. 400 is 4 hundreds. 2. 3 × 4 hundreds = 12 hundreds. 3. 12 hundreds = 1,200.
Tests: 120 (moving one place, not two); 1,204 (sticking the 4 on).
Hint: 400 is 4 hundreds.
Explanation: 12 hundreds is 1,200. Each digit moves two places left.

**B6 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: 8 rows of 7. Use 8 × 5 = 40 to find 8 × 7.
Answer: 56.
Worked steps: 1. Split 7 into 5 and 2. 2. 8 × 5 = 40; 8 × 2 = 16. 3. 40 + 16 = 56.
Tests: 42 (adding 2, not 8 rows of 2); 54 (a slip adding 14).
Hint: Split the rectangle: 8 rows of 5 and 8 rows of 2.
Explanation: Two smaller rectangles make the big one.

**B7 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Count in 25s: 25, 50, 75, 100. What comes next?
- A. 105 *(tests: switching to a smaller step at the hundred)*
- B. 110 *(tests: switching to tens at the hundred)*
- C. 125 *(right)*
Hint: Keep the same step past 100.
Explanation: Four 25s make 100. One more 25 is 125.
Feedback A: The step is still 25, even after 100.
Feedback B: Still 25 each time. 100 + 25?
Feedback C: Yes. Same step, past the hundred.

- B. Yes. 51 is in no table she knows. *(tests: judging prime by feel)*

Feedback B: Try rows, don't guess. 3 rows of 17 fits.
Feedback C: Yes. A second rectangle means not prime.

**D2 (Sapling, 3-choice)**

- B. Yes. Only 1 goes into it. *(tests: a prime has one factor)*
- C. No. A prime has exactly two factors. *(right)*

Explanation: 1 has one factor. Primes need exactly two, so 1 is not prime.

Feedback C: Yes. 1 is not prime, and not composite.

**D3 (Sapling, 3-choice)**

- B. No. Prime numbers are never even. *(tests: all primes are odd)*
- C. No. 2 is too small to be prime. *(tests: prime judged by size)*

Explanation: Exactly two factors: 1 and 2. It's the only even prime.
Feedback A: Yes. The only even prime.
Feedback B: Every other even number has 2 as a factor. 2 itself doesn't.
Feedback C: Size doesn't matter. Count the factors.

**D4 (Sapling, tap the primes on a 1 to 20 bed)**

**D5 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Is 97 prime? Which numbers must you test?
- A. Test 2, 3, 5 and 7. Then stop. *(right)*
- B. Test 2 only. It's odd, so it's prime. *(tests: odd means prime)*
- C. Test 2 and 5 only, from the last digit. *(tests: checking only the easy rules)*

Feedback A: 14 is 7 × 2, a thin rectangle. The square is much bigger.

**E2 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: Crates 4 wide, 4 deep, 4 high. How many crates?

Tests: 12 (4 × 3); 16 (only one layer).
Hint: How many in one layer? How many layers?
Explanation: 4³ = 4 × 4 × 4 = 64.

**E3 (Sapling, tap two squares)**
Prompt: From 1 to 99, tap two square numbers that make 100.

Worked steps: 1. Pick a square: 36. 2. 100 − 36 = 64. 3. 64 = 8 × 8, a square. Done.
Tests: 50 and 50 (50 is not square); 25 and 75 (75 is not square).
Hint: Pick a square. Is what's left also a square?
Explanation: 6² + 8² = 36 + 64 = 100. No other pair from 1 to 99 works.

**E4 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**

- B. Yes. It's even, and 1 + 5 + 6 = 12. *(right)*
- C. No. The 6s table stops at 72. *(tests: multiples stop at the table's end)*

Feedback B: Yes. 156 = 6 × 26.

**E5 (Keeper's Desk, number)**

Explanation: Split 24 before moving a factor. 4 × 25 = 100 does the rest.

**E6 (Keeper's Desk, factor tree)**

## Key terms (glossary)

- **pair**: two things that go together.
- **even**: makes pairs with none left over.
- **odd**: makes pairs with one left over.
- **row**: things side by side, across.
- **column**: things one behind another, down.
- **factor**: a number that goes into another with nothing left.
- **factor pair**: two numbers that multiply to make a number.
- **multiple**: a number in another number's times table.
- **common factor**: a factor of both numbers.
- **common multiple**: a multiple of both numbers.
- **prime number**: a number with exactly two factors, 1 and itself.
- **composite number**: a number with more than two factors.
- **square number**: a number times itself, like 7 × 7.
- **cube number**: a number used as a factor three times, like 4 × 4 × 4.
- **square root**: the number you square to get this one.
- **factor tree**: splits a number until every branch ends in a prime.
- **sieve**: crossing out multiples to find primes.
- **HCF**: highest common factor, the biggest factor both numbers share.
- **LCM**: lowest common multiple, the smallest multiple both share.
