# First Problems: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 204](../specs/204-first-problems.md). Use as written, after independent spec review and a math check. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion and is never gendered; `{courier}` is the explorer's courier. Pip is he/him, Dot she/her, Bramble she/her (with her magpie), Ember she/her, the miller she/her, the baker he/him, the spoon seller he/him (the cook is never given a pronoun), the librarian they/them. Real-world facts are in the [source audit](../source-audits/204-first-problems.md); items still marked `[check]` stay off screen until cleared. Nothing here is scored or timed. Anything a child says aloud stays in the room: it is never recorded, stored or graded.

## Labels used on every card

- **A story from Willowbank**: the town, the puzzle basket, the neighbors and every character are made up.
- **A true thing about math**: checked math, explained simply.
- **A true thing from history**: something people really did or wrote.
- **A made-up model**: a simple game rule, shown on the card, that is not a real measurement.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. The basket at the gate (Act I, chapter 1: find and feed)

**Hook:** After the storm, neighbors need help but are too busy to ask twice. They drop picture puzzles in Pip's basket.

**Short:**
> The first puzzle in the basket is from the cook. "Plates for the neighbors," it says. Pip starts counting. {guide} says, "Wait. What do we want?"

**Longer:**
> The storm has gone. Everyone in Willowbank is busy, so Pip hangs a basket on the garden gate. "Put your puzzles in here," says his sign. "Pip loves a plan."
>
> The first puzzle is a picture from the cook. Eight neighbors sit at a long table. Five plates sit on the shelf.
>
> Pip grabs the numbers. "Eight and five! Thirteen!"
>
> "Thirteen what?" asks {guide}.
>
> Pip looks again. "Thirteen... plates? We don't have thirteen plates."
>
> "So what does the cook want to know?"
>
> Pip reads it slowly, like a story. "Eight neighbors. Five plates. Each neighbor needs a plate." He counts on: six, seven, eight. "Three more plates!"
>
> Dot nods. "Now look back. Does it make sense?"
>
> "Five plates and three more is eight," says Pip. "One each." He writes it on the card. Then he draws the plates, just to be sure.

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> Pip's first answer used every number he saw. That is a common slip. Reading the puzzle as a story first, then asking "what do we want?", turned thirteen into three.

### Scene 2. Planks and a plan (Act I, chapter 4: mend the village)

**Hook:** The footbridge needs twelve planks. The miller's puzzle has more than one step, and one number nobody needs.

**Short:**
> "Twelve planks. Five are fixed. Pip fixes four more. The brook is two steps wide." Pip frowns. "Which numbers matter?"

**Longer:**
> The miller drops a puzzle in the basket. It has a bridge on it, and a lot of numbers.
>
> "The footbridge needs twelve planks. Five are fixed already. Today Pip fixes four more. The brook is two steps wide. How many planks are still to fix?"
>
> "Four numbers!" says Pip. He reaches for the counters.
>
> "Plan first," says Dot. "What do we know? What do we want?"
>
> Pip sorts the numbers. Twelve planks in all: know. Five fixed: know. Four more fixed today: know. How many still to fix: want. "And the brook being two steps wide?"
>
> "Does that help with planks?" asks {guide}.
>
> "No," says Pip. "It's just true."
>
> He draws a long bar for twelve planks. He colors five, then four more. Three are empty.
>
> "Three planks to fix," he says. "Look back: five, four and three make twelve." He stands on each new plank. Twice.

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> A long job keeps its whole picture in view. Pip's bar showed all twelve planks, so he did not stop at "nine fixed". The brook's width was a true number that did not help.

### Scene 3. The market window (Act II, chapter 1: the weekly market)

**Hook:** The market is back. Bramble bets Pip there is no math at a market. Pip finds some everywhere.

**Short:**
> Bramble leans on the spoon stall. "There's no math here. Just apples and spoons." Pip points. "How many spoons? Which pile is bigger?"

**Longer:**
> The weekly market is back on the green. Bramble leans on the spoon seller's stall, eating an apple.
>
> "Math is for puzzles," she says. "A market is just apples and spoons."
>
> Pip points at the spoon seller's table. "How many spoons are there? Which pile is bigger? How much is an apple?"
>
> "Those are just questions," says Bramble.
>
> "Math questions," says Pip.
>
> Bramble narrows her eyes. "Fine. Then tell me a story for three plus four."
>
> Pip thinks. "Three ducks swim on the pond. Four more ducks fly down. Now there are seven ducks."
>
> The magpie lands on the stall with a spoon in its beak.
>
> "Now there are seven ducks and one stolen spoon," says Bramble. "Is that math too?"
>
> The spoon seller sighs. "It's one spoon fewer," he says. "That's math."

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> Math goes both ways. You can spot a counting question in a real scene. You can also start with 3 + 4 and tell a story that fits it.

### Scene 4. Count them again, slower (Act II, chapter 3: Ember's birthday)

**Hook:** Ember counts the birthday cakes and gets a different answer each time. Pip asks her to show her working.

**Short:**
> "Nine cakes," says Ember. "No, ten." Pip gets the slate. "Show me how you counted. A drawing counts."

**Longer:**
> The cook sets out honey cakes for Ember's birthday. Ember counts them. "Nine." She counts again. "Ten!" She counts a third time, very slowly, and loses track in the middle.
>
> "Show me how you counted," says Pip. He brings the slate.
>
> Ember draws a ring for each cake. Then she draws a line through each ring as she counts it. "Six on the big tray," she says, "and three on the little one."
>
> "So how many?"
>
> "Six, then seven, eight, nine." She points at each ring as she says it. "Nine. I counted one cake twice before."
>
> "How do you know?" asks {guide}.
>
> Ember taps the slate. "Because every ring has one line now. No ring has two."
>
> Pip claps. Ember eats cake number nine, to celebrate. Now there are eight.

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> Ember's drawing showed her thinking, so she could find her own slip. Saying "because every ring has one line" is a real reason.

## Teach cards

Each card is under 30 words. Worked steps appear one tap at a time.

**T1. A puzzle is a little story** (A true thing about math)
> Read a puzzle like a story first. What is happening? What do we want to find out? Numbers come last.
Worked example: 1. Story: letters blow into the post office. 2. Want: how many letters now? 3. Numbers: 4 letters, then 3 more.

**T2. Know and want** (A true thing about math)
> Sort the puzzle. What do we know? What do we want? Some numbers may not help at all.
Worked example: 1. Know: 8 neighbors. Know: 5 plates. 2. Want: how many more plates? 3. Not needed: the table is long.

**T3. Sometimes we can't tell yet** (A true thing about math)
> Some puzzles are missing a piece. Then the smart answer is "We can't tell yet." Say what is missing.
Worked example: 1. "The baker baked buns. How many are in the oven?" 2. Nobody told us. 3. We can't tell yet.

**T4. Pick a tool that fits** (A true thing about math)
> Fingers are quick for small numbers but run out after ten. A ten frame shows how many empty spaces are left.
Worked example: 1. 8 + 7. 2. Fill a ten frame: 8 and 2 more make 10. 3. 5 are left over. 4. 10 and 5: 15.

**T5. Make the objects act it out** (A true thing about math)
> Your counters do what the story does. Eating takes away. Coming in puts together. Then say the answer with its words.
Worked example: 1. 5 apples: put out 5 counters. 2. The cook eats 2: take 2 away. 3. 3 apples left.

**T6. Spot the math** (A true thing about math)
> Math hides in every scene. Look for how many, how much, how long, or which is more.
Worked example: 1. A stall with apples and spoons. 2. Ask: how many spoons? 3. Ask: which pile is bigger?

**T7. A story for a number sentence** (A true thing about math)
> A number sentence can turn into a story. Each number gets a job in the story.
Worked example: 1. 3 + 4. 2. 3 ducks swim. 3. 4 more ducks fly down. 4. Now 7 ducks.

**T8. Say what the answer means** (A true thing about math)
> An answer needs its words. "5" is only half an answer. "5 apples left" or "5 cents change" is the whole answer.
Worked example: 1. 10 − 7 = 3. 2. Pip paid 10 cents for a 7-cent apple. 3. Answer: 3 cents change.

**T9. Four ways, one story** (A true thing about math)
> Counters, a picture, a number line and a number sentence can all show one story. Each number keeps its job.
Worked example: 1. 4 sacks, 2 more come. 2. Counters: 4, then 2. 3. Number line: start 4, jump 2, land 6. 4. 4 + 2 = 6.

**T10. The bar picture** (A true thing about math)
> Draw a long bar for the whole. Draw shorter bars under it for the parts. Put ? where the unknown is.
Worked example: 1. Whole: 12 planks. 2. Part: 9 fixed. 3. Other part: ?. 4. 9 + 3 = 12, so ? is 3.

**T11. Show me how** (A true thing about math)
> Show how you got your answer. Point to your counters. Draw dots, rings or jumps. A drawing counts as working.
Worked example: 1. 4 + 3. 2. Draw 4 dots, then 3 dots. 3. Count on from 4: 5, 6, 7.

**T12. Say why, with because** (A true thing about math)
> Say what you did. Then say why it works, starting with "because". Checking another way helps too.
Worked example: 1. "I made 10 first." 2. "Because 7 and 3 make 10, and 6 is 3 and 3." 3. 10 + 3 = 13.

**T13. Three ways to add** (A true thing about math)
> Count all from one. Count on from a number. Or use a fact you already know. Each one gets the same total.
Worked example: 1. 6 + 3. 2. Count all: 1 to 9, nine counts. 3. Count on: 7, 8, 9, three counts. 4. Know it: 9.

**T14. Use the pattern** (A true thing about math)
> Adding in any order gives the same total, so start with the bigger number. On a hundred square, adding ten moves straight down.
Worked example: 1. 2 + 9 is 9 + 2: two hops. 2. 47 + 10: one row down. 3. 57.

**T15. One fact gives you more** (A true thing about math)
> One array shows four facts. Adding a hundred keeps tens and ones the same. If hundreds reach ten, make a thousand.
Worked example: 1. 6 rows of 4: 24. 2. So 4 × 6 = 24. 3. 24 ÷ 4 = 6 and 24 ÷ 6 = 4.

**T16. Long jobs: plan, then look back** (A true thing about math)
> For a long job, plan the steps. Keep the whole picture in view. Look back: does the answer make sense?
Worked example: 1. 12 planks, 5 fixed, 4 more fixed. 2. Step 1: 5 + 4 = 9 fixed. 3. Step 2: 12 − 9 = 3. 4. Check: 9 + 3 = 12.

## Real-world and history cards

**H1. A book about solving problems** (A true thing from history; Seedling and up)
> In 1945, George Pólya wrote a book called *How to Solve It*. He gave four steps: understand, plan, do, look back.
Keeper's note:
> Pólya was a mathematician born in Budapest, Hungary. He taught at Stanford University in California and wrote the book there. It has been printed in many languages. Pip's plan card uses his four steps in simpler words.

**H2. Counting with clay** (A true thing from history; Sprout and up)
> Long ago, farmers in the Near East made small clay shapes. Each shape stood for one thing, like one sheep.
Keeper's note:
> These clay tokens were cones, balls, disks and other shapes. People used them for thousands of years, starting over 9,000 years ago. Later, some were sealed inside hollow clay balls so nobody could change the count. One archaeologist, Denise Schmandt-Besserat, thinks the tokens led to writing. Other experts are less sure.

## Bench (f): The Quick-Way Path

> Each way counts the hops it took. Fewer hops is quicker. Every way gives the same total.

**Hop tag pattern:** "That way took [number] hops."

**Guided item:** 2 + 9.
- Step 1: Count on from 2. *(Nine hops: 3 to 11.)*
- Step 2: Swap: 9 + 2. *(Two hops: 10, 11.)*
- Step 3: Same total, fewer hops: 11.

## Problem sets

Twenty-six three-choice items put the right answer at A nine times, B nine times and C eight times. Sprout items use no numbers.

### Set A. What do we know, what do we want? (Sprout and Seedling)

**A1 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Picture: Dot holds some letters. More letters blow in the window.
Prompt: More letters blew in. What does Dot want to find out?
- A. How many letters she has now. *(right)*
- B. What color the envelopes are. *(tests: noticing a detail in place of the question)*
- C. Where each letter should go. *(tests: answering a different question)*
Hint: Look at the letters coming in. What changes?
Explanation: More letters came in, so Dot wants to know how many she has now.
Feedback A: Yes. That is the question the picture asks.
Feedback B: Colors are fun to see. What changes when letters blow in?
Feedback C: That is a good job for later. What does this picture ask?

**A2 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Story: The miller had a pile of sacks. The wind blew some away.
Prompt: Which retelling matches the story?
- A. The miller got more sacks. *(tests: flipping the action)*
- B. The miller has fewer sacks now. *(right)*
- C. The wind brought the miller some sacks. *(tests: mixing up who did what)*
Hint: Did the wind bring sacks or take them?
Explanation: The wind took some sacks away, so there are fewer.
Feedback A: Check the wind. Did it bring sacks or take them?
Feedback B: Yes. You told the story back in your own words.
Feedback C: The wind did something to the sacks. Which way did they go?

**A3 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Picture: A tray of buns on the bakery table. The oven door is shut.
Prompt: How many buns are in the oven?
- A. The same as on the tray. *(tests: using whatever is shown)*
- B. More than on the tray. *(tests: guessing to fill a gap)*
- C. We can't tell yet. *(right)*
Hint: Can you see inside the oven?
Explanation: Nothing tells us about the oven. "We can't tell yet" is a smart answer.
Feedback A: The tray is outside the oven. Can you see in?
Feedback B: That's a guess. What would you need to know?
Feedback C: Yes. The puzzle is missing a piece.

**A4 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: The cook needs 8 plates. Pip has 5. How many more plates?
- A. 13 more plates. *(tests: adding every number seen)*
- B. 3 more plates. *(right)*
- C. We can't tell yet. *(tests: withholding when the story gives enough)*
Hint: Count on from 5 to 8.
Explanation: 5 plates and 3 more make 8, so 3 more plates.
Feedback A: 13 plates is more than the cook needs. Look back at the story.
Feedback B: Yes. 5 and 3 more make 8.
Feedback C: This time we can tell. We know the plates and the plates needed.

**A5 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Dot has 9 letters and 2 hats. She posts 4 letters. Letters left?
- A. 5 letters. *(right)*
- B. 7 letters. *(tests: using every number, even the hats)*
- C. 13 letters. *(tests: adding when the story takes away)*
Hint: Do the hats change how many letters there are?
Explanation: The hats don't matter here. 9 letters take away 4 is 5 letters.
Feedback A: Yes. You left out the hats. They didn't help.
Feedback B: The hats are not letters. Which numbers are about letters?
Feedback C: Posting sends letters away. Does Dot have more or fewer?

**A6 (Seedling, sort)**
Card: "The handrail needs 8 ropes. 5 are tied. The brook is high. How many ropes are still to tie?"
Prompt: Sort each tile: we know, we want, or not needed.
- "The handrail needs 8 ropes." → We know
- "5 ropes are tied." → We know
- "How many ropes are still to tie?" → We want
- "The brook is high." → Not needed
Tests: the question sorted as a known; a true but unhelpful fact sorted as needed.
Hint: Which tile is a question? Which tile doesn't help with ropes?
Explanation: Two knows, one want, and one true thing that doesn't help.

### Set D. Four ways to show it (Seedling and Sapling)

**D1 (Seedling, match)**
Story: The miller has 4 sacks. 2 more sacks come.
Prompt: Match each number to its job in the story.
- 4 → the sacks at first
- 2 → the sacks that came
- 6 → all the sacks now
Tests: the total matched to a part; a part matched to the total.
Hint: Which number is there at the start? Which is at the end?
Explanation: In counters, picture, number line and 4 + 2 = 6, each number keeps its job.

**D2 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which number line shows 7 − 3?
- A. Start at 3, jump on 7, land on 10. *(tests: adding, with the numbers swapped)*
- B. Start at 7, jump on 3, land on 10. *(tests: adding in place of taking away)*
- C. Start at 7, jump back 3, land on 4. *(right)*
Hint: Take away means jumping back.
Explanation: Start at 7. Three jumps back: 6, 5, 4. 7 − 3 = 4.
Feedback A: That adds. Where should a take-away start?
Feedback B: Right start. Which way does take-away jump?
Feedback C: Yes. Back 3 from 7 lands on 4.

**D3 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Picture: 5 cakes on a big tray and 3 cakes on a small tray.
Prompt: Which number sentence matches the picture?
- A. 5 − 3 = 2 *(tests: picking take-away when nothing is taken)*
- B. 5 + 3 = 8 *(right)*
- C. 8 + 5 = 13 *(tests: using the total as a part)*
Hint: Two trays of cakes. Are any cakes taken away?
Explanation: Two groups together: 5 + 3 = 8 cakes.
Feedback A: No cake is taken away. Both trays stay.
Feedback B: Yes. 5 and 3 together make 8.
Feedback C: 8 is all the cakes. The parts are 5 and 3.

**D4 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Picture: a bar model. Whole: 12. One part: 5. Other part: ?.
Prompt: Which number sentence fits this bar?
- A. 5 + ? = 12 *(right)*
- B. 12 + 5 = ? *(tests: adding the whole to a part)*
- C. ? = 5 *(tests: copying the part you can see)*
Hint: The whole is 12. The two parts make the whole.
Explanation: 5 and the missing part make 12, so 5 + ? = 12. The ? is 7.
Feedback A: Yes. The parts join to make the whole: ? is 7.
Feedback B: 12 is already the whole. Can a part be bigger?
Feedback C: Look at the bar. Is the ? part the same size as 5?

**D5 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: 8 + 4 = ☐ + 5. What goes in the box?
- A. 12 *(tests: reading = as "write the answer")*
- B. 7 *(right)*
- C. 17 *(tests: adding every number)*
Hint: = means both sides are the same amount.
Explanation: 8 + 4 is 12. So ☐ + 5 must be 12 too. 7 + 5 = 12.
Feedback A: 12 is the left side. Is 12 + 5 the same as 12?
Feedback B: Yes. Both sides are 12.
Feedback C: = is a balance. Does 17 + 5 match 8 + 4?

### Set E. Show me, tell me (Sprout to Sapling)

**E1 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Pip says, "I know I'm right." How can he show you?
- A. Say "I'm right" again, much louder this time. *(tests: being sure counts as proof)*
- B. Point to his counters and show what he did. *(right)*
- C. Write the same answer again, in big letters. *(tests: showing working means writing the answer again)*
Hint: How could you see what Pip did?
Explanation: Showing his counters lets you see his thinking.
Feedback A: Loud isn't the same as showing. What could he point to?
Feedback B: Yes. Show me how, with counters or a drawing.
Feedback C: That shows the answer twice. How did he get it?

**E2 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Dot worked out 4 + 3 = 7. Which shows her working?
- A. "It's 7, because I know it's 7." *(tests: restating the answer)*
- B. 7 dots drawn in one long row, all the same. *(tests: showing the answer, hiding the method)*
- C. 4 dots, then 3 more dots, counted on. *(right)*
Hint: Can you see the 4 and the 3 in it?
Explanation: The drawing shows both groups and how they were counted.
Feedback A: That says the answer again. How did she get it?
Feedback B: That shows 7. Where are the 4 and the 3?
Feedback C: Yes. A drawing counts as working.

**E3 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Bramble says 6 + 2 = 8. She counted on: 7, 8. Agree?
- A. Yes. Start at 6 and count 2 more. *(right)*
- B. No. You must always count from 1. *(tests: only counting all is allowed)*
- C. Yes, because 8 is a big number. *(tests: judging by how the answer looks)*
Hint: Does her method make sense? Try it.
Explanation: Counting on from 6 for 2 more lands on 8. Her method works.
Feedback A: Yes. You checked her method as well as her answer.
Feedback B: Counting on is a real method. Try it: 7, 8.
Feedback C: Big doesn't mean right. Does her counting work?

**E4 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Pip's working: "9 + 5. I take 1 from the 5 to make 10. Then 10 + 5 = 15."
Prompt: Where is Pip's slip?
- A. The 5 became 4, so it's 10 + 4 = 14. *(right)*
- B. Making 10 first is never allowed in adding. *(tests: thinking only one method is allowed)*
- C. There's no slip. 15 is right, and it looks neat. *(tests: trusting working that looks neat)*
Hint: After Pip takes 1 from the 5, how many are left?
Explanation: Taking 1 from 5 leaves 4. 10 + 4 = 14, so 9 + 5 = 14.
Feedback A: Yes. You found the slip and said why.
Feedback B: Making 10 is a great method. Check what is left of the 5.
Feedback C: Check another way: 9, then count on 5. Where do you land?

**E5 (Sapling, say why; spoken to a grown-up, not stored)**
Prompt: Tell a grown-up why 7 + 6 = 13. Start with "because".
Model answer for the grown-up: "Because 7 and 3 make 10, and 6 is 3 and 3. Then 10 and 3 more is 13." Another way: "6 + 6 is 12, and one more is 13."
Tests: saying the answer without a reason; "because I know it".
Hint: Can you make 10 first?
Explanation: A reason says why it works. Two ways that agree make it stronger.

### Set F. Quicker ways and longer jobs (Seedling to Keeper's Desk)

**F1 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which counting way is quickest for 6 + 3?
- A. Count all, from 1 up to 9. *(tests: always counting all)*
- B. Count on from 3: six counts. *(tests: always starting from the first number seen)*
- C. Count on from 6: 7, 8, 9. *(right)*
Hint: Start with the bigger number. How many counts?
Explanation: From 6, three counts reach 9. That is fewer than six or nine counts.
Feedback A: That works but takes nine counts. Can you start higher?
Feedback B: That works with six counts. Which start needs fewer?
Feedback C: Yes. Three counts, same total.

**F2 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Pip turns 2 + 9 into 9 + 2. Is that allowed?
- A. Yes. The order doesn't change the total. *(right)*
- B. No. You must start with the first number. *(tests: order is fixed)*
- C. Yes, and 2 − 9 is the same as 9 − 2. *(tests: swapping take-away too)*
Hint: Try both. Do they land on the same number?
Explanation: 2 + 9 and 9 + 2 are both 11. Swapping only works for adding.
Feedback A: Yes. Two hops in place of nine.
Feedback B: Try 9 + 2. Does it land on 11 too?
Feedback C: Adding can swap. Take-away can't: 9 − 2 is 7.

**F3 (Seedling, number)**
Prompt: On the hundred square, what is 47 + 10?
Answer: 57.
Worked steps: 1. Find 47. 2. Adding ten moves one row straight down. 3. Land on 57. Only the tens digit changed.
Tests: 48 (adding one in place of ten); 147 (writing a 1 in front).
Hint: Which digit changes when you add a ten?
Explanation: 4 tens and 7 ones, plus 1 ten, is 5 tens and 7 ones.

**F4 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: 34, 44, 54. Pip adds ten each time. What comes next?
- A. 55 *(tests: changing the ones digit)*
- B. 64 *(right)*
- C. 65 *(tests: changing both digits)*
Hint: Which digit has been changing?
Explanation: Each time the tens digit goes up one. 54 + 10 = 64.
Feedback A: That adds one. The pattern adds ten.
Feedback B: Yes. You used the pattern without counting.
Feedback C: Look at the ones digit. Has it changed so far?

**F5 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: You know 6 × 4 = 24. Which do you also know?
- A. 4 × 6 = 10 *(tests: adding in place of multiplying)*
- B. 24 ÷ 4 = 8 *(tests: working each fact out separately, and slipping)*
- C. 24 ÷ 6 = 4 *(right)*
Hint: Picture 6 rows of 4. What does the array also show?
Explanation: 6 rows of 4 is 24, so 24 shared into 6 rows is 4 in each.
Feedback A: 4 + 6 is 10. 4 rows of 6 is still 24.
Feedback B: Check with the array: 4 rows of 6, not 8.
Feedback C: Yes. One fact gave you another.

**F6 (Sapling, number)**

## "I'm stuck" worked examples

Step 2 of the ladder: an easier case, then how to start this one.

- **Know and want items:** "Pip has some acorns. He finds more. He wants to know how many now. Start: what does your puzzle want?"
- **Can't tell items:** "How many fish are under the bridge? Nobody can see. Start: is anything missing from your puzzle?"
- **Tool items:** "2 + 1 fits on fingers. 9 + 6 needs a ten frame. Start: are your numbers small or big?"
- **Model items:** "Dot had 3 hats. She gave 1 away: 3 counters, take 1, 2 hats left. Start: does your story add or take away?"
- **Spot and story items:** "A tree with birds: how many birds? 2 + 1: 2 birds, 1 more lands. Start: what could you count?"
- **Meaning items:** "4 − 1 = 3: 4 cups, 1 broke, 3 cups left. Start: what is your number counting?"
- **Four ways items:** "2 + 1: 2 counters and 1 more; start at 2, jump 1, land on 3. Start: where does your story start?"
- **Show me items:** "Pip drew 2 rings, then 2 more, and counted 4. Start: what did you do first?"
- **Quick way items:** "5 + 1: start at 5, one hop, 6. Start: which number is bigger?"
- **Pattern items:** "10, 20, 30: the tens digit goes up one each time. Start: which digit is changing?"
- **Long job items:** "4 cups, 1 washed, 1 more washed: 1 + 1 = 2 washed, 4 − 2 = 2 left. Start: what do you find first?"

Step 3: **Skip for now.** You can come back any time.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **puzzle**: a little story with a question in it.
- **know**: what the puzzle tells us.
- **want**: what the puzzle asks us to find.
- **not needed**: a true thing that doesn't help answer the question.
- **retell**: tell the story back in your own words.
- **tool**: something that helps you think, like counters, fingers or a ten frame.
- **ten frame**: a box with ten spaces in two rows of five.
- **number sentence**: numbers and signs that tell a story, like 3 + 4 = 7.
- **number line**: numbers in order on a line, for jumping on or back.
- **bar picture (bar model)**: a long bar for the whole, with shorter bars for the parts.
- **working**: what you did to get an answer, shown with objects, drawings or words.
- **because**: the word that starts a reason.
- **count on**: start at one number and count up from there.
- **pattern**: something that repeats, so you can predict it.
- **look back**: check that your answer makes sense in the story.
- **token**: a small clay shape that stood for one thing, long ago.

## Off-screen invitation (optional, grown-up-led)

A card in [spec 128](https://github.com/anotherpanacea-eng/learning-garden/pull/128)'s format, id `garden-puzzle-story`, from `#garden/puzzles`.

**Title:** A story for a sum
**Mission:** Pick a number sentence, like 3 + 4. Find spoons, socks or blocks to act it out. Tell a grown-up the story your objects tell.
**Grown-up line:** Choose safe, large objects. Listen to the story; there is nothing to check or write down.
**Come-back question:** What else could 3 + 4 be a story about?
**Needs:** a grown-up.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** reading a word problem as a story before computing; sorting what we know, what we want and what is not needed; saying "we can't tell yet" when a piece is missing; choosing a tool that fits; making objects act out the story; spotting math in a scene and telling a story for a number sentence; saying what an answer means, with its words; matching one story across counters, picture, number line, number sentence and bar; showing working (a drawing counts); giving a reason with "because" and finding a slip; quicker ways (count on, swap the order, jump a ten, one fact gives more); planning and checking two-step problems.

**What it does not claim:** spoken explanations are never recorded, stored or graded; the game keeps only taps and stamps. It does not teach addition and subtraction facts, number bonds or word-problem types as topics (other packs do). It does not judge whether a child "can" explain; a quiet child may be reasoning well.

**Sources in brief:** George Pólya's *How to Solve It* (1945) via encyclopedia and university summaries; Near Eastern clay tokens via summaries of Denise Schmandt-Besserat's work, with the debate about writing noted. Teaching approach from the Marble teaching-research records for these topics (paraphrased). Full list in the source audit.
