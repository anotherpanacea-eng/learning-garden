# Breath and Heartbeat: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 174](../specs/174-breath-and-heartbeat.md). Use as written, after independent review and a physiology-fact check. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion. Pip is a fox (he/him), Dot a ladybug (she/her), Bramble she/her, the librarian they/them, the baker he/him, the miller she/her. Nothing here is scored.

**House rules for this text:** every body fact is about "a person", a cast animal or a model. No line asks about the explorer's own body, breath, pulse, temperature, health or family. The "you" in prompts is the explorer doing the task.

## The story

### Scene 1. Little fires? (Winter Lantern Night)

**Hook:** Lanterns glow on the bridge. Every breath makes a small white cloud. Pip has a worrying idea.

**Short:**
> Pip puffs a white cloud. "Is that smoke? Do I have a fire inside?" The librarian smiles. "Something like one. Slow, with no flames."

**Longer:**
> The lanterns are lit along the bridge, and the brook is black and quiet.
>
> Pip breathes out. A small white cloud floats away. He does it again, then stops.
>
> "Is that smoke?" he whispers. "Do I have a *fire* inside?"
>
> "Don't be silly," says Bramble. Her magpie fluffs up against the cold. Then she watches her own cloud drift off, and says nothing more.
>
> Ember peeks out of the café door. "Mine are only for birthdays," Ember says, and a spark pops.
>
> The librarian pulls their scarf higher. "No smoke, Pip. That cloud is tiny drops of water from warm breath, meeting cold air. But you're not wrong about the fire. Inside every living thing, something slow is going on. It uses air, and it gives off warmth."
>
> {guide} looks at the lanterns. "Can we see how?"
>
> "Tomorrow," says the librarian. "With the big book, by the stove."

**Keeper's note:**
> Over the winter of 1782 to 1783, two French scientists, Antoine Lavoisier and Pierre-Simon Laplace, set a guinea pig in a box packed with ice. They measured the ice it melted and the gas it breathed out, and compared them with burning charcoal. Their verdict: breathing animals run a slow kind of burning. Later scientists found it is gentler and happens in many small steps.

*Ember's sparks are story magic. Real bodies make no flames.*

### Scene 2. Drip or pant? (Summer Market Fair)

**Hook:** It is the hottest market day of the year. Pip has run parcels all morning. The baker's oven is roaring.

**Short:**
> Pip flops in the shade, tongue out, panting. The baker mops his dripping forehead. Dot frowns. "Why does one drip and one pant?"

**Longer:**
> The Market Fair is loud and bright. Pip has carried eleven parcels for Dot, at a run.
>
> Now he lies flat in the shade of the miller's pump, tongue out, panting.
>
> By the oven, the baker wipes his forehead with a cloth. Little drops shine on his arms.
>
> Dot lands on the pump handle and lifts her hat. "Pip pants. The baker drips. Which of you is doing it right?"
>
> "Both," says the miller, working the pump. Cold water gushes into a bucket. Pip drinks half of it.
>
> "Both what?" says Dot.
>
> "Both cooling down," says {guide}. "In different ways."
>
> The miller pats the pump. "And this old thing has a trick too. A little leather door inside. Without it, the water would slide straight back down."
>
> Dot looks from the pump to Pip. "Hearts have doors too," she says. "I read it in the librarian's book."

**Keeper's note:**
> People cool down mainly when sweat evaporates from the skin. Dogs cool mainly by panting: water evaporates from the tongue and mouth. Foxes pant too, and lose heat through their ears and legs. Both tricks use the same idea: water turning to vapor carries heat away.

## (a) Spreading Glass

### Teach

**Card 1:** On the café counter is a still glass of water. The baker adds one drop of beet juice.

**Card 2:** Nobody stirs. Slowly, the red spreads until the whole glass is pink.

**Card 3:** Water and juice are made of tiny bits, called particles. They jiggle and wander all the time.

**Card 4:** Where there are lots of red bits, more wander out than wander in. So red spreads. This is **diffusion**.

**Card 5:** Nothing pushes the bits. They don't want anything. They just wander.

**Worked example, one tap per step: 12 dots**
1. All 12 red dots start on the left.
2. Step: 3 dots wander right. None can wander left yet.
3. Step: 2 more wander right. 1 wanders back left.
4. More cross right than left. The red is spreading.

**Card 6 (Sapling):** When both sides match, dots still cross both ways. Just as many go each way.

**Card 7 (Sapling):** Spreading is faster with a bigger difference, a wider gap, a shorter trip or warmer water.

**Card 8 (Sapling, recall from Small Worlds):** A cell's membrane is a thin edge. Small bits like oxygen can diffuse through it.

### Guided item

**Prompt:** The left side has 10 dots. The right has 2. Which side loses more dots?
**Visible steps:**
1. Count the dots on each side.
2. Each dot wanders at random.
3. More dots means more chances to wander out.

Choices: The right side · The left side (fits) · Neither side
- The left side: Yes. More dots there, so more wander out.
- The right side `swap-sides`: The right has only 2 dots. Fewer dots, fewer to wander away.
- Neither side `nothing-moves`: The dots keep wandering. Watch which side has more to lose.

### Practice

**Toggle labels (Sapling):** More dots · Wider gaps · Warmer glass · Back to the start
**After a toggle run:** Count the steps until the sides look even. Was it faster?

## (b) Air Sac

### Teach

**Card 1:** Air goes down the windpipe. It splits into two tubes, one for each lung.

**Card 2:** The tubes split again and again. They end in bunches of tiny air sacs.

**Card 3:** Each air sac has a thin wall. Tiny blood vessels wrap around it.

**Card 4:** Oxygen diffuses from the air sac into the blood. Carbon dioxide diffuses out, into the air sac.

**Card 5:** This swap is called **gas exchange**. It happens at the air sacs, not in the tubes.

**Card 6 (Sapling):** Blood keeps flowing past. It carries oxygen away, so more oxygen can keep crossing in.

**Card 7 (Sapling, pressure):** When the chest space grows, the air inside spreads thinner. Outside air pushes in.

**Card 8:** Three different words. **Breathing** moves air in and out. **Gas exchange** swaps gases. **Respiration** happens in cells.

**Worked example, one tap per step: one step at the air sac**
1. The air sac is full of oxygen dots. The blood has few.
2. The blood has lots of carbon dioxide dots. The air sac has few.
3. Step: oxygen dots cross into the blood.
4. Carbon dioxide dots cross into the air sac. A swap!

### Guided item

**Prompt:** Which way does carbon dioxide go at the air sac?
**Visible steps:**
1. Find where carbon dioxide is crowded.
2. Crowded bits spread to where there are fewer.

Choices: From the air sac into the blood · Nowhere: only oxygen moves · From the blood into the air sac (fits)
- From the blood into the air sac: Yes. Then it is breathed out.
- From the air sac into the blood `swap-sides`: That is oxygen's way. Carbon dioxide is crowded in the blood.
- Nowhere: only oxygen moves `only-oxygen`: Two gases cross, in opposite directions.

### Who breathes how? (Animal note)

**Prompt:** Tap an animal, then where its oxygen gets in.

| Animal | Fits | Feedback |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A person | Lungs | Air sacs deep in two lungs. |
| A fish | Gills | Gills take oxygen from water. |
| An earthworm | Damp skin | Oxygen diffuses through its moist skin. |
| Dot, a ladybug | Tiny holes and tubes | Air goes in at holes on her sides, then down tubes. |

**After matching:** An earthworm has five pairs of pumping tubes. Some people call them five hearts.

## (c) Four-Room Heart

### Teach

**Card 1:** The miller's pump has a leather flap inside. Water can push it open going up.

**Card 2:** Water can't push it open coming down. The flap is a one-way door.

**Worked example, one tap per step: the pump**
1. Push the handle. Water rises past the flap.
2. Lift the handle. The flap shuts. The water stays up.
3. Take out the flap. Push again.
4. Lift: the water slides back down. The door mattered.

**Card 3:** The heart is two pumps side by side. Each side has two rooms, called chambers.

**Card 4:** The top rooms, **atria**, collect blood. The bottom rooms, **ventricles**, squeeze it out.

**Card 5:** Four **valves** are one-way doors. They keep blood moving forward.

**Card 6:** The right side sends blood to the lungs. The left side sends it to the body.

**Card 7:** The heart pumps blood. The lungs give it oxygen.

**Card 8:** **Arteries** carry blood away from the heart. **Veins** bring it back. Remember: artery, away.

**Card 9:** **Capillaries** are the tiniest vessels. Their walls are one cell thick, so things can cross.

**Card 10:** A little over half of blood is a liquid called plasma. It is mostly water and carries food, like glucose. Red cells carry oxygen.

**Card 11 (Keeper's):** The left ventricle has the thickest wall. It pushes blood all the way round the body.

**Card 12 (Keeper's):** Blood slows in the lungs. Coming back to the heart gives it a second strong push.

**Card 13 (Keeper's, history):** Ibn al-Nafis described blood crossing the lungs in the 1200s. Harvey showed it circles, in 1628.

**Diagram code card:** Blood is always red: bright red with more oxygen, dark red with less. Blue is only a code.

### Guided item

**Prompt:** A drop comes back from the body. Which room does it enter first?
**Visible steps:**
1. Blood from the body needs oxygen.
2. Oxygen comes from the lungs.
3. Which side pumps to the lungs?
4. Top rooms collect.

Choices: Right atrium (fits) · Left ventricle · Right ventricle
- Right atrium: Yes. The right side's top room collects it first.
- Left ventricle `one-loop`: The left side sends blood to the body. This drop needs the lungs first.
- Right ventricle `valves-pump`: Close. Top rooms collect first, then the bottom room squeezes.

### Practice: both loops

**Step button:** Squeeze
**Captions:** Right atrium · Right ventricle · Pulmonary artery, to the lungs · Lungs: oxygen on, carbon dioxide off · Pulmonary vein · Left atrium · Left ventricle · Aorta, to the body · Body: oxygen off, carbon dioxide on · Vena cava, back home
**After both loops:** Twice through the heart for each trip round. That is a double loop.

**Fish compare (Animal note):** A fish heart has two rooms and one loop: heart, gills, body, heart. Blood goes round once per trip.

## (d) Slow Fire, Steady Warm

### Teach: the slow fire

**Card 1:** The café stove burns wood with air. It gives off heat, carbon dioxide and water vapor.

**Card 2:** Every living cell does something like this with food. It is called **respiration**.

**Card 3:** Cells use glucose, a sugar from food, and oxygen. Out come carbon dioxide and water.

**Card 4:** Energy is **released**, not made. It was stored in the glucose.

**Card 5:** Respiration has no flames. It happens slowly, in tiny steps, inside cells.

**Card 6:** Plants respire too, day and night. So do the orchard trees and the willows.

**Card 7 (Sapling):** Respiration happens mostly in tiny cell parts called mitochondria.

**Worked example, one tap per step: build the word equation**
1. What goes into the stove? Wood and air.
2. What goes into a cell? Glucose and oxygen.
3. What comes out? Carbon dioxide and water.
4. glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water (energy released)

**Stove or cell? sort:** Uses oxygen (Both) · Makes carbon dioxide (Both) · Has a flame (Stove) · Happens inside cells (Cell) · Releases heat (Both) · Works at body warmth (Cell)

### Teach: steady warm

**Card 8:** Birds and mammals keep their bodies warm and steady, even in snow or sun.

**Card 9:** Inside, a person's body stays close to one warm temperature. Scientists give a typical value of about 37 °C. People differ a little.

**Card 10:** Too hot? Sweat evaporates and carries heat away. Skin blood vessels widen to let heat out.

**Card 11:** Too cold? Muscles shiver and release heat. Skin blood vessels narrow to keep heat in.

**Card 12:** Each response pushes the other way from the change. That is how the body stays steady.

**Card 13:** Vessels don't move closer to the skin. They get wider or narrower where they are.

**Card 14 (Animal note):** A lizard makes little heat inside. It basks in sun to warm, and finds shade to cool.

### Guided item

**Prompt:** Pip's race nudges the dial up. Which card brings it back?
**Visible steps:**
1. The needle went up: too warm.
2. Pick a response that cools.

Choices: Shiver · Skin vessels narrow · Sweat evaporates (fits)
- Sweat evaporates: Yes. Evaporating water takes heat away. The needle drops.
- Shiver `wrong-way`: Shivering warms. The needle goes up even more.
- Skin vessels narrow `wrong-way`: That keeps heat in. Try one that lets heat out.

**Dial captions:** Too warm · Too cool · Steady again · That pushed the same way as the change.
**Lizard panel captions:** Basking rock: warming up · Shade: cooling down

- Hint: Remember, nobody touched the glass.

**A2 (Sprout, match).** Match each animal to where its oxygen gets in.
- Answer: person, lungs; fish, gills; earthworm, damp skin; ladybug, tiny holes and tubes.
- Tags: giving every animal lungs `all-have-lungs`.

- Explanation: Animals take in oxygen in different ways. Each has a thin, damp surface.

**A3 (Seedling, 3-choice, model).** 12 dots start on the left. After many steps, what is most likely?
- About 6 on each side (fits): Yes. They spread out until the sides are about even.
- All 12 on the right `swap-sides`: Dots wander both ways. They don't all pile up on one side.
- Still 12 on the left `nothing-moves`: The dots keep wandering, so some cross over.
- Hint: Where does the model stop changing much?
- Explanation: Random wandering evens things out. Each run gives a slightly different count.

**A4 (Seedling, true or not true).** "When both sides are even, the dots stop moving."
- Answer: Not true `diffusion-stops`.
- Feedback (true): Watch the arrows. Dots still cross, the same number each way.
- Feedback (not true): Right. They keep moving. The sides just stay even.
- Hint: Look at the crossing arrows after the sides match.
- Explanation: Particles never stop. When sides match, as many cross each way.

**A5 (Sapling, 3-choice).** Blood keeps flowing past the air sac. Why does that help?
- It pushes oxygen in `particles-pushed`: Nothing pushes oxygen. It diffuses.
- It brings in oxygen for the air sac `blood-brings-oxygen`: Oxygen comes from the air, then goes into the blood.
- It keeps blood oxygen low (fits): Yes. It carries oxygen away, so more keeps crossing in.
- Hint: What makes diffusion keep going?
- Explanation: Moving blood keeps a big difference between air and blood. Big differences diffuse fast.

**A6 (Sapling, number).** Setup card: two lungs hold about 480 million air sacs.
**Prompt:** Shared equally, about how many air sacs is that per lung?
- Answer: about 240 million.
- Worked steps: 1) Two lungs. 2) Share 480 million into 2 equal parts. 3) 480 ÷ 2 = 240. 4) About 240 million each.
- Tags: adding instead of dividing `add-not-divide`.
- Hint: Share into two equal groups.
- Explanation: 480 million is one study's average. Real lungs vary. The right lung, with three lobes, is a bit bigger than the left.

**A7 (Sapling, sort).** Sort each card: Breathing, Gas exchange or Respiration.
- Answer: "Chest muscles move air in and out" is Breathing; "Oxygen crosses into blood at the air sacs" is Gas exchange; "Cells release energy from glucose" is Respiration.
- Tags: putting the cell card under Breathing `breathing-is-respiration`.
- Hint: Where does each one happen: chest, air sacs or cells?
- Explanation: Three steps, three places. They work together, but they are different things.

**A8 (Keeper's Desk, number).** Setup card: an example figure says all the air sacs together have about 70 m² of surface. The café floor is 7 m by 5 m.
**Prompt:** How many café floors would that surface cover?
- Answer: 2.
- Worked steps: 1) Café floor: 7 × 5 = 35 m². 2) 70 ÷ 35 = 2. 3) About two café floors.
- Tags: adding 7 + 5 for the area `units-slip`.
- Hint: Find the floor's area first.
- Explanation: Folding a huge surface into a chest speeds up diffusion. Estimates range from about 50 to 100 m²; 70 is an example. The café floor is made up.

### Set B. The four-room heart

**B1 (Sprout, 3-choice).** What happens to blood after it leaves the heart?
- It comes back again (fits): Yes. Blood goes round and round.
- It gets used up `used-up`: Blood isn't used up. It drops off oxygen and comes back for more.
- It stays inside the heart `blood-stays`: The heart pumps blood out to the whole body.
- Hint: Think of the miller's water going round the mill.
- Explanation: Blood travels in a loop. It is refilled with oxygen each time round.

**B2 (Seedling, 3-choice).** An artery carries blood in which direction?
- Toward the heart `artery-vein-swap`: That's a vein. Artery, away.
- Away from the heart (fits): Yes. Artery, away.
- Only where there's oxygen `artery-means-red`: Arteries are named by direction. One artery carries oxygen-poor blood.
- Hint: Artery and away start with the same letter.
- Explanation: Arteries carry blood away from the heart; veins carry it back.

**B3 (Seedling, 3-choice).** Which vessel has walls only one cell thick?
- A main artery `thick-everywhere`: Artery walls are thick and stretchy, to take strong pushes.
- A vein `vein-thinnest`: Veins have thinner walls than arteries, but more than one cell.
- A capillary (fits): Yes. Thin walls let oxygen and food cross.
- Hint: Where do things cross between blood and cells?
- Explanation: Capillaries are the tiniest vessels. Their thin walls let things pass in and out.

**B4 (Sapling, order).** Put the drop's trip in order, starting from the body.
- Answer: right atrium, right ventricle, lungs, left atrium, left ventricle, body.
- Tags: going from the right side straight to the body `one-loop`.
- Hint: Lungs come between the two sides.
- Explanation: The right side pumps to the lungs. The left side pumps to the body.

**B5 (Sapling, 3-choice).** What do the heart's valves do?
- Keep blood one way (fits): Yes. Like the miller's flap, they stop blood flowing backward.
- Add oxygen to blood `heart-adds-oxygen`: The lungs add oxygen. Valves are doors.
- Squeeze the blood `valves-pump`: The chambers squeeze. Valves open and shut.
- Hint: Remember the pump without its flap.
- Explanation: Valves are one-way doors. Without them, blood would slosh back.

**B6 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice).** Why does the left ventricle have the thickest wall?
- It adds oxygen to blood `heart-adds-oxygen`: The lungs add oxygen. The heart pumps.
- It holds much more blood `left-holds-more`: Both ventricles hold about the same. The difference is the push.
- It pushes blood farthest (fits): Yes. It sends blood all round the body, so it needs more force.
- Hint: Compare where each ventricle sends blood.
- Explanation: The right side pushes blood a short way to the lungs. The left pushes it everywhere else.

**B7 (Keeper's Desk, number).** Setup card: an example grown-up has 5 liters of blood. At rest the heart pumps 5 liters a minute; in hard work, 20 liters a minute.
**Prompt:** On average, how long does one trip round take in each case?
- Answer: about 1 minute at rest; about 15 seconds in hard work.
- Worked steps: 1) 5 ÷ 5 = 1 minute. 2) 5 ÷ 20 = 0.25 minute. 3) 0.25 × 60 = 15 seconds.
- Tags: multiplying instead of dividing `add-not-divide`; leaving 0.25 as seconds `units-slip`.
- Hint: How many minutes to pump all 5 liters?
- Explanation: These are round example numbers. A faster heart sends oxygen to working muscles sooner.

**B8 (Keeper's Desk, say why, grown-up reads).** Setup card: a fish's blood passes through its heart once per trip. A person's passes through twice.
**Prompt:** Why might going through twice help?
- Listen for: blood slows in the lungs (or gills); a second push sends it to the body strongly.
- Hint: What happens to the push after the lungs?
- Explanation: The second trip through the heart gives the blood a fresh, strong push.

**C8 (Keeper's Desk, number).** Setup card (A true thing from history): scientists measure many people and report a typical value. In 1868 Carl Wunderlich reported about 37 °C. A 2017 study of over 35,000 people reported about 36.6 °C. To change °C to °F: multiply by 9, divide by 5, add 32.

## Key terms

- **Particle:** a tiny bit that things are made of. Far too small to see.
- **Diffusion:** particles spreading from crowded to less crowded places, by wandering.
- **Air sac (alveolus):** a tiny bubble at the end of the lung's tubes.
- **Gas exchange:** oxygen into the blood, carbon dioxide out, at the air sacs.
- **Breathing:** moving air in and out of the lungs.
- **Respiration:** cells releasing energy from glucose using oxygen.
- **Glucose:** a sugar the body gets from food.
- **Organ:** one body part with a job, like the heart.
- **Organ system:** organs working together as a team, like the circulatory system.
- **Circulatory system:** heart, blood vessels and blood.
- **Respiratory system:** windpipe, lungs and breathing muscles.
- **Atrium (atria):** a top heart room that collects blood.
- **Ventricle:** a bottom heart room that squeezes blood out.
- **Valve:** a one-way door.
- **Artery:** carries blood away from the heart.
- **Vein:** carries blood back to the heart.
- **Capillary:** the tiniest blood vessel, one cell thick.
- **Plasma:** the liquid part of blood.
- **Coronary arteries (Keeper's):** vessels that feed the heart's own muscle.
- **Evaporate:** turn from liquid into invisible vapor.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** how particles spread (diffusion); how gases cross at the lungs' air sacs; the heart's four chambers, valves and double loop; arteries, veins and capillaries; respiration as cells releasing energy from glucose; how birds and mammals hold a steady temperature, compared with a lizard.

**What it does not claim:** the models are simplified and say so. Nothing here is health advice, and nothing measures anyone. The pack has no pulse, breathing or temperature activity; spec 148 has one optional grown-up-led pulse card. Blood also contains white cells, which help defend the body; the pack leaves them out on purpose. Enzymes, which work best near body temperature, are mentioned only here.

**History notes:** the Lavoisier and Laplace experiment (winter 1782 to 1783) is told briefly, as the setup only. The Wunderlich card shows how scientists measured a typical value and later revised it; it is not a standard to check anyone against, and nothing in the pack asks anyone to take a temperature.
