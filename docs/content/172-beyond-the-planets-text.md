# Beyond the Planets: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 172](../specs/172-beyond-the-planets.md). Use as written, after independent review and a subject check. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion and is never gendered. Pip is a fox (he/him), Dot a ladybug (she/her), Bramble a girl witch (she/her) with a magpie, Ember a pocket-dragon, the orchard keeper she/her, Wren Halloway she/her. The librarian is they/them. Nothing here is scored.

**Labels used on every card:** *A story from Willowbank* (fiction), *A true thing about space* or *A true thing from history* (checked fact), *A story people told* (myths and gods), *A made-up model* (bench results).

## Arriving

**Place title:** The Far Window

**Hook (first visit, 30 words max):**
> Up a short stair from the library roof is a round room with one big window. A star map covers the wall. Someone has left lanterns on the bench.

**Room labels:** Word Wall · Planet Table · Newton's Hill · Lantern Lane · The Long Zoom · Star Story

**Sun rule card (first visit, and wherever the Sun is drawn):**
> **Never look at the Sun.** Not with sunglasses, not through clouds, never through binoculars or a telescope. The Sun is a star, and it can hurt your eyes for good. Stars at night are safe to look at, with a grown-up.

## The story

### Scene 1. Ember on the bridge *(A story from Willowbank)*

**Short:**
> On Lantern Night, Bramble says the brightest star is the biggest. Ember walks to the far end of the bridge and looks dim. Ember's glow never changed.

**Longer:**
> It is Lantern Night, and the bridge lanterns are dimmed for the stars.
>
> "That one's the brightest," says Bramble, pointing. "So it's the biggest. Obviously."
>
> Her magpie agrees loudly. The magpie agrees with everything shiny.
>
> "Ember, stand here," says {guide}. Ember glows beside them, bright as a lamp. "Now walk to the far end."
>
> Ember trots off across the bridge. By the far rail Ember is a small, soft dot.
>
> "You've gone dim!" Pip calls.
>
> "I have not," Ember calls back, offended. "I'm glowing exactly the same."
>
> Dot adjusts her hat. "Then something else changed."
>
> "Same glow," says {guide}. "Ember just got farther away."
>
> Bramble looks up at her star and says nothing for a whole minute.

**Keeper's note:**
> Bramble's star might be big, or close, or both. Its brightness alone can't say which. Astronomers need another way to measure distance, and finding one took a long time.

### Scene 2. Two Tuesdays *(A story from Willowbank, with a true thing about words)*

**Short:**
> Nuria writes that her letter left on *martes*. Léon's left on *mardi*. Both words come from Mars. Pip asks which day is named for pie.

**Longer:**
> Dot brings two letters up the stair, one in each hand.
>
> Nuria writes from Vadoalto: *I posted this on martes. Our teacher says martes comes from Mars, the red planet.*
>
> Léon writes from Les Saules: *Mine went on mardi. Same planet! And mercredi is Mercury.*
>
> "Our Tuesday hides it," says the librarian. "Spanish and French kept the old Roman names: five planets, plus the Moon."
>
> {guide} lines the letters up. "So *jueves* and *jeudi* are…"
>
> "Jupiter," says the librarian.
>
> Pip has been listening hard. "Is any day named for pie?"
>
> "No," says Dot. "But you can write and ask."

**Keeper's note:**
> The Romans named days after the Sun, the Moon and the five planets they could see. English swapped some for gods from northern stories.

### Scene 3. The orchard of all ages *(A story from Willowbank)*

**Short:**
> Pip worries the Sun is half used up. The orchard keeper shows him seedlings, young trees and old trees, all at once. "That's how you read a star."

**Longer:**
> "The book says the Sun is halfway through," says Pip. "Halfway through what? Is it half eaten?"
>
> The orchard keeper laughs and takes him outside. Her orchard has seedlings in pots, young trees, tall trees and one old tree held up by a prop.
>
> "Nobody here has watched one tree for a hundred years," she says. "But look along the row. You can see a whole tree's life in one morning."
>
> "Stars live much longer than trees," says {guide}. "Billions of years."
>
> "So astronomers look at lots of stars," says the librarian, "young, middle and old, and put the story together."
>
> Pip looks up. "And the Sun's in the middle part?"
>
> "The long, steady middle," says the librarian. "It'll be the same Sun for longer than anyone can count."
>
> Then Pip asks if the old tree has any apples.

**Keeper's note:**
> The Sun formed about 4.6 billion years ago. In about 5 billion more years it will swell into a red giant, then shrink to a small white dwarf. It is too small to explode.

### Scene 4. Wren's ledger *(Keeper's Desk; A story from Willowbank, with a true thing from history)*

**Short:**
> Wren opens a ledger of rows and numbers. "Someone once measured stars like this, row after row," she says. "It helped show how far the galaxies are."

**Longer:**
> Wren Halloway sets a ledger on the Keeper's Desk, every page neat rows of numbers. "Ledgers look dull," she says. "Until somebody notices a pattern."
>
> She opens a library book beside it. It shows a woman at a desk with glass plates covered in tiny stars.
>
> "Henrietta Leavitt," says the librarian. "She measured how bright stars were, on thousands of plates. She noticed that stars that pulse more slowly are brighter."
>
> "That's just a pattern," says Bramble.
>
> "That pattern became a ruler," says Wren. "Years later, someone used it to measure a far-off smudge of light. It was a whole other galaxy."
>
> {guide} turns a page of the ledger. "So keeping careful rows can change everything."
>
> "Sometimes," says Wren. "Mostly it's just careful. That's the point."

**Keeper's note:**
> Wren's ledger is fiction. Leavitt's work is real and is on the history cards. The 1912 paper with her result was signed by the observatory's director, and says she prepared it.

## Teach cards

### Word Wall

**Teach 1 (A true thing about space):** A star makes its own light. The Sun is our star.

**Teach 2:** A planet goes around a star. A moon goes around a planet.
*Worked example, tap by tap:* 1. The Moon goes around Earth. 2. Earth goes around the Sun. 3. So the Moon is a moon, and Earth is a planet.

**Teach 3:** A shooting star isn't a star. It's a tiny bit of space rock, glowing as it rushes into the air.

**Teach 4:** Everything nests. Earth is in the solar system. The solar system is in the Milky Way. The Milky Way is in the universe.

**Teach 5 (Seedling):** Space pictures squash things together. Real planets are far apart and very different sizes.

**Word cards (one feature each):**
- **Star:** makes its own light.
- **Planet:** goes around a star; big and round.
- **Moon:** goes around a planet.
- **Dwarf planet:** round, goes around the Sun, shares its path with other things.
- **Asteroid:** a small rocky body; most are between Mars and Jupiter.
- **Comet:** a ball of ice and dust; near the Sun it grows a tail.
- **Meteor:** a "shooting star": a speck of rock glowing in the air.
- **Galaxy:** a huge group of stars, gas and dust.
- **Universe:** everything there is.

### Names *(A true thing from history; gods are A story people told)*

**Teach 6:** Five planets you can see without a telescope have Roman gods' names: Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn.

**Teach 7:** Spanish and French weekdays keep the old Roman names for the Moon and planets.

**Weekday table:**

| English | Spanish | French | Named for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Monday | lunes | lundi | the Moon |
| Tuesday | martes | mardi | Mars |
| Wednesday | miércoles | mercredi | Mercury |
| Thursday | jueves | jeudi | Jupiter |
| Friday | viernes | vendredi | Venus |

**Uranus card:** In 1781 William Herschel found a new planet and wanted to name it after King George. Johann Bode suggested Uranus, the sky in Greek stories.

**Neptune card:** Neptune, found in 1846, is named for a Roman god of the sea.

**Keeper's note:** *Planet* comes from a Greek word for "wanderer". Planets drift slowly against the stars.

### Planet Table

**Teach 8 (A true thing about space):** The farther a planet is from the Sun, the longer its year.
*Worked example:* 1. Earth: 1 year. 2. Mars is farther: about 2 Earth years. 3. Neptune is farthest: about 165 Earth years.

**Teach 9 (Sapling):** Faraway planets have a longer path and move more slowly along it. Both make the year longer.

**Teach 10 (Sapling):** The solar system has more than planets: dwarf planets, asteroids and comets.

**Teach 11 (Sapling):** Three tests for a planet: it goes around the Sun, it's round, and it has cleared its path.
*Worked example:* 1. Ceres goes around the Sun: yes. 2. Ceres is round: yes. 3. Ceres shares its path with many asteroids: no. 4. So Ceres is a dwarf planet.

**Teach 12 (Sapling):** A comet's tail points away from the Sun. Leaving the Sun, the comet goes tail first.

**Planet Table card lines:** Mercury: 0.4, a year of 88 days · Venus: 0.7, 225 days · Earth: 1, 1 year · Mars: 1.5, 687 days · Jupiter: 5.2, about 12 years · Saturn: 9.6, about 29 years · Uranus: 19, 84 years · Neptune: 30, about 165 years. *(Distance from the Sun, with Earth's distance as 1.)*

**Asteroid belt card:** The belt is mostly empty space. Spacecraft fly straight through.

**Keeper's note:** In 2006 astronomers voted on what counts as a planet. The "cleared its path" test has no number, so some still argue about it.

**Model line:** Simplified numbers rounded from NASA's table. The chart shows the pattern; real orbits are slightly oval.

### Newton's Hill

**Teach 13 (A true thing about space):** Every bit of stuff pulls on every other bit. That pull is gravity.

**Teach 14:** The same pull drops an apple and keeps the Moon going around Earth.

**Teach 15:** Throw a ball sideways and it curves down. Throw faster, and it lands farther.
*Worked example, tap by tap:* 1. Slow: it lands near the hill. 2. Faster: it lands far away. 3. Just right: the ground curves away as fast as it falls. 4. It falls all the way around. That's an orbit.

**Teach 16:** The space station falls around Earth like this too. Gravity is still there.

**Teach 17 (Sapling):** Mass is how much stuff. Weight is how hard gravity pulls it. The Moon pulls less.
*Worked example:* 1. A flour sack has 30 kg of mass. 2. Take it to the Moon. 3. Still 30 kg of stuff. 4. It feels like lifting about 5 kg.

**Teach 18 (Keeper's Desk):** Weight = mass × g. On Earth g is 9.8. On the Moon it's 1.62.

**Newton card (A true thing from history):** Isaac Newton told a friend, in 1726, that a falling apple once set him wondering. He was remembering about 60 years back.

**Neptune card (A true thing from history, Sapling):** Uranus kept wandering off its path. Urbain Le Verrier worked out where another planet's pull must be. In 1846, astronomers in Berlin looked there and found Neptune.

**Keeper's note:** John Couch Adams in England had done the math too, on his own. People argued about credit for years.

**Model line:** A made-up model: the world is tiny, there is no air, and the paths are drawn to show the idea.

### Lantern Lane

**Teach 19 (A true thing about space):** The same light looks dimmer when it's farther away.
*Worked example:* 1. Ember stands close: bright. 2. Ember walks away: dimmer. 3. Ember's glow didn't change. Only the distance did.

**Teach 20:** How bright it looks isn't how much light it gives. Those are two different things.

**Teach 21:** The Sun looks brightest because it's the nearest star. Many stars give far more light.

**Teach 22 (Keeper's Desk):** Light spreads out. Twice as far, it covers 4 squares. It looks a quarter as bright.
*Worked example:* 1. One step: 1 square. 2. Two steps: 4 squares. 3. Three steps: 9 squares, a ninth as bright.

**Star cards:**
- **Sirius:** the brightest star in our night sky. About 8.6 light-years away. It gives about 25 times the Sun's light.
- **Rigel:** a little fainter to us. It gives tens of thousands of times the Sun's light. It's much farther away: about 850 light-years, though measurements disagree.

**Daytime stars card (Seedling):** Stars are still there in the day. The Sun's light hides them.

**Model line:** A simplified picture: a point of light, no air or fog, made-up strengths.

### The Long Zoom

**Teach 23 (A true thing about space):** A light-year is a distance: how far light travels in one year.
*Worked example:* 1. Light goes about 300,000 km every second. 2. In a year, that adds up. 3. About 9.46 trillion km. That's one light-year.

**Teach 24:** Say it as "away", never "ago". Proxima Centauri is about 4 light-years away.

**Zoom stops (one line each, with light time):**
1. **Earth and Moon:** Moonlight takes about 1.3 seconds to reach you.
2. **The solar system:** Sunlight takes about 8 minutes. Neptune's light takes about 4 hours.
3. **The nearest stars:** Proxima Centauri's light takes about 4 years.
4. **The Milky Way:** About 100,000 light-years across. The Sun is partway out, far from the middle.
5. **Galaxies:** Andromeda's light takes about 2.5 million years. Billions of galaxies lie beyond.

**Teach 25 (Sapling):** Light takes time to get here. So you see Andromeda as it was long ago.

**20 cm Sun panel (building on the Scale Walk):** On the river path, Neptune was 649 m away. At the same scale, the nearest star is about 5,770 km away. That's a little less than from the Pyrenees to New York.

**Milky Way panel (Keeper's Desk):** At that scale, the Milky Way would still be about 136 million km across.

**Leavitt card (A true thing from history):** Henrietta Leavitt measured stars on glass photographs at Harvard. In 1912 her rule was published: slower-pulsing Cepheid stars are brighter.

**Leavitt detail (Sapling):** All her stars were in one star cloud, so about the same distance away. So brighter-looking meant really brighter.

**Hubble card (A true thing from history):** In 1923 Edwin Hubble found a Cepheid in Andromeda. Leavitt's rule showed it was far outside the Milky Way.

**Hubble detail (Keeper's Desk):** Hubble's number was a little under 1 million light-years. Today's is about 2.5 million. The big idea held.

### Star Story

**Teach 26 (A true thing about space):** A star begins as a cloud of gas and dust. Gravity pulls it together.

**Teach 27:** Squeezed tight, the middle gets so hot that tiny bits join and give off energy. That's called fusion. Fire is a different thing.

**Teach 28:** For most of its life, gravity pulls in and the energy pushes out. They balance.

**Teach 29:** A star like the Sun swells into a red giant, then shrinks to a white dwarf.
*Worked example:* 1. Cloud. 2. Young star. 3. Long, steady life. 4. Red giant. 5. White dwarf, cooling slowly.

**Teach 30 (Sapling):** A star's mass decides its ending. Stars about 8 times the Sun's mass or more end in a huge burst, a supernova.

**Teach 31 (Sapling):** Nobody watches one star's whole life. Astronomers look at many stars, at every stage.

**Teach 32 (Keeper's Desk):** Most things heavier than helium were made inside stars. That includes the carbon in living things.

**Payne card (A true thing from history):** In 1925 Cecilia Payne showed that stars are made mostly of hydrogen and helium.

**Payne detail (Keeper's Desk):** A leading expert doubted it. Her thesis called the result "almost certainly not real". Later work showed she was right.

**Model line:** A simplified story: real stars take millions to billions of years, and the pictures are not to scale.

## Problem sets

### Set A. Names and kinds

**A1 · Sprout · 3-choice**
Prompt: Which one goes around Earth?
A. The Moon ✓ · B. The Sun `sun-planet` · C. Mars `moon-planet`
Feedback: A: Yes. The Moon goes around Earth. · B: Earth goes around the Sun. The Sun is our star. · C: Mars goes around the Sun, like Earth.
Hint: Which one is Earth's moon?
Explanation: A moon goes around a planet. The Moon is Earth's moon; Mars is another planet.

**A2 · Sprout · match**
Prompt: Match each word to what it does.
Pairs: star → makes its own light · planet → goes around a star · moon → goes around a planet · comet → grows a tail near the Sun.
Misconception: `sun-planet` (star and planet swapped).
Hint: Which one makes light?
Explanation: Each word names one kind of thing and what it goes around.

**A3 · Seedling · 3-choice**
Prompt: What is a shooting star?
A. A real star falling out of the sky `meteor-star` · B. A bit of rock glowing in the air ✓ · C. A small planet passing close by `moon-planet`
Feedback: A: Stars don't fall. A shooting star is a speck of rock. · B: Yes. It glows as it rushes into the air. · C: Planets move too slowly to see. This is a speck of rock.
Hint: Look at the meteor card.
Explanation: A meteor is a tiny piece of space rock glowing in the air.

**A4 · Seedling · 3-choice**
Prompt: *Martes* and *mardi* are named for what?
A. The sea `sounds-like` · B. The Moon · C. Mars ✓
Feedback: A: *Mar* means sea in Spanish, but this comes from Mars. · B: The Moon's day is *lunes*, *lundi*. · C: Yes. Tuesday is Mars's day in both.
Hint: Look at the weekday table.
Explanation: Spanish and French keep the Roman weekday names: *martes* and *mardi* come from Mars.

**A5 · Seedling · order**
Prompt: Put these in order, smallest to biggest.
Cards: the Milky Way · Earth · the universe · the solar system
Answer: Earth → the solar system → the Milky Way → the universe.
Misconception: solar system, galaxy and universe treated as one thing.
Hint: Which one holds all the others?
Explanation: Each one sits inside the next: a planet, its star's family, a galaxy, everything.

**A6 · Sapling · 3-choice**
Prompt: Ceres is round and goes around the Sun, among many asteroids. What is it?
A. A small planet, like Earth `pluto-small-planet` · B. A dwarf planet ✓ · C. A moon of the Sun `moon-planet`
Feedback: A: It shares its path, so it fails the third test. · B: Yes. Round, around the Sun, but path not cleared. · C: A moon goes around a planet. Ceres goes around the Sun.
Hint: Check the three tests one by one.
Explanation: Ceres passes two planet tests but shares its path with asteroids, so it is a dwarf planet.

**A7 · Sapling · 3-choice**
Prompt: A comet is heading away from the Sun. Which way does its tail point?
A. Behind it, as usual `tail-trails` · B. Toward the Sun · C. Away from the Sun ✓
Feedback: A: A comet's tail isn't like a kite's. The Sun pushes it. · B: The Sun pushes the tail away from itself. · C: Yes. So this comet travels tail first.
Hint: What pushes the tail?
Explanation: Light and particles from the Sun push the tail away from the Sun, whichever way the comet moves.

### Set B. Years, pulls and weight

**B1 · Seedling · 3-choice**
Prompt: Which planet has the longest year?
A. Mercury `distance-no-effect` · B. Jupiter `biggest-slowest` · C. Neptune ✓
Feedback: A: Mercury is nearest, with the shortest year: 88 days. · B: Jupiter is biggest, but Neptune is farther. · C: Yes. Farthest out, about 165 Earth years.
Hint: Find the planet farthest from the Sun.
Explanation: Year length follows distance from the Sun. Size doesn't decide it.

**B2 · Seedling · number with steps**
Prompt: The mill is 60 years old. How many Jupiter years is that?
Shown: one Jupiter year is about 12 Earth years.
Steps: 1. How many 12s make 60? 2. 12 × 5 = 60.
Answer: 5 Jupiter years.
Misconception: multiplying instead of sharing (answers 720).
Hint: Count in twelves.
Explanation: 60 ÷ 12 = 5. With the real 11.9 years, it's still about 5.

**B3 · Sapling · 3-choice**
Prompt: Why is Neptune's year so long?
A. It's the biggest planet, so it's slowest `biggest-slowest` · B. Its path is longer, and it moves slower ✓ · C. It spins slowly, so its year drags `spin-orbit`
Feedback: A: Jupiter is bigger, with a shorter year. · B: Yes. Both make the trip take longer. · C: Spin makes a day. The year is the trip around.
Hint: Think about the length of the path.
Explanation: A farther planet has a longer path and moves more slowly along it, so its year is long.

**B4 · Keeper's Desk · number with steps**
Prompt: An asteroid is 4 times Earth's distance from the Sun. How long is its year?
Rule shown: year × year = distance × distance × distance.
Steps: 1. 4 × 4 × 4 = 64. 2. Which number times itself makes 64? 3. 8 × 8 = 64.
Answer: 8 Earth years.
Misconception: `distance-no-effect` (answers 4: year equals distance).
Hint: Work out the right side first.
Explanation: The rule gives 64 = 8 × 8, so 8 years. It is Kepler's pattern, and it fits all eight planets closely.

**B5 · Sapling · 3-choice**
Prompt: Newton's cannonball goes just fast enough. What happens?
A. It falls all the way around ✓ · B. It floats, because gravity stops `no-gravity-space` · C. It flies straight off into space
Feedback: A: Yes. It keeps falling and keeps missing. · B: Gravity never stops. It keeps pulling it around. · C: That's the "too fast" speed.
Hint: Look at the "just right" path.
Explanation: An orbit is falling sideways fast enough that the ground curves away beneath you.

**B6 · Keeper's Desk · number with steps**
Shown: a flour sack, 30 kg of mass. On the Moon, g = 1.62.
Prompt: On the Moon, what is its mass? What is its weight?
Steps: 1. Mass doesn't change: 30 kg. 2. Weight = mass × g. 3. 30 × 1.62 = 48.6.
Answer: 30 kg of mass; 48.6 newtons of weight (on Earth, 30 × 9.8 = 294 newtons).
Misconception: `mass-is-weight` (mass drops to about 5 kg).
Hint: Which one depends on the place?
Explanation: The stuff stays the same; the Moon pulls about a sixth as hard, so the weight is smaller.

**B7 · Sapling · 3-choice**
Prompt: Does the Moon pull on Earth?
A. Yes, every bit of stuff pulls ✓ · B. No, only Earth is big enough to pull `only-big-pull` · C. Only when the Moon is full
Feedback: A: Yes. Earth pulls the Moon, and the Moon pulls Earth. · B: Small things pull too, just weakly. · C: The pull is always there, every day.
Hint: Look back at Teach 13.
Explanation: Gravity acts between any two things with mass, both ways at once.

### Set C. Light, distance and far stars

**C1 · Seedling · 3-choice**

A. Ember is farther away ✓ · B. Ember's glow got weaker `output-changed` · C. The light ran out on the way `light-runs-out`

**C2 · Seedling · 3-choice**

Hint: Think of Ember close up.
Explanation: The Sun is an ordinary star that is very near. Nearness makes it look brightest.

**C3 · Sapling · 3-choice**
Prompt: Sirius looks brighter than Rigel. What does that tell you for sure?
A. Sirius really gives out more light `look-is-real` · B. Sirius must be the bigger star `bigger-brighter` · C. How bright each looks from here ✓

**C4 · Keeper's Desk · number with steps**
Shown: a lantern moves from 1 step away to 3 steps away.
Prompt: How many squares does its light cover now? How bright does it look?
Steps: 1. 1 step: 1 square. 2. 3 steps: 3 × 3 = 9 squares. 3. Same light, 9 squares.

Misconception: `light-runs-out`; a third as bright (spreading in one direction only).
Hint: The light spreads up-down and side to side.
Explanation: Light spreads in two directions at once, so 3 times as far means 3 × 3 = 9 times the area.

**C5 · Sapling · 3-choice**
Prompt: What is a light-year?
A. A very long time, like a million years `year-is-time` · B. How far light travels in one year ✓ · C. One year's time on another planet
Feedback: A: It sounds like time, but it's a distance. · B: Yes. About 9.46 trillion km. · C: Planet years are just years. This is a distance.
Hint: Say "4 light-years away".
Explanation: A light-year measures how far, like a kilometer, only much bigger.

**C6 · Sapling · number with steps**
Shown: on the 20 cm Sun walk, Neptune is 649 m away. The nearest star is about 5,770 km.
Prompt: About how many times farther is the star?
Steps: 1. Change km to m: 5,770 km = 5,770,000 m. 2. 5,770,000 ÷ 649 ≈ 8,900. 3. Round it.

Misconception: the nearest star sits just past Neptune (answers about 9).
Hint: Make both distances meters first.
Explanation: 5,770,000 ÷ 649 is about 8,900, so roughly 9,000. The gap to the stars dwarfs the whole solar system.

**C7 · Seedling · order**
Prompt: Put a Sun-like star's life in order.
Cards: red giant · cloud of gas and dust · white dwarf · long, steady life · young star
Answer: cloud → young star → long, steady life → red giant → white dwarf.
Misconception: `stars-forever`.
Hint: Where does a star begin?
Explanation: Gravity gathers a cloud into a star, which shines steadily for a long time, swells, then shrinks.

**C8 · Sapling · 3-choice**
Prompt: Will the Sun end as a supernova?
A. No, it will shine just as it is forever `stars-forever` · B. Yes, every star ends in a supernova `all-same-end` · C. No, it's too light to explode ✓

## Key terms

- **Star:** a huge ball of hot gas that makes its own light.
- **Planet:** a big round body that goes around a star and has cleared its path.
- **Dwarf planet:** round and goes around the Sun, but shares its path.
- **Moon:** a body that goes around a planet.
- **Asteroid:** a small rocky body going around the Sun.
- **Comet:** ice and dust; near the Sun it grows a tail pointing away from the Sun.
- **Meteor:** a "shooting star": a speck of space rock glowing in the air.
- **Orbit:** the path of one thing going around another.
- **Gravity:** the pull between everything that has mass.
- **Mass:** how much stuff something has. It doesn't change from place to place.
- **Weight:** how hard gravity pulls on something. It changes from place to place.
- **Light-year:** how far light travels in one year. A distance.
- **Galaxy:** a huge group of stars, gas and dust. Ours is the Milky Way.
- **Universe:** everything there is.
- **Nebula:** a cloud of gas and dust in space.
- **Fusion:** tiny bits joining together in a star's middle, giving off energy.
- **Red giant:** a swollen old star. **White dwarf:** the small hot core left after.
- **Supernova:** the huge burst at the end of a very heavy star's life.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** space words and the nest; sorting bodies; the distance-year pattern; gravity, mass and weight; apparent versus actual brightness; the light-year and the galaxy's scale; a star's life; how Herschel, Le Verrier, Leavitt, Payne and Hubble added to what we know.

**What it does not claim:** star and galaxy counts and Rigel's distance are estimates. Bench results are simplified models: drawn cannonball paths, made-up lantern strengths, rounded table values. Gods and myths are labeled as stories people told. Willowbank, its letters and Wren's ledger are fiction; real people appear only on checked cards with no invented words.

**Sun safety:** never look at the Sun, even briefly, with sunglasses, through cloud, or through any lens, binoculars or telescope. To see the Sun's shape, use a pinhole picture on paper with your back to the Sun.

**Off-screen activity (optional, grown-up led):** in a dark hallway or room, one person holds a small flashlight at one, two and three big steps away while the other watches it look dimmer. It is never shone into anyone's eyes. Nothing from it is recorded.
