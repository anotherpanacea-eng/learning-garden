## Story scenes

### Scene 1. The honey jars (Act III, chapter 1: the floating market)

**Hook:** Two honey jars from Vadoalto. One label says 0.5 kg, one says 0.45 kg. Pip picks the "bigger" number.

**Short:**
> Two jars of honey: 0.5 kg and 0.45 kg. "Forty-five beats five," says Pip. The scale tips the other way.

**Longer:**
> The Vadoalto baker sets two jars of dark honey on the market scale. One label says 0.5 kg. The other says 0.45 kg.
>
> "Easy," says Pip. "Forty-five is way more than five. I'll have the big one."
>
> The baker puts one jar on each pan. The scale tips toward the 0.5 jar and stays there.
>
> Pip stares at it. "The scale is broken."
>
> "The scale is fine," says {guide}. "Read the places. Five tenths, or four tenths and a bit?"
>
> Pip counts on his paws. "Five tenths is more than four tenths." He sighs. "So the short number is the heavy jar."
>
> "This time," says the baker, and gives him a taste of each anyway.

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> 0.5 is five tenths, the same as 0.50, fifty hundredths. 0.45 is forty-five hundredths. Fifty hundredths is more. A longer decimal is not always a bigger one.

### Scene 2. Three bags for ten families (Act III, chapter 2: the seed exchange)

**Hook:** Nuria brings 3 kg of reed seed for ten Willowbank families. Bramble says three can't be shared by ten.

**Short:**
> Nuria has 3 kg of reed seed for ten families. "Three into ten? Impossible," says Bramble. Nuria starts cutting tenths.

**Longer:**
> Nuria opens a sack of reed seed on the landing. "Three kilograms," she says. "One share for each of ten families."
>
> "You can't share three into ten," says Bramble. "Three is smaller. Everyone knows that."
>
> Nuria pours the first kilogram into ten little bags, the same amount in each. "Each bag gets one tenth of a kilogram." Then she does it again. And again.
>
> "Three tenths each," says {guide}. "Zero point three of a kilogram."
>
> Bramble weighs one bag. Then another. "Fine," she says. "But I'm keeping the one with the most seeds in it."
>
> "They're all the same," says Nuria.
>
> "Then I'll keep any of them," says Bramble, with dignity.

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> 3 ÷ 10 = 3/10 = 0.3. Sharing a small number among more people is fine: each share is just less than one.

### Scene 3. Two posts, two numbers (Act III, chapter 4: letters up and down the river)

**Hook:** Nuria's letter says Vadoalto's post reached about 240 centimeters in the storm. Willowbank's reached 160. Dot wants both in meters.

**Short:**
> Nuria writes: about 240 cm at Vadoalto's post. Willowbank's mark is 160 cm. Dot writes both in meters for the wall.

**Longer** (Spanish line for a fluent reader's check):
> *¡Hola desde Vadoalto!* (Hello from Vadoalto!)
>
> Dear friends,
>
> Our post has a mark every 50 centimeters. In the storm the brook came almost to the fifth mark: about 240 centimeters. How high did yours reach?
>
> Nuria
>
> Dot reads it twice. "Ours was 160 centimeters. The wall chart is in meters." She writes 240 cm as 24.0 m and stops. "That's taller than the mill."
>
> "A meter is 100 centimeters," says {guide}. "So share by 100."
>

> 240 ÷ 100 = 2.4 and 160 ÷ 100 = 1.6. The digits move two places; the point stays put. Vadoalto's post read 0.8 m more.

### Scene 4. Hollis's ledger in meters (Act IV, chapter 1: the old map and Hollis's account)

**Hook:** Hollis wrote the flood heights in centimeters. The library chart is in meters. Pip converts, fast.

**Short:**
> Hollis wrote 40 cm, 60 cm, 120 cm. Pip writes 40 cm as 4.0 m. "A tiny flood, as tall as a barn?" asks the librarian.

**Longer:**
> The librarian pins Hollis's flood ledger beside the new chart. The ledger says 40 cm, 60 cm, 120 cm, 80 cm, 120 cm, 140 cm, 100 cm. The chart's side says meters.
>
> "I'll do it," says Pip. He writes 40 cm as 4.0 m.
>
> The librarian raises an eyebrow. "Hollis called that one a small flood. Four meters is higher than the library roof."
>
> Pip looks at the post. "Oh. A meter is a hundred centimeters." He crosses it out. "Forty hundredths of a meter. 0.4 m."
>

> 40 cm ÷ 100 = 0.4 m, not 4.0 m. A bigger unit means a smaller number. Hollis's heights become 0.4, 0.6, 1.2, 0.8, 1.2, 1.4 and 1.0 m.

## Teach cards

Each card is under 30 words. Worked steps appear one tap at a time.

**T1. Tenths, and past one** (A true thing about math)
> A tenth is one of ten equal parts of one whole. Ten tenths make one whole again. Keep counting: one and one tenth comes next.
Worked example: 1. 0.8, 0.9. 2. Next: ten tenths, 1.0, one whole rod. 3. Then 1.1: one rod and one tenth.

**T2. Sharing makes tenths** (A true thing about math)
> Share 3 among 10 and each gets three tenths. 3 ÷ 10 = 3/10 = 0.3.
Worked example: 1. Cut each of the 3 rods into tenths. 2. That makes 30 tenths. 3. 30 tenths among 10: 3 tenths each, 0.3.

**T3. A tenth of a tenth** (A true thing about math)
> Cut one tenth into ten equal parts. Each is a hundredth. More parts means smaller parts. A hundredth is smaller than a tenth.
Worked example: 1. One tenth of the rod. 2. Split it into ten. 3. Each piece: 1/100, written 0.01.

**T4. A tenth of a hundredth** (A true thing about math)
> Cut one hundredth into ten. Each is a thousandth. On a meter rod, a thousandth is one millimeter.
Worked example: 1. 10 thousandths make 1 hundredth. 2. 100 thousandths make 1 tenth. 3. 1000 thousandths make the whole meter.

**T5. Tenths become hundredths** (A true thing about math)
> One tenth is ten hundredths. Change tenths to hundredths before adding. Then the parts are the same size.
Worked example: 1. 3/10 + 4/100. 2. 3/10 = 30/100. 3. 30/100 + 4/100 = 34/100.

**T6. What each digit is worth** (A true thing about math)
> A digit is worth its number times its place. Each place is one tenth of the place to its left.
Worked example: 1. 4.378. 2. The 3 is 3 tenths, 0.3. 3. The 7 is 7 hundredths, 0.07. 4. The 8 is 8 thousandths, 0.008.

**T7. Zeros hold places** (A true thing about math)
> A zero keeps a place open. Read a decimal by its last place: 2.035 is two and thirty-five thousandths.
Worked example: 1. 4 ones, 0 tenths, 7 hundredths, 3 thousandths. 2. Keep the zero: 4.073. 3. Without it, 4.73 means something else.

**T8. Decimal to fraction, and back** (A true thing about math)
> The last place names the bottom number. 0.62 ends in hundredths: 62/100. 0.07 ends in hundredths too: 7/100.
Worked example: 1. 0.025. 2. Last place: thousandths. 3. 25/1000.

**T9. Four friends worth knowing** (A true thing about math)
> On a hundred grid: half is 50 squares, 0.5. A quarter is 25, 0.25. Three quarters is 75, 0.75. A fifth is 20, 0.2.
Worked example (Keeper's Desk): 1. 1/4 = 0.25. 2. 1/8 is half of a quarter. 3. Half of 0.25 is 0.125.

**T10. The fraction bar means divide** (A true thing about math)
> 3/8 means 3 ÷ 8. Top number divided by the bottom. Going back, write the decimal over its last place, then simplify.
Worked example: 1. 3 ÷ 8 = 0.375. 2. 0.375 = 375/1000. 3. Divide both by 125: 3/8.

**T11. Ends, repeats, or neither** (A true thing about math; Keeper's Desk)
> 1/3 = 0.333..., repeating forever. Ending or repeating decimals are fractions: rational. Pi never ends or repeats: irrational.
Worked example: 1. 7/20: 20 is 2 × 2 × 5. 2. Only 2s and 5s: it ends. 3. 7/20 = 0.35.
Keeper's note: √2 is about 1.414, between 1.4 and 1.5 on the line. Its decimals never end or repeat, so we write √2. But √9 is just 3.

**T12. Compare from the left** (A true thing about math)
> Start at the biggest place. The first place that differs decides.
Worked example: 1. $4.75 and $4.57. 2. Dollars: both 4. 3. Tenths: 7 beats 5. 4. $4.75 costs more.

**T13. Longer is not bigger** (A true thing about math)
> More digits does not mean more. Fewer digits does not either. Line up the places and compare from the left.
Worked example: 1. 0.5 and 0.45. 2. Write 0.5 as 0.50. 3. Fifty hundredths beats forty-five: 0.5 is more.

**T14. Line up the points** (A true thing about math)
> To add or take away decimals, line up the points. Fill empty places with zeros. Then work place by place.
Worked example: 1. 2.5 m − 0.75 m. 2. Write 2.50 − 0.75. 3. The answer is 1.75 m.

**T15. Divide by 10 or 100** (A true thing about math)
> Divide by 10: every digit moves one place right. By 100: two places. The point stays still.
Worked example: 1. 37 ÷ 10: 3 tens become 3 ones, 7 ones become 7 tenths: 3.7. 2. 4 ÷ 100: 0.04. The zero holds tenths.

**T16. Changing units** (A true thing about math)
> A bigger unit needs a smaller number. Centimeters to meters: divide by 100. Grams to kilograms: divide by 1000.
Worked example: 1. 235 cm ÷ 100 = 2.35 m. 2. 3250 g ÷ 1000 = 3.25 kg. 3. Check: a bit more than 3 kg. Makes sense.

**T17. Round to the nearer post** (A true thing about math)
> Rounding picks the nearer of two posts. 3.2 is nearer 3. 3.7 is nearer 4. Exactly halfway, we agree to go up.
Worked example: 1. 6.5 sits halfway between 6 and 7. 2. Distance alone can't decide. 3. The agreed rule: up, to 7.

**T18. Round once** (A true thing about math)
> Round in one step from the first number. Only the digit just after your place decides. Keep the place you rounded to.
Worked example: 1. 3.47 to a whole: the 4 tenths says 3. 2. To one place: the 7 says 3.5. 3. 12.95 to one place: 13.0.

**T19. Significant figures** (A true thing about math; Keeper's Desk)
> Count significant figures from the first digit that isn't zero. Zeros in front never count. Round once, then keep the size.
Worked example: 1. 0.04863 to 2 significant figures. 2. The first two are 4 and 8. 3. The 6 rounds up: 0.049.

## Real-world and history cards

**H1. A stroke over the ones** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> In Damascus, around the year 952, al-Uqlidisi wrote an arithmetic book. It is the oldest known book that uses decimal fractions.
Keeper's note:
> To show where the ones digit was, he put a small mark over it. Only one old copy survives, made about 200 years later. We know almost nothing else about his life.

**H2. The Tenth** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> In 1585 Simon Stevin printed a small book called *De Thiende*, "The Tenth". It showed merchants, surveyors and others how to use decimals every day.
Keeper's note:
> Stevin wrote a little circled number after each digit to show its place, so 2.35 looked like 2⓪3①5②. He hoped coins, weights and measures would one day all go in tens. In 1792 the United States named a coin worth one tenth of a dollar the "disme". Today we spell it dime.

**H3. Pi to sixteen places** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> In 1424, in Samarkand, Jamshid al-Kashi worked out two times pi. His answer was right to 16 decimal places.
Keeper's note:
> No one beat that for nearly 200 years. Three years later he finished *The Key of Arithmetic*, a book for students that uses decimal fractions. Later mathematicians proved that pi's decimals never end and never repeat, so 16 places is very close, but never all of it.

Worked steps: 1. 3 ÷ 8: 8 into 3.000. 2. 30 ÷ 8 = 3, 6 left. 60 ÷ 8 = 7, 4 left. 40 ÷ 8 = 5. 3. 0.375.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **tenth**: one of ten equal parts of one whole.
- **hundredth**: one of a hundred equal parts. A tenth cut into ten.
- **thousandth**: one of a thousand equal parts. A hundredth cut into ten.
- **decimal point**: the dot after the ones place.
- **place value**: what a digit is worth because of where it sits.
- **placeholder zero**: a zero that keeps a place open.
- **equivalent**: worth the same amount, written a different way.
- **round**: replace a number with the nearer of two posts.
- **significant figures**: digits counted from the first one that isn't zero.
- **kilogram (kg)**: 1000 grams.
- **meter (m)**: 100 centimeters, or 1000 millimeters.
- **rational number**: a number you can write as a fraction of two whole numbers.
- **irrational number**: a number whose decimals never end and never repeat.
- **repeating decimal**: a decimal whose digits repeat forever, like 0.333....
- **infinite**: going on forever. The whole numbers, the fractions and the decimals are all infinite.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** tenths, hundredths and thousandths as equal parts that nest by ten; counting in tenths past one; 3 ÷ 10 = 0.3; tenths as hundredths; each digit's value to three places, placeholder zeros and reading decimals aloud; decimals and fractions both ways, with 1/2, 1/4, 3/4, 1/5 and (Keeper's Desk) 1/8 and 3/8; comparing and ordering decimals of different lengths; dividing by 10 and 100; changing metric units; adding and subtracting to three places in money and measures; rounding to whole numbers, decimal places and significant figures; (Keeper's Desk) decimals that end, repeat or never repeat, and room between any two numbers.

**What it does not claim:** these are made-up weights, prices and river readings. It does not teach percentages (a sibling pack does), multiplying or dividing by decimals, or proofs that a number is irrational. A screen rod rehearses the reasoning; real rods and scales teach measuring.
