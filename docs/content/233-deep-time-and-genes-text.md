## Story scenes

### Scene 1. Stripes in the bank (Act I, chapter 3: investigate and plant)

**Short:** The storm sliced the riverbank. Pip sees stripes: brown, gray, brown. "Which stripe came first?" {guide} points down. "Start at the bottom."

**Longer:** The flood had bitten a fresh edge out of the riverbank below the garden. Pip sniffed along it, then stopped.

"Stripes," he said. "Brown, then gray, then brown again. Who painted it?"

"Nobody," said {guide}. "Each stripe is mud or sand the river left. One lot settled. Then the next lot settled on top."

Dot leaned in, holding her hat. "So the bottom stripe is the oldest?"

"If nothing has moved them, yes. The top one is the newest. That's where today's flood mud is."

Pip pawed at the thin gray stripe. It was soft and pale. "This one looks different."

"Good noticing," said {guide}. "Some pale stripes are ash from faraway volcanoes. Ash can even tell us how old a layer is. Shall we find out how?"

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** Many old layers of mud rock can't be dated directly. Scientists date a layer of volcanic ash with a natural clock inside its crystals, then use the layers above and below.

### Scene 2. Green peas in a yellow row (Act II, chapter 2: a garden across the seasons)

**Short:** Hob Pennick shells his peas. Most are yellow. About one pea in four is green. "Where did green come from? Both plants grew from yellow peas!"

**Longer:** Hob Pennick sat on an upturned bucket by the ford, shelling dried peas into a bowl. Yellow, yellow, yellow, green.

"Green," said Hob, holding one up. "From two plants that grew from yellow peas. My granny grew green peas, mind you."

"Maybe the green was hiding," said Pip.

Hob laughed. Then he stopped laughing. "Maybe it was."

{guide} picked up a yellow pea and a green one. "Each plant got two instructions for pea color, one from each parent plant. Yellow can cover up green. But green can still be passed on, quietly."

"So my plants were carrying green?" said Hob.

"Some of them. We can work out how many green peas to expect."

Hob looked at the bowl. "I'll write it in the garden book. So next time we'll know."

**Keeper's note (A true thing from history):** In the 1850s and 1860s, Gregor Mendel grew thousands of pea plants in a monastery garden in Brno. From plants grown from yellow hybrid peas, he counted 6,022 yellow peas and 2,001 green ones: about three to one.

### Scene 3. A smooth peach (Act III, chapter 2: the seed exchange)

**Short:** Nuria's letter comes with a box of peach stones. "Some of our peach seedlings grow smooth fruit," she writes. "Why?"

**Longer:** The courier set a little box on the counter. Inside lay a dozen peach stones and a letter in Nuria's neat hand.

*"Hola from Vadoalto! These stones are from our peach trees. Our peaches are fuzzy. But some seedlings grow smooth fruit, like nectarines. Mi abuela says the smooth ones are peaches that forgot their coats."*

*(Hello from Vadoalto! ... My grandmother says the smooth ones are peaches that forgot their coats.)*

The orchard keeper turned a stone over. "We have one smooth tree too. Grew from a fuzzy tree's stone."

"A long time ago," said {guide}, "one peach tree had a tiny change in one of its genes. Now the smooth version gets passed on, hidden, from tree to tree."

"So the coat isn't forgotten," said Dot. "It's just switched off."

"Let's write back," said {guide}. "With a square."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** A nectarine is a kind of peach. Scientists found that smooth skin comes mostly from one changed gene. A tree shows smooth skin only when it carries two smooth copies.

### Scene 4. Am I a dinosaur? (Act III, chapter 4: letters up and down the river)

**Short:** Léon writes from Les Saules: "My cousin says I'm a dinosaur! I'm a kingfisher. Am I closer to a crocodile or a Triceratops?"

**Longer:** Léon's letter had a damp corner, as usual.

*"Bonjour from Les Saules! My cousin says birds are dinosaurs. I have checked myself all over. No horns. No tail spikes. Is it true? And if it is, which dinosaur is my nearest cousin? I am hoping for something with a frill."*

*(Hello from Les Saules!)*

Pip read it twice. "Is he a dinosaur?"

"Birds are a living branch of the dinosaur family," said {guide}. "But that's not the interesting part. The interesting part is *which* branch."

The librarian unrolled a long paper tree on the table. Six names sat at the tips: crocodile, Stegosaurus, Triceratops, Diplodocus, T. rex, kingfisher.

"Don't look at who sits next to whom," said the librarian. "Trace each pair down to where their branches join."

Pip traced. "Oh no," he said. "Léon is not going to like this. No frill."

**Keeper's note (A true thing from history):** In 1950 the German scientist Willi Hennig set out a way to build these trees: group living things only by shared new features, not by how alike they look overall.

### Scene 5. Who ended the mammoths? (Act IV, chapter 3: the newspaper's two explanations)

**Short:** The Gazette's back page asks: who ended the mammoths? One letter says hunters. One says a changing climate. The librarian says: weigh the evidence.

**Longer:** The *Willowbank Gazette* had run out of town news for the back page, so the librarian filled it with a very old question.

*WHO ENDED THE MAMMOTHS?* Two letters sat side by side. One said: "Hunters, of course. People arrived, and the big animals vanished." The other said: "The ice age ended. Their cold grassland shrank away."

Bramble read both. "One of them must be mistaken."

"Maybe," said the librarian. "Or maybe both are part of it. And the answer might be different on different continents."

"How would you even know?" asked Pip.

"Dates," said the librarian. "When people arrived. When the climate changed. When the last bones were left. We line them up and weigh them."

Bramble crossed her arms. "Fine. But I'm keeping my opinion until I've seen the dates."

"That," said the librarian, "is the best opinion in the paper."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** Woolly mammoths lived on Wrangel Island, off northern Siberia, until about 4,000 years ago. That is long after mainland mammoths were gone.

### Scene 6. What lived in our valley? (Act V: the Willowbank newspaper path)

**Short:** Your newspaper wants a feature: what lived here long ago? The librarian lends you a box from the ford quarry. Four kinds of clue are inside.

**Longer:** The ford quarry is a made-up dig, but the librarian treats its box like treasure. Inside sit four trays.

A slice of rib bone with thin rings, like a tree stump. A tooth with tiny drilled dots down its side. A lump of fossil dropping, cut open. And a stack of pressed leaves: ferns, needles, a fan-shaped palm.

"All from one animal?" asked Dot.

"The bone, tooth and dropping are from the same kind," said the librarian. "We call it the ford duckbill. The leaves are from the same layer."

"Which tray tells the whole story?" asked Pip.

"None of them," said {guide}. "Each one answers a different question. Put them together and the story gets stronger."

"And if two trays disagree?"

The librarian smiled. "Then you've found the most interesting part."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** Real scientists study fossil teeth, bones, droppings and plants in labs, using chemistry and microscopes. Digging is only the start.

## Teach cards

Each worked example reveals one step per tap.

**T1. Layers stack up** (A true thing about science; Sprout). Mud and sand settle in layers. A new layer settles on top. If nothing moves them, the bottom layer is the oldest.
Worked example: (1) The river leaves brown mud. (2) Then gray ash falls. (3) Then more brown mud. (4) The bottom brown is oldest.

**T2. A town under ash** (A true thing from history; Sprout and Seedling). Long ago, a mountain called Vesuvius blew out ash. The ash fell on a Roman town called Pompeii and covered it.
Worked example: (1) A busy town. (2) The mountain erupts. (3) Ash and little stones fall. (4) The town is buried.

**T3. Buried and kept** (Seedling). The ash covered Pompeii fast. It kept streets, walls and pots in their places. Much later, diggers found them.
Worked example: (1) Ash covers a bakery. (2) Hundreds of years pass. (3) Diggers clear the ash. (4) The oven is still there.

**T4. Two kinds of hip** (Sapling). Dinosaurs had two main hip shapes. Scientists call them "lizard-hipped" and "bird-hipped." Surprise: birds come from the lizard-hipped side.
Worked example: (1) T. rex: lizard-hipped. (2) Triceratops: bird-hipped. (3) Birds: on T. rex's side. (4) The name misleads.

**T5. Trace to the join** (Sapling). In a branching tree, find how close two kinds are. Trace both down to where their branches join. A closer join means closer cousins.
Worked example: (1) Find kingfisher and T. rex. (2) Trace down. (3) They join early. (4) Close cousins.

**T6. Turn it; nothing changes** (Sapling). You can spin a branch around a join. The tips move on the page. Who joins whom stays the same.
Worked example: (1) Diplodocus sits left. (2) Spin its join. (3) Now it sits right. (4) Same joins, same cousins.

**T7. Joins, not tips, are ancestors** (Sapling). Every tip is a living thing or a fossil kind. The ancestors sit at the joins. No tip grew from another tip.
Worked example: (1) T. rex and kingfisher are tips. (2) Their join is an older animal. (3) Both came from it.

**T8. New shared features mark a branch** (Sapling). A feature that first appeared at a join marks that branch. A feature everyone in the tree has can't split anyone.
Worked example: (1) All six lay eggs. (2) That splits no one. (3) Only T. rex and kingfisher have a wishbone. (4) It marks their branch.

**T9. The halving clock** (Sapling). Some atoms slowly change into other atoms. One half-life is how long it takes half of them to change. Then half the rest change.
Worked example: (1) Start with 64. (2) One half-life: 32 left. (3) Two half-lives: 16 left. (4) Never quite zero.

**T10. Read the share, not a stopwatch** (Keeper's Desk). Labs compare a sample's carbon-14 with the amount living things have. That shows the share left, and the share shows how many halvings happened.
Worked example: (1) A quarter of the carbon-14 is left. (2) That is two halvings. (3) 2 × 5,730 = 11,460 years.

**T11. Pick the clock that fits** (Keeper's Desk). Carbon-14 works on things once alive, up to about 50,000 years. Uranium-lead compares uranium left with lead made in rock crystals, over millions and billions of years.
Worked example: (1) A burnt log in a cave: carbon-14. (2) A dinosaur bone: too old. (3) Date the ash around it instead.

**T12. Ash above, ash below** (Keeper's Desk). We usually date the volcanic ash, not the fossil. A fossil between two dated ash layers is between their ages.
Worked example: (1) Ash below: 76 million years. (2) Ash above: 75 million. (3) The fossil: between them.

**T13. Each clue answers one question** (Keeper's Desk). Growth rings tell age and growth. Tooth chemistry tells movement and seasons. Droppings tell food. Fossil plants tell the place.
Worked example: (1) Rings: at least 9 years. (2) Tooth: moved with the seasons. (3) Dropping: wood. (4) Plants: warm and wet.

**T14. Agreeing clues, stronger claim** (Keeper's Desk). When separate clues point the same way, be surer. When two clues clash, look closer. A clash is a clue too.
Worked example: (1) Teeth say plant-eater. (2) One dropping holds shell. (3) Maybe a seasonal snack. (4) Check more droppings.

**T15. The DNA ladder** (Keeper's Desk). DNA is a twisted ladder. Each rung is a pair of bases. A always pairs with T. C always pairs with G.
Worked example: (1) One side reads A T T. (2) Partners: T A A. (3) Each half can rebuild the other.

**T16. Gene to protein** (Keeper's Desk). A gene is a stretch of DNA. Its message is copied into mRNA. The cell reads it and joins amino acids, already there, into a protein.
Worked example: (1) DNA: ATG GAA. (2) mRNA: AUG GAA. (3) Read in threes. (4) Join two amino acids.

**T17. A change in the letters** (Keeper's Desk). A mutation is a random change in DNA letters. Sunlight's UV, some chemicals and copying slips cause them. Most change nothing that matters.
Worked example: (1) GAA becomes GAG. (2) Both mean the same amino acid. (3) The protein is the same.

**T18. Helpful depends on where** (Keeper's Desk). Mutations happen whatever a living thing needs. Where it lives decides if a change helps. Changes in the cells that make young can be passed on.
Worked example: (1) A dark moth appears by chance. (2) On sooty trees, it hides. (3) On pale trees, it shows.

**T19. Alleles and the square** (Keeper's Desk). A gene can come in versions called alleles. A plant gets one from each parent plant. A dominant allele shows even when only one copy is there.
Worked example: (1) Y is yellow. (2) y is green. (3) Yy looks yellow. (4) Only yy looks green.

**T20. A chance, not a promise** (Keeper's Desk). A square gives chances over many young. One in four is not a promise for any four. Each seed is a fresh chance.
Worked example: (1) Yy and Yy. (2) One square in four is yy. (3) Four peas might give none green.

**T21. A race of rates** (Keeper's Desk). Living things adapt across generations, not on demand. If their world changes faster than they can adapt, they fall behind. Slow change gives time.
Worked example: (1) Change: 3 steps a decade. (2) Adapting: 2. (3) Gap grows 1 a decade. (4) Behind by decade 4.

**T22. Weigh more than one cause** (Keeper's Desk). Big extinctions often have more than one cause. Scientists line up dates for climate, people and the last fossils, then weigh them.
Worked example: (1) People arrive. (2) Climate shifts. (3) Last bones. (4) Which came when, and where?

**T23. Can we, and should we?** (Keeper's Desk). Two questions, kept apart. Can we bring back a lost kind? Should we? Science answers the first. People argue the second, with reasons.
Worked example: (1) Can: ancient DNA is broken. (2) Should: reasons for. (3) Reasons against. (4) Both sides heard.

## Activity (d): The Case Board (Keeper's Desk)

Opening: Four trays, four questions. What can each clue tell you?
Trays: **Bone rings** · **Tooth chemistry** · **Fossil dropping** · **Fossil plants**.
Each tray shows a "Can tell" line and a "Can't tell" line:
- Bone rings. Can tell: about how old, and how fast it grew. Can't tell: what it ate.
- Tooth chemistry. Can tell: what water and rock it lived near, season by season. Can't tell: how old it was.
- Fossil dropping. Can tell: some of what it ate. Can't tell: exactly which animal left it, without other clues.
- Fossil plants. Can tell: what the place was like. Can't tell: what this animal ate.
Guided first tray (2 steps): (1) Count the rib rings: 9. (2) Some early rings can be lost. So: at least about 9 years.
The ford duckbill readouts (A made-up model):
- Rib rings: 9 rings, wide then narrow. It grew fast, then slowed.
- Tooth chemistry: the tooth's signal swings three times, like three summers and winters. Its water signal fits a warm, rainy place. It matches hill rock, then coast rock.
- Droppings: mostly chewed conifer wood. Two of six hold bits of shell.
- Plants: ferns, conifers and palms. Warm and wet.
**Claim cards:** Lived on a warm, wet plain · Moved between hills and coast · At least about 9 years old · Ate rotting wood · Was dark green.
Confidence line for each claim: *Lines that agree: {n}.* (0 says: No clue tells us this yet.)
Clash card: Teeth and jaws say plant-eater. Two droppings hold shell. A mistake, or a clue?
Clash reply: A clue. Maybe a seasonal snack. More droppings would tell.
Model line (A made-up model): The ford duckbill is made up. Its clues copy the kinds real scientists use.

Real cards (A true thing about science, Keeper's Desk):
- **A mammoth's travels.** Scientists sampled a mammoth tusk, layer by layer. Its chemistry showed the mammoth roamed huge distances across Alaska in its life of about 28 years.
- **T. rex rings.** Growth marks in T. rex bones show its growth sped up, then slowed. Studies of more bones keep changing the guess for when it was fully grown.
- **Wood and shells.** Fossil droppings from Utah, about 75 million years old, hold chewed rotting wood and shell bits. Duck-billed dinosaurs probably left them.

## Activity (e): The Pea Bench (Keeper's Desk)

**DNA ladder.** Opening: Build the partner side of the ladder.
Tiles: **A** · **T** · **C** · **G**.
Guided first rung (1 step shown): A pairs with T. Place T.
Mismatch reply: A fits only with T. C fits only with G.
Zoom strip: pea plant · leaf cell · nucleus · chromosome · DNA · gene.

**Gene to protein.** Opening: Copy the gene. Then read it in threes.
Model gene (coding side): ATG GAA AAA TGG TAA.
mRNA copy line: In mRNA, U stands where DNA has T.
Readout: AUG GAA AAA UGG UAA → start (Met) · Glu · Lys · Trp · stop.
Amino acid line: The cell already has amino acids. The mRNA says what order to join them in.
Model line (A made-up model): A real gene is hundreds or thousands of letters long. This one is fifteen.

**Change a letter.** Opening: Pick a change. What happens to the protein?
Changes (shown in the mRNA copy): GAA→GAG · AAA→AAG · GAA→GAU · GAA→GCA · AAA→UAA · UGG→UGA.
Results:
- Same: Same amino acid. The protein doesn't change.
- Changed: One amino acid swaps. The protein might work the same, or not.
- Stop: The reading stops early. The protein comes out short.
**Twenty changes** (A made-up model): 14 change nothing that matters · 5 cause trouble · 1 helps here. *A made-up tally. Real shares vary from gene to gene and place to place.*

**Pea square.** Opening: Pick each parent's two alleles. Then fill the square.
Parent buttons: **YY** · **Yy** · **yy**.
Guided first square (4 steps): (1) Parent 1 is Yy: put Y and y across the top. (2) Parent 2 is Yy: put Y and y down the side. (3) Fill each box with one from each. (4) Count: three yellow, one green.
Header reply (if two letters go on one header): Each header holds one allele. A pollen grain or egg cell carries just one.
Box readout: {genotype} looks {yellow|green}.
**Grow 4** (a made-up batch): yellow, green, yellow, green. *Two green, not one. Four is too few to show the pattern.*
**Grow 400** (a made-up batch): 296 yellow, 104 green. *About three to one.*
Mendel card: Mendel's real count: 6,022 yellow and 2,001 green.

**Snip a gene** (CRISPR card, A true thing about science): Scientists can now cut DNA at a chosen spot. In 2016, a US farm agency said a mushroom edited to brown more slowly fell outside its rules for gene-changed crops.
Two-sides board: Reasons for · Reasons against. Chips (child places any): less food thrown away · we don't know every effect yet · faster than breeding · who decides? · could help crops in drought · edits might spread to wild plants. *No chip is the right answer. Place two on each side.*

## Activity (f): The Weighing Table (Keeper's Desk)

Calm line shown on arrival.

**Rate race.** Opening: Set how fast the world changes. Set how fast they adapt.
Buttons: change **Slow (1)** / **Medium (2)** / **Fast (3)** steps a decade; adapting **Slow breeders (1)** / **Fast breeders (2)** steps a decade.
Readout: Decade {d} · Gap {g} · Can still keep up / Fallen behind.
Guided first run (3 steps): (1) Change: Slow. (2) Adapting: Slow breeders. (3) Run. Does the gap grow?
Model rule line: Each decade the gap grows by change minus adapting, never below zero. At a gap of 4 they fall behind.
Model line (A made-up model): Real living things can also move, or survive in odd corners. This race shows only the rates.

**Two kinds of extinction.** Background card: Kinds of living things have always died out, a few at a time. Mass card: Five times in Earth's history, very many kinds died out quickly. Recovery took millions of years.

**The Bramble Cay melomys** (A true thing about science; Keeper's Desk). This small rodent lived on one tiny, low island near Australia. Storm waves washed over it again and again, and most of its food plants were lost. In 2019 Australia listed it as extinct.

**Today** (A true thing about science; Keeper's Desk). Many scientists say kinds are dying out faster now than the slow, usual pace. The main reason is people changing land and climate. Many people work to protect living kinds.

**Which kinds keep up?** Cards: a mouse that breeds every year and eats many things · a big beast that breeds every ten years and eats one plant · a moth with many color versions. Prompt: Which might keep up with fast change?

**The giants' timeline.** Three strips: Australia · North America · Wrangel Island. Each strip shows: people arrive · climate shifts · last big bones.
Strip labels (A true thing about science):
- Australia: people arrived, and later most giant animals were gone. Scientists still argue about how long they lived side by side, and about the climate's part. (No dates on this strip.)
- North America: people spread, the climate swung cold, and mammoths, camels and giant sloths disappeared about 13,000 to 11,000 years ago.
- Wrangel Island: mammoths lasted on this island until about 4,000 years ago.
Balance card: Hunters · Climate · Both. Place each strip's weight. *Scientists still argue. Many think both mattered, in different mixes in different places.*

**Ancient DNA.** Card: DNA breaks into short pieces over time. The oldest found is about 2 million years old. The big dinosaurs died out 66 million years ago, so no DNA from them is left. (Birds, the living dinosaurs, have their own.)

**The bucardo** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk). The bucardo was a wild goat of the Pyrenees. The last one died in 2000. In 2003 scientists made a copy from her saved cells. It lived only a few minutes.

**Can we, and should we?** Board: Can we? · Should we? Chips: ancient DNA is broken into pieces · a mammoth-like elephant is not a mammoth · its old home has changed · it could help lost habitats · money could protect living animals instead · we may owe it to kinds people lost. *Put each chip under a question. Then pick a side and say why to a grown-up.*

## History cards

**Pliny's letters (A true thing from history).** A young Roman named Pliny saw Vesuvius erupt. He was across the bay. Years later he wrote two letters about it. He said the ash cloud rose like a tall pine tree.

**Digging Pompeii (A true thing from history).** Digging at Pompeii began in 1748. Diggers have been uncovering it ever since. Some of the town is still under ash.

**The rock clock checks itself (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk).** In 1997, scientists dated pumice from Pompeii's eruption with an argon clock. They got about 1,925 years, give or take 94. History said 1,918.

**Mendel's peas (A true thing from history).** Gregor Mendel was a friar in Brno, in today's Czech Republic. He grew peas for years and published his results in 1866. Few noticed until 1900.

**Libby's carbon clock (A true thing from history).** In the late 1940s Willard Libby, an American chemist, worked out how to date old wood and bone with carbon-14. He won a Nobel Prize in 1960.

**How old is Earth? (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk).** In 1956 Clair Patterson measured lead in a meteorite. He worked out that Earth is about 4.55 billion years old. Today's best figure, about 4.54 billion, is very close.

**Hennig's trees (A true thing from history).** Willi Hennig, a German scientist who studied flies, published his way to build family trees of living things in 1950.

**Kimura's quiet changes (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk).** In 1968, Motoo Kimura, a scientist in Japan, argued that many changes in DNA make little difference. They drift along by chance.

**From salt ponds to gene snipping (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk).** In 1993, Francisco Mojica in Spain noticed odd repeats in the DNA of salt-pond microbes. Later he worked out they were part of a defense system. In 2012, Emmanuelle Charpentier and Jennifer Doudna turned it into a tool. They won a Nobel Prize in 2020.

## "I'm stuck" worked examples (step 2 of the ladder)

- **Layers:** Three books dropped on a table, one at a time. Which landed first? Start: the one at the bottom.
- **Pompeii:** A sandcastle buried by a wave of sand, then dug out. What's left? Start: the shape is still inside.
- **Tracing:** Three tips: cat, lion, dog. Cat and lion join first. Start: trace cat down to its first join.
- **Spinning:** A mobile spins. Do the strings change? Start: watch where each string ties on.
- **Halving:** 16 beads. Halve twice. Start: 16 → 8 → 4.
- **Carbon clock:** Half the carbon-14 is left. Start: one half-life, so about 5,730 years.
- **Clues:** A tree stump with 20 rings. What does it tell? Start: how old the tree was.
- **Partner bases:** G C A. Start: G pairs with C.
- **Squares:** Yy and yy. Start: put Y, y across the top and y, y down the side.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Layer:** a flat sheet of mud, sand, ash or rock lying on others.
- **Ash:** tiny bits of rock and glass a volcano throws out.
- **Pumice:** a light rock full of holes, thrown out by a volcano.
- **Erupt:** when a volcano throws out ash, rock or lava.
- **Friar:** a member of a religious group who lives by its rules; a monastery is a home for such a group.
- **Archaeologist:** a scientist who digs up and studies things people left behind.
- **Branching tree (cladogram):** a drawing that shows which living things share ancestors.
- **Tip:** the end of a branch, where one kind sits.
- **Join (node):** where two branches meet; it stands for a shared ancestor.
- **Ancestor:** a living thing that later ones came from.
- **Shared new feature:** a feature that first appeared in an ancestor and was passed on to its branch.
- **Beak bone:** an extra bone at the front of the lower jaw in "bird-hipped" dinosaurs.
- **Wishbone:** two collarbones joined into a V. T. rex and birds have one.
- **Atom:** a tiny building block of everything.
- **Argon clock:** a rock clock that reads the argon made as potassium atoms in volcanic crystals slowly change.
- **Isotope:** one version of an element's atoms. Carbon-14 is one version of carbon.
- **Half-life:** how long it takes half of a batch of changing atoms to change.
- **Carbon-14:** a kind of carbon that slowly changes. Used to date things once alive.
- **Growth ring:** a line in bone or wood laid down as it grows, often one a year.
- **Fossil dropping (coprolite):** animal dung that turned to stone.
- **Conifer:** a tree with needles or scales and cones, like a pine.
- **Evidence:** the clues that support a claim.
- **DNA:** the long, twisted molecule in a cell that holds its instructions.
- **Base:** one rung-half of the DNA ladder: A, T, C or G.
- **Gene:** a section of DNA with instructions, often for a protein.
- **Protein:** a working molecule made of amino acids joined in order.
- **mRNA:** a short copy of a gene's message that the cell reads.
- **Amino acid:** a small building block that proteins are made from.
- **Mutation:** a random change in DNA letters.
- **UV:** an invisible part of sunlight that can change DNA letters.
- **Allele:** one version of a gene.
- **Dominant:** an allele that shows even when only one copy is there.
- **Recessive:** an allele that shows only when both copies are that version.
- **Genotype:** the alleles a living thing carries.
- **Phenotype:** the feature you can see.
- **Punnett square:** a grid that shows the chances of each allele pair.
- **Natural selection:** living things with helpful features leave more young, so those features spread over generations.
- **Adapt:** to change over many generations to fit a place better.
- **Extinct:** when no living members of a kind are left.
- **Mass extinction:** a time when very many kinds died out quickly.
- **Megafauna:** very big animals, like mammoths.
- **Ancient DNA:** DNA recovered from old bones, hair or soil.
- **CRISPR:** a tool scientists use to cut DNA at a chosen spot.
- **Ethics:** thinking about what is right to do.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** layers and their order; Pompeii as a Roman town buried and kept by ash in 79 CE and dug out from 1748; reading and building branching trees by shared new features; half-lives and matching a dating clock to an age; reconstructing a lost world from several kinds of evidence; DNA, genes, mutation and Punnett squares with peas and peaches; extinction as change outrunning adaptation; the debate over the end of the ice-age giants; and the science and ethics of de-extinction and gene editing.
