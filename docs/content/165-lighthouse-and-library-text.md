## Story scenes (A story from Willowbank)

### Act III. Two words for one tower

**Short:**
> Léon writes that his coast has *un phare*. Nuria writes back: *un faro*. "Both words come from one island in Egypt," says the librarian.

**Longer:**
> Two letters arrive on the same day. Léon's has a drawing of a tall white tower on a rocky point. "*Le phare* (the lighthouse) blinks all night," he writes. "Ships use it to find the way in."
>
> Nuria's letter says, "*¡Qué bonito!* (How lovely!) We call it *un faro*."
>
> Pip reads both twice. "Phare. Faro. They're cousins."
>
> "They are," says the librarian, pulling down a picture of a harbor. "Long ago, on an island called Pharos, near a city called Alexandria, stood the most famous lighthouse in the world. Its name traveled into French, Spanish and Italian."
>
> "So a word can keep a lighthouse alive," says {guide}, "even after the tower is gone."

**Keeper's note:**
> The Greek word *pharos* came from the island's name. Where the island's own name came from, nobody knows. French has *phare*; Spanish and Italian have *faro*.

### Act IV. Bramble's one great fire

**Short:**
> "The great library burned down in one night," says Bramble. The librarian lays out three cards. "There was a fire," they say. "But that's not the whole story."

**Longer:**
> At the meeting about the low fields, Bramble waves a book. "Old records don't last. The great library of Alexandria burned down in one night. Everything, gone."
>
> The librarian sets three dated cards on the table. "You're right that there was a fire," they say. "In 48 BCE, during a war, fire spread from ships in the harbor and burned some books. But scholars kept working there afterward."
>
> They tap the other cards. "Scholars were sent away a hundred years before that. Later rulers stopped paying. A temple holding more books was destroyed centuries later. The library faded slowly."
>
> Bramble frowns. "So my story was too tidy."
>
> "Tidy stories are easy to remember," says the librarian. "That's why we check them."

### Act V. The short shadow

**Short:**
> Pip sees the flood post's noon shadow is short in June. "Shadows can measure the whole world," says Wren Halloway. "Someone did it once, in Egypt."

**Longer:**
> On a bright June day, Pip lies flat beside the flood post. "Its shadow is so short. Is the post shrinking?"
>
> "The Sun is high," says Wren Halloway. "At noon in summer, shadows are short." She sets a wooden board on the bench, with a circle, two little sticks and a row of golden lines for sunlight.
>
> "Long ago, a librarian in Alexandria compared two shadows in two towns on one day. From that, he worked out how big the whole Earth is."
>
> Pip sits up. "With a stick?"
>
> "With a stick, a distance and a good idea," says Wren.

### Act VI. Closing card

> A lighthouse keeps a way open, so ships come home. A library keeps a record, so people remember. In Willowbank, you have done both.

## Reading 1. The Crossroads City (A true thing from history)

**Short:**
> Alexander, a king from Macedonia, took Egypt in 332 BCE. He founded Alexandria by the sea. Greek-speaking rulers called the Ptolemies ruled Egypt from there.

**Longer:**
> Alexander was a young king from Macedonia, north of Greece. His army beat the Persian Empire, which had ruled Egypt. In 332 BCE he came to Egypt, and the Persian governor handed it over without a fight.
>
> On the coast, by an Egyptian village, Alexander chose a spot for a new city: Alexandria. Most books date it to 331 BCE. Then he marched east, as far as the Indus River, and died in Babylon in 323 BCE. His generals split his lands.
>
> One general, Ptolemy, took Egypt. His family ruled for about 300 years. They spoke Greek at court. In temples, they were shown as pharaohs, with Egyptian crowns. Greeks, Egyptians, Jews and many others lived in the city. Greek and Egyptian ways mixed. This time is called the Hellenistic Age.

**Keeper's note:**
> Greeks and Persians had fought for generations before Alexander. Persia had ruled Egypt on and off since 525 BCE. Alexander's army beat King Porus by the Hydaspes River in 326 BCE, then refused to go farther. The Rosetta Stone (196 BCE, in the Nile Year pack) comes from the Ptolemies' time: one decree in Greek and Egyptian scripts.

**Teach card:** Alexander's general Ptolemy ruled Egypt. His family were Greek. They spoke Greek, but dressed as pharaohs in temples.

**Worked example (revealed step by step):** Was a Ptolemy Greek or Egyptian? → His family came from Macedonia. → He spoke Greek at court. → In temples he was shown as a pharaoh. → So: a Greek ruler of Egypt, using Egypt's old ways.

**Map card (A simplified picture, not to scale):** Macedonia · Athens · Alexandria · Memphis · Syene · Babylon · the Indus · Rome. One arrow shows Alexander's route.

## Reading 2. The Lighthouse (A true thing from history)

**Short:**
> On Pharos island stood a giant lighthouse. A fire at the top guided ships. Earthquakes knocked it down. Divers have found its stones.

**Longer:**
> At the harbor entrance, on a little island called Pharos, the Ptolemies built a tall stone tower. Old writers link it to a man named Sostratus. It was finished about 280 BCE. A fire burned at the top at night so ships could find the harbor. Later writers named it one of the Seven Wonders of the World.
>
> How tall was it? Nobody knows exactly. Experts think it was over 100 meters, perhaps as much as 140.
>
> It stood for more than 1,500 years. Earthquakes cracked it, and in the 1300s it fell. Later, a sultan named Qaitbay built a fort on its base, using some of its stones.
>
> In 1994, divers mapped hundreds of huge blocks on the harbor floor, and a giant statue of a king.

**Teach card:** Sort what you read. *We know* has evidence. *Experts think* is a careful estimate. *Nobody knows* is still open.

**Worked example:** "It was over 100 meters tall." → Is there a measurement? No. → Did experts estimate it? Yes. → So it goes in *Experts think*.

## Reading 3. The Library (A true thing from history)

**Short:**
> The Library collected books from everywhere, by copying them. It did not burn in one night. It faded slowly over hundreds of years.

**Longer:**
> The Ptolemies wanted every book worth reading. Near the palace they built a Museum, a home for scholars, and a great Library.
>
> They gathered books on purpose. When a ship came into the harbor, books on board were taken and copied. Some were labeled "from the ships." An old writer, Galen, says a king borrowed Athens's best copies of famous plays, left a huge pledge of silver, and kept the originals.
>
> A scholar named Callimachus made long lists of writers and their works, like an early catalog.
>
> How many scrolls? Experts guess anywhere from 40,000 to 400,000.
>
> The Library did not end in one fire. Scholars were sent away. A fire in a war burned some books. Rulers stopped paying. A temple with more books was destroyed much later. It faded slowly.

**Keeper's note (Hypatia):**
> Hundreds of years later, Hypatia taught mathematics, astronomy and philosophy in Alexandria. Old records say she wrote explanations of hard math books by older scholars. Most of her writing is lost. People still remember her as one of the city's great teachers.

**Teach card:** The Library grew by copying. Copies, kept in many places, are how books survive.

**Worked example:** Follow one ship's scroll. → The ship docks. → The scroll goes to the Library. → A scribe copies it. → The copy is listed and shelved.

## Reading 4. The Last Pharaoh (A true thing from history)

**Short:**
> Cleopatra VII was the last Ptolemy to hold real power in Egypt. In 30 BCE Rome took over. Egyptian language and ideas went on.

**Longer:**
> Cleopatra VII began to rule in 51 BCE, at first with her younger brother. Like all her family, she grew up speaking Greek. An old writer, Plutarch, says she spoke many languages and was the first in her family to learn Egyptian.
>
> Rome was growing strong. Cleopatra made alliances with Roman leaders, trying to keep Egypt free. In 31 BCE, the Roman leader Octavian beat the ships of Cleopatra and her Roman ally, Mark Antony, at Actium. In 30 BCE he took Alexandria. Cleopatra died that year, and Egypt became part of the Roman world. Its grain helped feed the city of Rome.
>
> The Ptolemies' rule was over. Egypt carried on. People kept speaking Egyptian. Its last form, Coptic, is still used in church services today.

**Keeper's note:**
> Egypt had been conquered before: by Assyria in 671 BCE, by Persia in 525 BCE, and by Alexander in 332 BCE. Persian, Greek and Roman rulers borrowed Egyptian ways. Since 1836, an obelisk from Luxor Temple has stood in Paris; its twin is still in Egypt.

**Teach card:** A kingdom can end while its people, language and ideas carry on.

## The Shadow Bench (A simplified picture)

**Model line (always shown):** *A simplified picture: a perfectly round Earth, a Sun so far away its rays arrive parallel, and two towns on one north-south line. Not to scale.*

**Step 1. Sweep a ray.**
- **Teach card:** Cut a full turn into 360 equal tiny turns. Each one is a degree.
- Prompt: Drag the ray. Watch the degrees add up.
- At 90: *A quarter turn. That's 90 degrees.* · At 180: *A half turn. 180 degrees.* · At 360: *A full turn. 360 degrees.*
- **Longer arm** button: *Longer arm, same turn. The degrees stay the same.*

**Step 2. The two sticks.**
- **Teach card:** At Syene, at noon on the longest day, a stick had no shadow. At Alexandria, a stick did.
- Prompt: Turn the arc to measure Alexandria's shadow angle.
- On 7.2: *7.2 degrees. Greeks then said "one fiftieth of a circle."*
- Ray label: *A picture of the light's path.*

**Step 3. Stack the wedges.**
- **Teach card:** The angle at the sticks matches the angle at Earth's center. Copy that wedge all the way round.
- Worked example (step by step): One wedge is 7.2°. → Ten wedges make 72°. → Fifty wedges make 360°. → So the gap between the towns is 1/50 of the way round.

**Step 4. Times the distance.**
- **Teach card:** If the gap is 1/50 of the way round, the whole way is 50 gaps.
- Worked example: The towns are 5,000 stadia apart. → 50 × 5,000 = 250,000 stadia. → That's the whole way round.
- **Stadion toggle:** *Nobody knows exactly how long his stadion was.* · *If 157.5 m: about 39,000 km.* · *If 185 m: about 46,000 km.* · *Today's measurement: about 40,000 km.*

**Step 5. Flat contrast.**
- **Flat ground** switch: *On flat ground, parallel sunlight gives both sticks the same shadow.* · Back to round: *Different shadows mean the ground curves.*

**Step 6. Made-up towns (A made-up model).**
- Prompt: Pick an angle and a distance. See how big the world is.
- Result line: *A made-up model. Real measurements have errors.*

**Off-screen card (a grown-up leads):** On a sunny day, stand a stick upright in open ground. Mark the tip of its shadow every half hour. When is it shortest? **Never look at the Sun.**

### Set B. Books from the Ships

**C9 · Keeper's Desk · 3-choice (A made-up model)**
- **Prompt:** Two planets, towns 800 km apart. Planet B's angle is bigger. Planet B is...
- **Choices:** (a) Bigger · (b) The same size · (c) Smaller ✔
- **Misconceptions:** (a) `bigger-angle-bigger-earth`; (b) `angle-irrelevant`.
- **Feedback:** (a) A bigger angle means fewer wedges round. Smaller planet. · (b) The angle changes the answer. Try it on the bench. · (c) Yes. Fewer wedges fit, so less way round.
- **Hint:** Bigger wedges: do more or fewer fit in 360°?
- **Easier one first:** 7.2° gives 50 wedges. 14.4° gives 25. Fewer wedges, same distance each.
- **Explanation:** Same distance, bigger angle: the ground curves faster, so the planet is smaller.

**C10 · Keeper's Desk · say why (tell a grown-up)**
- **Prompt:** Name two things Eratosthenes assumed. Which matters most if wrong?
- **A good answer might say:** the Sun is so far its rays are parallel; Syene is due south of Alexandria; the distance is 5,000 stadia; Syene's Sun was exactly overhead. Any answer with a reason is a good one.
- **Hint:** Look at the bench's model line.
- **Explanation:** Every measurement rests on assumptions. Naming them shows where errors can hide.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** how Alexander's conquests put Greek-speaking rulers, the Ptolemies, over Egypt; Alexandria as a city where many peoples lived; the Pharos lighthouse, sorted into what we know, what experts estimate and what nobody knows; how the Library gathered books by copying and why the "one great fire" is a myth of slow decline; what a degree is; how Eratosthenes estimated the Earth's size from two shadows and a distance, with honest uncertainty about his unit; Cleopatra VII as a ruler, and Rome's takeover in 30 BCE; and that Egyptian language and ideas carried on.

**What it does not claim:** no single height for the lighthouse or count for the Library; no exact modern value for Eratosthenes' result (it depends on a unit nobody can pin down); no romance framing for Cleopatra and no description of her death. Hypatia appears only as a teacher and writer. Her life ended violently in 415 CE during conflict in the city; the pack leaves that out of children's text. Willowbank and its letters are fiction.

**Sources in brief:** Encyclopaedia Britannica (Library of Alexandria, Pharos survey, Hypatia, Battle of the Hydaspes); MacTutor (University of St Andrews) and the Mathematical Association of America on Eratosthenes; ASOR on Cleopatra's languages; World History Encyclopedia on Alexandria and Cleopatra; the French Ministry of Culture on the underwater survey. Full list in the source audit.
