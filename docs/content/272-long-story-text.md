# The Long Story: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 272](../specs/272-long-story.md). Use as written, after review. `{guide}` is never gendered. Pronouns: the librarian they/them; Pip he/him; Dot and Bramble she/her; Bramble's magpie "it" (as in packs 265 and 273); Hollis they/them, named only in scene 3 (Act V). The explorer is "you". Sources: [audit](../source-audits/272-long-story.md). Nothing is scored, timed or locked.

**Labels used throughout:**
- **A story from Willowbank** marks the town and its people (fiction).
- **A true thing about language** marks how stories and books work.
- **A true thing from history** marks real authors, dates and facts about the two books.
- **Book cards** are real public-domain text. Each shows its source line: book, author, year, chapter. Quoted words are exact; `[...]` marks a cut. Chapter cards marked **Retold for this pack** are our own plain words about what happens in a chapter.

**Reading lengths.** Scenes have Short, Longer and a Keeper's note. Every bench task and every problem-set item shows its own book card, so it can be answered at any reading length. Read-aloud is offered on every card.

## Arriving

**Place title:** The Long Story

**First visit** (A story from Willowbank):
> At the back of the library there is a deep window seat and a shelf of fat old books. The librarian keeps a lamp there for long stories.
>
> "Short stories are a walk," they say. "A long story is a journey. You meet people early and find out who they become."

**Every bench screen (always shown):** You're reading short pieces of long books. Each card says where in the book it comes from.

**Grown-up panel (always available):** This page reads excerpts from two public-domain novels: Kenneth Grahame's *The Wind in the Willows* (1908) and Frances Hodgson Burnett's *The Secret Garden* (1911). Every quotation is exact and short. *The Secret Garden* carries colonial and racist attitudes of its time, especially about India, from its first chapters to its last. We quote only passages free of them, and the chapter cards leave them out. If your child reads the whole book, you may want to talk about this. The book also presents Colin's illness as cured mainly by happy thoughts and fresh air. That is the novel's idea, from 1911, and the pack never teaches it as medical fact.

## The two books

**Card: The Wind in the Willows (A true thing from history):** Kenneth Grahame wrote *The Wind in the Willows*. It came out in 1908. It has twelve chapters. Mole, Rat and Toad live by a river. Badger lives in the Wild Wood nearby.

**Card: The Secret Garden (A true thing from history):** Frances Hodgson Burnett wrote *The Secret Garden*. It came out as a book in 1911. It has twenty-seven chapters. Mary Lennox, a girl who is hard to please, comes to live at her uncle's big house on the Yorkshire moor.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. The Mole goes home (Winter Lantern Night)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** Lanterns hang along the footbridge. The librarian reads a chapter aloud. Pip promises to stay awake this time.

**Short:**
> On Lantern Night the librarian reads aloud. The Mole smells his old home, but his friend the Rat hurries on. Mole won't leave his friend behind.

**Longer:**
> Lanterns hang along the footbridge. The librarian opens a fat green book to chapter 5 and begins to read.
>
> Pip is asleep before the second page.
>
> In the story it is a snowy December night. The Mole catches a smell in the dark. "Home!" reads the librarian. His old house is close by.
>
> But the Rat is far ahead, worried about snow, and doesn't hear him call.
>
> "Why doesn't Mole just go?" says Dot. "It's his house."
>
> "Listen," says {guide}.
>
> The Mole follows his friend. Later he sits on a tree stump and sobs. The Rat asks what is wrong, and then they go back and find Mole's house together.
>
> Pip wakes up. "Was there supper?"
>
> "Sardines," says the librarian. "And field mice came singing carols."
>
> "Best chapter," says Pip, who missed it.

**Keeper's note (A true thing from history):**
> Chapter 5 of *The Wind in the Willows* is called "Dulce Domum." That is Latin for "sweet home." It is the chapter where the Mole finds his way back to his own small house.

### Scene 2. Has Toad changed? (Winter Lantern Night, a later week)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** The librarian reads the last chapter. Toad has his house back. Bramble has an opinion about Toad. So does everyone.

**Short:**
> Toad promises to be a different Toad. Dot thinks he has changed. Bramble thinks he is only acting. The librarian asks for lines from the book.

**Longer:**
> It is the last chapter. Toad and his friends have won back Toad Hall.
>
> Toad promises to be "a very different Toad." Then he locks his bedroom door, lines up the chairs, and sings a song all about himself. Twice.
>
> At the dinner, he answers compliments with "Not at all!"
>
> "He's changed," says Dot. "Look how modest he is."
>
> "He's acting modest," says Bramble, "so everyone will stare at him. And he likes it when they stare."
>
> The magpie bobs its head, which may mean yes.
>
> "So who's right?" says Pip.
>
> The librarian closes the book on one finger, to keep the place. "Find me the line that shows it. Then we'll see."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> A question like "Has Toad changed?" can have more than one good answer. It has no answer key. What matters is the evidence: which lines you choose, and how closely each one fits your point.

### Scene 3. A guide for the next Keeper (Act V, after page 5)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** The fifth page comes free on the baker's windowsill. It is blank, except for its heading: *For the next Keeper*.

**Short:**
> Hollis left blank pages for the next Keeper. The librarian has an idea: a reader's guide to one long book, with lines from the book as proof.

**Longer:**
> The fifth page lifts off the windowsill without tearing. There is nothing on it but a heading: *For the next Keeper*.
>
> "Hollis kept the flood post," says the librarian. "But a Keeper needs other things. Something to read on long winter nights."
>
> "A whole book?" says Pip. "On one page?"
>
> "A guide," says the librarian. "Pick one book. Pick one thread: a character who changes, or an idea that keeps coming back. Find three moments, early, middle and late. Then a line from the book for each."
>
> "And say what your guide can't tell them," says Dot. "That's the honest bit."
>
> Bramble reads over your shoulder. "Pick Toad," she says. "Then argue with me."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> A reader's guide follows one thread through a whole book. Three moments from the beginning, middle and end show change better than three from one chapter. A stated limit, such as "I read excerpts, not the whole book," makes a guide more trustworthy, not less.

**Card W1** (*The Wind in the Willows*, Kenneth Grahame, 1908, chapter 1)
> It was small wonder, then, that he suddenly flung down his brush on the floor, said “Bother!” and “O blow!” and also “Hang spring-cleaning!” and bolted out of the house without even waiting to put on his coat.

Words to know: *small wonder* means "no surprise." The Mole has been spring-cleaning all morning.

**Card W1b** (chapter 1, a little later)
> “Hold up!” said an elderly rabbit at the gap. “Sixpence for the privilege of passing by the private road!” He was bowled over in an instant by the impatient and contemptuous Mole

Words to know: *contemptuous* means "looking down on others." A *sixpence* was a small coin.

**Card W2** (chapter 1)
> Never in his life had he seen a river before [...] this sleek, sinuous, full-bodied animal, chasing and chuckling, gripping things with a gurgle and leaving them with a laugh [...] The Mole was bewitched, entranced, fascinated.

Words to know: *sinuous* means "bending and curving."

Words to know: *sculls* are oars; *sculling* is rowing.

**Card G1** (*The Secret Garden*, Frances Hodgson Burnett, 1911, chapter 5)

Mary has asked Martha, a maid, whether she hears someone crying in the house.

**Card G2** (chapter 8)
> There is no doubt that the fresh, strong, pure air from the moor had a great deal to do with it. Just as it had given her an appetite, and fighting with the wind had stirred her blood, so the same things had stirred her mind. [...] Already she felt less “contrary,” though she did not know why.

Words to know: *contrary* means "always wanting the opposite; hard to please."

**Card G3** (chapter 9, inside the garden for the first time)
> “How still it is!” she whispered. “How still!”

> “No wonder it is still,” she whispered again. “I am the first person who has spoken in here for ten years.”

> “I wonder if they are all quite dead,” she said. “Is it all a quite dead garden? I wish it wasn’t.”

**Card G4** (chapter 13)
> “It isn’t the wind now,” she said in a loud whisper. “That isn’t the wind. It is different. It is that crying I heard before.”

The crying leads Mary to Colin, her cousin, who has been kept in his room.

**Card G5** (chapter 18, Mary and Colin talking)
> “Ben Weatherstaff said I was like him,” said Mary. “He said he’d warrant we’d both got the same nasty tempers. I think you are like him too. We are all three alike [...] But I don’t feel as sour as I used to before I knew the robin and Dickon.”

> “I wish I was friends with things,” he said at last, “but I’m not. I never had anything to be friends with, and I can’t bear people.”

Words to know: *warrant* here means "be sure." Ben Weatherstaff is the old gardener. Dickon is a boy from the moor who is friends with animals.

**Card G6** (chapter 27, the last chapter)
> While the secret garden was coming alive and two children were coming alive with it, there was a man wandering about certain far-away beautiful places in the Norwegian fiords and the valleys and mountains of Switzerland and he was a man who for ten years had kept his mind filled with dark and heart-broken thinking.

The man is Mr. Craven, Mary's uncle and Colin's father.

**Card G7** (chapter 27)
> “Where you tend a rose, my lad,
> A thistle cannot grow.”

Words to know: to *tend* is to look after. A *thistle* is a prickly weed.

**Card G8** (chapter 27, the book's last scene, seen from a window of the house)
> Across the lawn came the Master of Misselthwaite and he looked as many of them had never seen him.

The "Master" is Mr. Craven, home at last. Colin is walking beside him.

## Chapter cards (Retold for this pack)

*The Wind in the Willows*, twelve chapters, in order:
1. **The River Bank.** Mole leaves his spring-cleaning, finds the river and meets the Rat, who takes him boating.
2. **The Open Road.** Mole and Rat travel in Toad's cart. A motor-car scares the horse, and Toad falls in love with cars.
3. **The Wild Wood.** In winter, Mole goes into the Wild Wood alone and gets lost. The Rat finds him.
4. **Mr. Badger.** Lost in the snow, Mole and Rat find Badger's house. He takes them in.
5. **Dulce Domum.** Mole smells his old home. The Rat goes back there with him.
6. **Mr. Toad.** His friends try to stop Toad's car craze. He gets away, takes a car, and is locked in prison.
7. **The Piper at the Gates of Dawn.** Mole and Rat search the river for Otter's lost son, Portly, and find him on an island.
8. **Toad's Adventures.** Toad escapes from prison dressed as a washerwoman.
9. **Wayfarers All.** The Rat almost leaves for the South. Mole stops him and helps him settle.
10. **The Further Adventures of Toad.** More escapes. The river sweeps Toad to a hole in the bank: the Rat's.
11. **"Like Summer Tempests Came His Tears."** Toad learns that weasels and stoats from the Wild Wood have taken Toad Hall.
12. **The Return of Ulysses.** The four friends win Toad Hall back. Toad seems changed.

*The Secret Garden* (only the chapters this pack uses):
- **Chapter 5.** Mary hears crying in the house at night. Martha says it is the wind.
- **Chapter 8.** The moor air and a robin start to change Mary. She finds the door to the locked garden.
- **Chapter 9.** Mary is inside the garden, which has been shut for ten years.
- **Chapter 13.** The crying leads Mary to Colin, her cousin, who has been kept in his room.
- **Chapter 18.** Mary and Colin talk about being sour, and about the robin and Dickon.
- **Chapter 27.** The story follows Mr. Craven abroad, then home, where Colin walks across the lawn.

## Teach cards (A true thing about language)

Each card has one idea and a worked example revealed one step per tap.

1. **A long story in pieces.** A novel is a long story told in chapters. Here you read excerpts: short pieces cut from the book. Each card says which chapter it's from.
   - Worked: Card W1 says *chapter 1*. → So this is near the start of the book. → What we learn here is how Mole begins.
2. **Four ways to know a character.** Writers show a character through what they say, what they do, what they think, and how others treat them.
   - Worked: "Hang spring-cleaning!" → That is something Mole says. → It shows he is fed up.
3. **A place has a feeling.** A setting is where and when a story happens. The words about it set the mood: the feeling it gives.
   - Worked: "chasing and chuckling" → The river sounds playful. → The mood is lively and happy.
4. **Alike and different.** To compare, find how two things are alike. To contrast, find how they differ. Use a detail from the book for each.
   - Worked: Mole has "never been in a boat." The Rat says boats are "the only thing." → Different: one is new, one loves it.
5. **One event leads to another.** A plot is a chain of events. Ask what made each event happen.
   - Worked: Mole grabs the oars. → The boat tips over. → The Rat pulls him out.
6. **Characters change.** Watch how the writer describes a character early and late. A turning point is a moment after which they act differently.
   - Worked: Chapter 1: "the triumphant Mole." → Chapter 9: "the tactful Mole." → He has grown more careful of others.
7. **Early questions, late answers.** In a long book, an early chapter can plant a question that a later chapter answers. Each chapter does a job for the whole.
   - Worked: Chapter 1: "We don't go there." → Chapter 3: Mole goes into the Wild Wood. → The warning set up the danger.
8. **How a book is built.** Structure is how a book is put together: the order of chapters, whose story each tells, and what repeats.
   - Worked: Chapter 6 ends with Toad in prison. → Chapter 7 is about Mole and Rat. → We wait to find out about Toad.
9. **Topic and theme.** A topic is what a book is about, in a few words. A theme is what the book seems to say about it.
   - Worked: Topic: home. → Theme: home can call you back, even after adventures.
10. **A theme grows.** Follow a theme through moments early, in the middle and late. Each moment adds something.
    - Worked: Chapter 1: Mole runs from home. → Chapter 5: home calls him back. → The idea is growing.
11. **Summary, not opinion.** A summary says what happens, in order, in a few sentences. It leaves out what you think of it.
    - Worked: "Toad sings" is a summary. → "Toad is silly" is an opinion. → Keep the first, cut the second.
12. **Strong and weak evidence.** Evidence is a detail or quotation that backs up a point. Strong evidence fits the point closely. Weak evidence only touches it.
    - Worked: Point: Mole is loyal. → "His loyalty to his friend stood firm" fits closely. → Strong.
13. **Quotes inside your sentence.** Put a short quotation inside your own sentence, in quotation marks. Read it aloud: the whole sentence should still make sense.
    - Worked: The river is "chasing and chuckling." → Quotation marks show the borrowed words. → The sentence still reads smoothly.
14. **Comparing two books.** A critical comparison goes past "both have animals." It asks how each writer does things differently, and what each expected of readers.
    - Worked: Burnett writes her idea as a rhyme. → Grahame shows his through Mole's nose. → Two ways to carry an idea.

## Benches

### (a) Close Look (Seedling)

**Intro:** Read a card. Then sort what it shows about the character or the place.

**Mole, four ways:** sort four lines into *Says*, *Does*, *Thinks*, *How others treat him*.
- "Hang spring-cleaning!" → Says
- "flung down his brush on the floor" → Does
- "his pride began to whisper that he could do it every bit as well" → Thinks
- "It was the Rat, and he was evidently laughing" → How others treat him
- Feedback for a wrong tray: Look at the card again. Who is speaking, moving or thinking?

**The river's words:** tap three words or phrases on Card W2 that make the river seem alive. Any of "chasing", "chuckling", "gripping", "a gurgle", "a laugh", "animal" fit.
- Feedback when a tapped word is about the Mole: That word is about the Mole. Find one about the river.

**The garden's words:** on Card G3, tap the word Mary says twice about the garden. ("still")
- Done line: A quiet, shut-away place. Ten years with no one talking.

### (b) Side by Side (Seedling, Sapling)

**Intro:** Two characters or two places. Put each card where it fits: one side, the other side, or both.

**Mole and Rat in chapter 1** (cards show their line):
- "never been in a boat before in all my life" → Mole
- "nothing [...] half so much worth doing as simply messing about in boats" → Rat
- "a pleasant laugh" after the boat hits the bank → Rat
- "his pride began to whisper" → Mole
- Lives in a home dug into the ground (Mole's "dark and lowly little house"; the Rat's home, "a dark hole in the bank") → Both

**River and Wild Wood:** Cards W2 and W7 side by side. Sort: "chuckling" → river; "a laugh" → river; "very still" → wood; "draining away" → wood.

**Mary and Colin in chapter 18** (Card G5): "the same nasty tempers" → both, as Mary tells it; "I don't feel as sour as I used to" → Mary; "I can't bear people" → Colin.
- Feedback: Check who says it. Then check whether it's about now or before.

### (c) The Chapter Map (Sapling)

**Intro:** Twelve chapter cards in a row. Some early lines ask a question that a later chapter answers. Join them with string.

**Join the string:** drag (or tap, then tap) each early card to the later chapter it sets up.
- Card W4 (chapter 1, the Wild Wood) → chapter 3
- Card W5 (chapter 1, "I'm never going") → chapter 9
- Card W6 (chapter 2, "O poop-poop!") → chapter 6
- Card W11's first face (chapter 1) → chapter 10
- Feedback for a wrong join: Read the later chapter card. Does it answer this line?

**Mole's line:** three narrator words for Mole, to place on the map: "contemptuous" (chapter 1), "triumphant" (chapter 1), "tactful" (chapter 9). Then: *What changed?*
- Done line: Early on, Mole barges about. Later, he thinks about his friend first.

**The Secret Garden map (second book):** join Card G1 (chapter 5, "It's th' wind") to chapter 13.

### (d) Two Threads (Keeper's Desk)

**Intro:** Grahame tells two stories in one book. Find where they take turns, and where they meet.

**Whose chapter?** Tag chapters 6 to 10: *Toad* or *Mole and Rat*. (6 Toad, ending in prison; 7 Mole and Rat; 8 Toad; 9 Mole and Rat; 10 Toad.)
- Done line: They take turns. At the end of chapter 10, Toad lands at the Rat's door.

**The same face:** Card W11. Tap the words that repeat from chapter 1 to chapter 10. ("Brown", "with whiskers")

**Then, then, then:** Card W7. Tap the three short paragraphs. Then choose what they do (item D3).

**The last chapter:** Card G6. The book leaves the garden for Mr. Craven abroad, then brings him home (Card G8).

### (e) The Thread of a Theme (Sapling, Keeper's Desk)

**Intro:** A theme is an idea a book keeps coming back to. Follow one through the book.

**Home, four times:** order four moment cards from *The Wind in the Willows*: Mole bolts from his home (chapter 1); home calls Mole back (chapter 5); the Rat nearly leaves his home for the South (chapter 9); the friends win Toad Hall back (chapter 12).

**Coming alive:** three moments from *The Secret Garden*: "I wish it wasn't" (chapter 9); "I don't feel as sour as I used to" (chapter 18); "coming alive with it" (chapter 27).

**Say it plain:** choose the summary of chapter 5 that has no opinion in it (item E4).

### (f) Weigh the Evidence (Keeper's Desk)

**Intro:** Bramble says Toad hasn't changed. Dot says he has. Sort the lines from the book.

**Toad's lines:** sort each line of Cards W12 and W13 into *Shows a change*, *Shows he still loves attention*, or *Could be read either way*.
- "Hence forth I will be a very different Toad." → Could be read either way (a promise, not yet a deed)
- "locked the door, drew the curtains" and the song sung twice → Shows he still loves attention
- "making his modest responses" → Shows a change (in how he acts)
- "an object of absorbing interest to every one" → Shows he still loves attention
- "He was indeed an altered Toad!" → Could be read either way (the narrator says it right after the song)
- Done line: Both of them have evidence. The strongest lines say what Toad did and felt, not what he promised.

**No ruling:** the bench never says whether Dot or Bramble is right. *Tell a grown-up which side your strongest line supports.*

**Two books, two ways:** Cards G7 and W8 side by side. Which writer states the idea as a rule? (item F6)

### (g) A Guide for the Next Keeper (Act V reading project; Sapling, Keeper's Desk)

**Intro:** One book. One thread. Three moments. A line from the book for each. And one honest limit.

**Step 1, choose a book:** *The Wind in the Willows* or *The Secret Garden*.

**Step 2, choose a thread:**
- Wind in the Willows: *How Mole changes*; *Has Toad changed?*; *Home*.
- Secret Garden: *How Mary changes*; *Coming alive*.

**Step 3, three moments:** pick one book card from the beginning, one from the middle and one from the end of the book. Keep it as your first version.

**Step 4, check your evidence:** for each moment, is your card strong or weak for this thread? You may swap one card for a stronger one. Your first version stays kept beside the new one.

**Step 5, say a limit:** choose one, or write your own on paper:
- I read parts of the book, not every chapter.
- Another reader could pick different moments.
- My thread is one idea. The book has others.
- I used a retold chapter card for one moment.

**Step 6, the writing:** write your guide on paper, or tell it to a grown-up. *Nothing you write is stored or graded.*

**Kept line:** Bound into the Keeper's Pages: your book, your thread, three moments, your evidence, and your limit.

## Problem sets

Three-choice right answers sit A 8 / B 8 / C 8. Each item shows its own card.

### Set A. Close look (Seedling)

**A1 (3-choice, guided).** Card W1. Steps shown first: 1. Find what Mole does. 2. Find what Mole says. 3. Put them together.
- Prompt: What does the flung brush tell you about Mole right now?
- Choices: He's tired of cleaning and wants to get out. ✔ · He's cross with the Rat for making him clean. (`outside-the-text`) · He's a careful mole who finishes every job. (`ignores-detail`)
- Feedback: Rat: The Rat isn't in the story yet. Mole meets him later. · Careful: He flung the brush down and ran. That's not finishing.
- Hint: What does he do with the brush? Where does he go?
- Explanation: His actions and his words both say: enough cleaning.

**A2 (3-choice).** Card W2.
- Prompt: Which words make the river seem like a living animal?
- Choices: "Never in his life" (`not-the-setting`) · "chasing and chuckling" ✔ · "The Mole was bewitched" (`character-not-setting`)
- Feedback: Never: That's about Mole's life, not the river. · Bewitched: That's how Mole feels, not what the river does.
- Hint: Find words for something an animal does.
- Explanation: Chasing and chuckling are things a playful animal does.

**A3 (3-choice).** Card W2.
- Prompt: How does the river make Mole feel?
- Choices: Scared that it will sweep him off. (`mood-guess`) · Bored, as he's seen many rivers. (`contradicts-text`) · Amazed, as if under a spell. ✔
- Feedback: Scared: Look for a scared word. The card has none. · Bored: The card says he'd never seen a river before.
- Hint: Read the last sentence of the card.
- Explanation: "Bewitched, entranced, fascinated": three words for being amazed.

**A4 (3-choice).** Card G3.
- Prompt: What does "the first person who has spoken in here for ten years" show?
- Choices: The garden has been shut a long time. ✔ · Mary has come here every single day. (`contradicts-text`) · The garden is full of noisy birds singing. (`contradicts-text`)

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Novel:** a long story told in chapters.
- **Excerpt:** a short piece cut from a longer text.
- **Chapter:** one of the main parts a book is divided into.
- **Character:** a person, or animal, in a story.
- **Setting:** where and when a story happens.
- **Mood:** the feeling a place or a scene gives the reader.
- **Plot:** the chain of events in a story.
- **Turning point:** a moment after which a character or the story goes a different way.
- **Structure:** how a text is put together: its order, its parts, what repeats.
- **Thread:** one storyline or idea you can follow through a book.
- **Echo:** words or a picture that come back later in a book.
- **Topic:** what a text is about, in a few words.
- **Theme:** what a text seems to say about its topic.
- **Summary:** what happens, in order, in a few sentences, without opinion.
- **Evidence:** a detail or quotation that backs up a point.
- **Quotation:** someone else's exact words, inside quotation marks.
- **Narrator:** the voice that tells the story.
- **Compare / contrast:** find how things are alike / find how they differ.
