# The Truss Yard: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 182](../specs/182-angle-rules.md). Use as written, after independent spec review and a math check. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion and is never gendered; `{courier}` is the explorer's courier. Pip is he/him, Dot she/her, Bramble she/her (with her magpie), the miller she/her, the baker he/him, Hob Pennick he/him, Nuria she/her, Wren Halloway she/her. Real-world facts are in the [source audit](../source-audits/182-angle-rules.md); items still marked `[check]` stay off screen until cleared. Nothing here is scored or timed.

**Diagram convention used in every item.** Diagrams are *not drawn to scale*, and say so. A small square marks a right angle. On the Parallel Beams, the two beams carry matching arrow marks to show they are parallel. Where a diagonal crosses a beam, the four angles are named by position: **above-left, above-right, below-left, below-right**. At the top beam they are **a, b, c, d**; at the bottom beam **e, f, g, h**, in the same order.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. Roofs for the market (Act II, chapter 1: the weekly market)

**Hook:** The market stalls need new roofs. Hob Pennick brings poles. Pip wants a square roof frame.

**Short:**
> Pip builds a square roof frame. It leans, then flops. Hob adds one pole corner to corner. Now it stands. "Triangles," says Hob.

**Longer:**
> The stalls are coming back to the green, and every stall wants a roof. Hob Pennick drives up with a cart of poles and a bag of pegs.
>
> Pip ties four poles into a square. It looks very fine. Then the baker leans a tray on it, and the square slumps sideways into a squashed diamond.
>
> "It's still got four sides," says Pip, offended.
>
> "Four sides can lean," says Hob. He ties one more pole from corner to corner. Now the frame is two triangles. The baker leans his tray on it again. Nothing moves.
>
> "Three sides can't lean," says Hob. "Once you fix three poles, the shape is fixed."
>
> Pip turns his frame upside down, then on its side. "Still triangles," he says. "Even pointing down."

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> A triangle's three side lengths fix its shape, so a triangle frame cannot lean. A four-sided frame can change shape with the same sides. That is why roofs and bridges are full of triangles.

### Scene 2. The footbridge truss (Act III, chapter 3: the route by the high ford)

**Hook:** Before the path to Vadoalto can open, the footbridge needs its side frames rebuilt. The miller draws the plan.

**Short:**
> The footbridge side is a long row of triangles. "Top beam and bottom beam must stay parallel," says the miller. "The angles tell us."

**Longer:**
> The old footbridge took the worst of the storm. Its two side frames are on trestles in the Truss Yard: a top beam, a bottom beam, and diagonals zigzagging between them, making a long row of triangles.
>
> "Whoever built this knew what they were doing," says the miller. "The old Keeper, I'd guess. Look at these marks." Small notches are cut beside every joint.
>
> She chalks one diagonal. "If the beams are parallel, this angle up here matches that one down there. If they don't match, something has bent."
>
> Bramble crouches with the big wooden square. "This corner's a square corner. That one isn't."
>
> "It isn't meant to be," says the miller. "Measure it anyway. Then we can work out all the others without measuring."
>
> Pip looks at the forty joints. "All of them?"
>
> "That's the good part," says {guide}. "Find a few. Reason out the rest."

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> In a frame like this, the diagonals cross two parallel beams. Angles in matching positions are equal. So one careful measurement, plus reasons, tells you many angles.

### Scene 3. Reed roofs from upstream (Act III, chapter 2: the seed exchange)

**Hook:** Nuria brings reed for roofs. Vadoalto's roof frames have a different shape.

**Short:**
> Nuria sketches a Vadoalto roof: a steep triangle. "*¡Triángulos!*" she says. "Ours are steeper, so the rain runs off fast."

**Longer** (Spanish line for a fluent reader's check):
> Nuria unrolls a drawing of a reed roof. "In Vadoalto, our roofs are steep," she says. "The rain runs off before it soaks in."
>
> The baker looks at Willowbank's flatter roof frames. "Steeper means a sharper top corner?"
>
> "Sharper top, steeper sides," says Nuria. "But the three corners always make the same total. *Siempre.* Always."
>
> "Even for a giant roof?" asks Pip.
>
> "Even for a tiny one," says Nuria. "Size doesn't change it."

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> Every triangle's three angles add to 180°. A steep roof has a smaller top angle and bigger angles at the eaves. The total stays 180°.

### Scene 4. Hollis's bridge drawing (Act IV, chapter 1: the old map and Hollis's account)

**Hook:** A page in Hollis's ledger shows the footbridge frame. Bramble says the new bottom beam sags.

**Short:**
> Bramble measures two angles that should match. They don't. "Then the beams aren't parallel," she says. "The bottom one sags."

**Longer:**
> Tucked in Hollis's ledger is a drawing of the footbridge frame, with every angle written in small neat numbers.
>
> Bramble checks the new frame against it. At the top beam, the diagonal makes 50°. At the bottom, in the same position, she measures 54°.
>
> "Hollis's drawing says both should be 50," she says. "If the beams were parallel, those would match. They don't. So the bottom beam isn't parallel. It sags."
>
> Pip squints along the beam. "It looks straight to me."
>
> "Looks aren't reasons," says Bramble. "Matching angles are."
>
> The miller fetches a level and a long straightedge. The bottom beam does dip, just a little, near the middle.
>
> "Well," says Pip. "You were right. Annoyingly."

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> If two lines are parallel, angles in matching positions are equal. So if those angles are unequal, the lines cannot be parallel. Bramble used the rule backwards, correctly.

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer. Worked steps appear one tap at a time. Spec 181 teaches what an angle is, degrees and the protractor; T3 is only a recap.

**T1. What makes a triangle** (A true thing about math)
> A triangle has three straight sides that join up all the way round. Size, color and which way it points don't matter.
Worked example: 1. A long thin triangle, pointing down. 2. Three sides? Yes. 3. All straight? Yes. 4. Joined up? Yes. 5. It is a triangle.

**T2. Count the corners to name a shape** (A true thing about math)
> Count the straight sides, marking each once. Three: triangle. Four: quadrilateral. Five: pentagon. Six: hexagon. Corners and sides always match.
Worked example: 1. Start at one corner. 2. Mark each side as you count: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5. 3. Five sides, five corners. 4. A pentagon, even a squashed one.

**T3. Square corners** (A true thing about math)
> A square corner is a right angle: a quarter turn, 90°. A carpenter's square fits it exactly. A small square on a diagram marks one.
Worked example: 1. Put the square's corner in the frame's corner. 2. Both arms lie flat along the beams. 3. It's a right angle.

**T4. A straight line shares 180°** (A true thing about math)
> A straight line is a half turn: 180°. Angles side by side on a straight line share out 180°. Find a missing one by taking away.
Worked example: 1. The strut makes 110° with the rail. 2. The other angle: 180 − 110. 3. It is 70°.

**T5. Around a point shares 360°** (A true thing about math)
> A whole turn is 360°. Angles that fill all the way round a point share out 360°. Four right angles make 360°.
Worked example: 1. At the hub: 150°, 90° and one unknown. 2. 150 + 90 = 240. 3. 360 − 240 = 120°.

**T6. Opposite angles match** (A true thing about math)
> Where two straight beams cross, opposite angles are equal. Each one makes a straight line with the same neighbor.
Worked example: 1. One angle is 40°. 2. Its neighbor on the line: 180 − 40 = 140°. 3. The opposite angle also sits beside 140°: 40°.

**T7. Three corners make a straight line** (A true thing about math)
> Cut out a triangle. Tear off its three corners. Fit them point to point. They make a straight line: 180°.
Worked example: 1. Corners of 50°, 60° and 70°. 2. Side by side, no gaps. 3. 50 + 60 + 70 = 180°.

**T8. Bigger triangle, same total** (A true thing about math)
> Make a triangle bigger without changing its shape. The sides grow. The angles stay the same. The total is always 180°.
Worked example: 1. A roof triangle: 50°, 60°, 70°. 2. Double every side. 3. Still 50°, 60°, 70°.

**T9. Find the missing corner** (A true thing about math)
> Know two angles of a triangle? Add them. Take the total from 180°. That is the third angle.
Worked example: 1. A right angle, 90°, and 30°. 2. 90 + 30 = 120. 3. 180 − 120 = 60°.

**T10. Four sides: two triangles** (A true thing about math)
> Draw one line from corner to corner. A quadrilateral becomes two triangles. So its angles add to 2 × 180° = 360°.
Worked example: 1. Angles 80°, 100°, 90° and one unknown. 2. 80 + 100 + 90 = 270. 3. 360 − 270 = 90°.

**T11. Any polygon: count the triangles** (A true thing about math)
> From one corner, draw lines to every other corner. A shape with n sides makes n − 2 triangles. Multiply by 180°.
Worked example: 1. Hexagon: 6 sides. 2. From one corner: 4 triangles. 3. 4 × 180 = 720°.

**T12. Regular shapes and outside angles** (A true thing about math)
> Regular means all sides and angles equal. Only then, divide the total by the number of corners. Outside angles all round always add to 360°.
Worked example: 1. Regular hexagon: 720 ÷ 6 = 120° inside. 2. Outside: 180 − 120 = 60°. 3. 6 × 60 = 360°.

**T13. Slide the crossing** (A true thing about math)
> A diagonal crosses two parallel beams. Slide the top crossing down the diagonal. It lands exactly on the bottom one. Matching positions are equal: corresponding angles.
Worked example: 1. At the top, a = 50°. 2. Slide it down. 3. e sits in the same position: 50°.

**T14. Alternate and co-interior** (A true thing about math)
> Between parallel beams, on opposite sides of the diagonal: alternate angles, equal. On the same side: co-interior angles, adding to 180°.
Worked example: 1. d = 50°. 2. e is alternate to d: 50°. 3. c = 130°, so c + e = 180°.

**T15. Give a reason for every step** (A true thing about math)
> Angle chasing: find one angle, write its reason, use it to find the next. Reasons come from rules, never from how it looks.
Worked example: 1. 65° on a straight line. 2. Neighbor: 180 − 65 = 115°, angles on a straight line. 3. Opposite it: 115°, opposite angles are equal.

**T16. Rectangle facts** (A true thing about math)
> A rectangle has four right angles, and opposite sides are equal. Use those facts and the numbers, never the picture.
Worked example: 1. Frame sides: 7 cm and one unknown. Perimeter 24 cm. 2. Half the perimeter: 24 ÷ 2 = 12, one long side plus one short. 3. 12 − 7 = 5 cm.

**T17. Longer chains** (A true thing about math)
> Name the whole each time: 180° on a line, 360° at a point. Opposite angles are equal, so set them equal. Never take away.
Worked example: 1. Opposite angles: 3x + 20 and 110°. 2. They are equal: 3x + 20 = 110. 3. 3x = 90, so x = 30. 4. Check: 3 × 30 + 20 = 110.

**T18. Three parts can fix a triangle** (A true thing about math)
> These always give one triangle: three sides; two sides and the angle between; two angles and the side between; right angle, longest side and one side.
Worked example: 1. Sticks of 3 m, 4 m and 5 m. 2. Build a triangle. 3. Try to build a different one. 4. Every try is the same triangle, turned or flipped.

**T19. When three parts aren't enough** (A true thing about math)
> Two sides and an angle not between them can make two triangles. Three matching angles fix the shape, but not the size.
Worked example: 1. Sides 6 m and 4 m; 30° not between them. 2. Swing the 4 m side. 3. It meets the base twice: third sides about 7.8 m and 2.6 m.

## Real-world and history cards

**H1. Euclid's triangle proof** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> About 2,300 years ago in Alexandria, Euclid wrote a geometry book called the *Elements*. One part shows why a triangle's three angles make a straight line.
Keeper's note:
> In Book I, Proposition 32, Euclid draws a line through one corner, parallel to the opposite side. Parallel-line angle facts move the other two corners up beside it, making a straight line. Euclid gathered many older results. A much later writer, Proclus, says an older history credited Thales with noticing that opposite angles are equal.

**H2. A roof truss from the 500s** (A true thing from history; Seedling and up)
> At Saint Catherine's Monastery in Sinai, Egypt, a wooden church roof from the 500s still stands. Its triangle frames are often called the oldest roof trusses still standing.
Keeper's note:
> The frame is a king post truss: two sloping rafters, a tie beam along the bottom, and a post down the middle. Historians date the roof to between 548 and 565. Some call it the oldest surviving truss; careful writers say "reputed to be", since nobody has checked every old roof.

**H3. The carpenter's square from Japan** (A true thing from history; Sprout and up)
> Japanese carpenters use an L-shaped steel square called a sashigane. Its two arms meet in a square corner.
Keeper's note:
> It is also called a kanejaku. One arm is about twice as long as the other. Carpenters use it to measure, to mark right angles and to mark lines with ink. Special scales on the back help with the corners of roofs.

- Step 1: How many triangles? *(3.)*

## Bench (e): Parallel Beams

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> Two beams with arrow marks are parallel. The diagonal turns in steps of 5°. Tilt a beam to see the rules stop working.

**Opening:** Slide the top crossing down the diagonal. Where does it land?
**Buttons:** Slide the crossing · Color equal pairs · Color pairs that make 180° · Tilt the bottom beam · Shift the beams
**After tilting:** Now the beams aren't parallel. The slid crossing doesn't fit. The rules need parallel lines.

**Guided item:** At the top beam, a = 50°. Find e, then c.
- Step 1: Slide the crossing. e sits where a was. *(50°.)*
- Step 2: c is opposite b. b is a's neighbor on the beam: 180 − 50 = 130°.
- Step 3: c = 130°. Reason: opposite angles are equal.

## Bench (f): The Chase Board

**Model card:** Every diagram is not drawn to scale. Measuring won't help. Reasons will.
**Reason tiles:** angles on a straight line · angles at a point · opposite angles are equal · angles in a triangle · angles in a quadrilateral · corresponding angles · alternate angles · co-interior angles · rectangle facts
**Buttons:** Pick an angle · Write the number · Choose a reason · Carry it to the diagram
**After each step:** Your angle is on the diagram now. Use it for the next one.

**Guided item:** Euclid's picture. The bottom beam is parallel to the top. At the bottom corner, 50° on the left and 60° on the right.
- Step 1: The middle angle: 180 − 50 − 60 = 70°. *(Angles on a straight line.)*
- Step 2: The top-left corner: 50°. *(Alternate angles.)*
- Step 3: The top-right corner: 60°. *(Alternate angles.)* So 50 + 60 + 70 = 180°.

### Set A. Triangles and corners (Sprout and Seedling)

Sprout items are pictures and words, with no digits; counting corners aloud is enough.

**A1 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which one is a triangle?
- A. Three sides, but one side is curved. *(tests: near-miss accepted, ignores straight)*
- B. Three straight lines with a gap at one corner. *(tests: near-miss accepted, ignores closed)*
- C. A long, thin shape with three straight sides, pointing down. *(right)*
Hint: Check each side: straight? Do they all join up?
Explanation: A triangle has three straight sides joined all the way round. Pointing down is fine.
Feedback A: A curved side means it isn't a triangle. Every side must be straight.
Feedback B: There's a gap. A triangle's sides join all the way round.
Feedback C: Yes. Thin and upside down, still a triangle.

**A2 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: A small blue triangle, then a huge red copy. Same kind of shape?
- A. Yes. Both have three straight sides, joined up. *(right)*
- B. No. The big one is a different shape. *(tests: size changes identity)*
- C. No. Red ones aren't triangles. *(tests: color changes identity)*
Hint: What changed: the sides, or the size and color?
Explanation: Size and color don't matter. Three straight sides, joined up, make a triangle.
Feedback A: Yes. Bigger and red, still a triangle.
Feedback B: Bigger isn't different. Count its sides.
Feedback C: Color doesn't change a shape. Count its sides.

**A3 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which roof frame won't lean when you push it?
- A. A square frame of four poles. *(tests: four sides seem strong)*
- B. A square frame with one pole corner to corner. *(right)*
- C. A frame with five poles round the edge. *(tests: more poles means stronger)*
Hint: Try the wobble test. Which one is made of triangles?
Explanation: The diagonal pole makes two triangles. Triangles can't lean.
Feedback A: Push it. Four sides can lean into a diamond.
Feedback B: Yes. Two triangles, and the frame holds.
Feedback C: Five sides can lean too. Look for triangles.

**A4 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Pip was asked to draw a pentagon. Which drawing works?
- A. A neat shape with six equal sides. *(tests: miscounting sides)*
- B. A squashed shape with five straight sides, joined up. *(right)*
- C. Five straight lines in a zigzag that doesn't close. *(tests: forgetting closed)*
Hint: Count the sides, marking each one. Is it closed?
Explanation: A pentagon is any closed shape with five straight sides. Squashed ones count.
Feedback A: Count again, marking each side. That's six: a hexagon.
Feedback B: Yes. Five sides, closed. Squashed is fine.
Feedback C: It doesn't join up. A pentagon must be closed.

**A5 (Seedling, match)**
Prompt: Match each name to its number of corners.
- triangle → 3
- quadrilateral → 4
- pentagon → 5
- hexagon → 6
Tests: losing count on irregular shapes; mixing up pentagon and hexagon.
Hint: Corners and sides always match. Count either one.
Explanation: Tri means three, quad means four, penta five, hexa six.

**A6 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Bramble turns a square onto its corner. Is it still a quadrilateral?
- A. Yes. It still has four straight sides, joined up. *(right)*
- B. No. Turning it made a new shape. *(tests: orientation as defining)*
- C. Only if you turn it back. *(tests: shape needs a flat base)*

Explanation: Turning doesn't change sides or corners. It is still a quadrilateral, and still a square.
Feedback A: Yes. Four straight sides, whichever way it sits.
Feedback B: Turning changes how it sits, not its sides.
Feedback C: It doesn't need a flat bottom. Count its sides.

### Set B. Lines, points and crossings (Seedling and Sapling)

**B1 (Seedling, 3-choice)**

**B2 (Seedling, number)**

**B5 (Sapling, 3-choice)**

**B6 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Why must opposite angles at a crossing be equal?
- A. You can't know without a protractor. *(tests: withholding; the straight lines already fix it)*
- B. Each makes a straight line with the same neighbor. *(right)*
- C. Because they look the same size. *(tests: reasoning from looks)*

Explanation: Each one plus the same neighbor makes 180°, so they must be equal.

Feedback B: Yes. Same neighbor, same 180°, same size.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **triangle**: a closed shape with three straight sides.
- **quadrilateral**: a closed shape with four straight sides.
- **pentagon**: a closed shape with five straight sides.
- **hexagon**: a closed shape with six straight sides.
- **polygon**: a closed shape made of straight sides.
- **corner (vertex)**: where two sides meet.
- **right angle**: a square corner, a quarter turn, 90°.
- **regular**: all sides equal and all angles equal.
- **straight line angle**: a half turn, 180°.
- **opposite angles**: the angles across from each other where two lines cross; they are equal.
- **parallel**: always the same distance apart, never meeting.
- **diagonal (transversal)**: a line that crosses two or more other lines.
- **corresponding angles**: angles in the same position at two crossings.
- **alternate angles**: angles between parallel lines, on opposite sides of the crossing line.
- **co-interior angles**: angles between parallel lines, on the same side; they add to 180°.
- **outside (exterior) angle**: the turn at a corner when you walk round a shape.
- **truss**: a frame of beams joined into triangles.
- **not drawn to scale**: a picture whose sizes may be off, so use the numbers.
- **rectangle**: four right angles, with opposite sides equal.
- **perimeter**: the distance all the way round a shape.
- **congruent (≅)**: exactly the same shape and size; one fits on the other, turned or flipped.
- **side a**: in triangle ABC, the side opposite corner A.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** what makes a triangle (and a pentagon or hexagon) whatever its size or direction; why triangles keep frames rigid; angles on a straight line (180°) and around a point (360°); opposite angles; the triangle angle sum (180°) and why size doesn't change it; quadrilateral and polygon angle sums by splitting into triangles; inside and outside angles of regular polygons; corresponding, alternate and co-interior angles with parallel lines; missing sides and angles in rectangles; chasing angles step by step with a reason for each step, including a simple equation; and which three parts of a triangle fix it (congruence).

**What it does not claim:** angle measuring and degrees are taught in spec 181; this pack uses them. It does not teach area formulas or similar triangles; its one equation item (F4) is a first step only. Torn corners show the 180° rule for one triangle; Euclid's argument is the reason it holds for all. The bridge, roofs and model angles are made up.
