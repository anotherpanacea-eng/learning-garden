# The Summer Show: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 276](../specs/276-summer-show.md). Use as written, after review. `{guide}` is never gendered. Pronouns: Pip he/him; Dot, Bramble, Nuria and the miller she/her; the librarian they/them; Puff and Ember have no pronouns here. Sources: [audit](../source-audits/276-summer-show.md). Nothing is scored, timed or locked.

**Labels:** **A story from Willowbank** (the town, its people, the hat story in all three versions); **A true thing about language** (how poems, scripts and prose work); **A true thing from history** (real poets, books, dates); **A poem from long ago** (each public-domain poem, with its source line).

**Speaking rule (binding):** every "say it" card is spoken to a grown-up. The garden never listens, records or keeps a voice. A child's own poem is written on paper or said aloud; it is never typed in and never stored.

**Language rule:** every Spanish line sits beside an English line and is marked *(fluent reader)*.

## Arriving

**Place title:** The Summer Show

**First visit** (A story from Willowbank):
> Up in the Story Loft, the librarian has pinned a program to the curtain. Poems between the scenes. A play about a hat. Lanterns on every rail.
>
> "Shows need words," they say. "Poems, scripts, stories. Come and see how each one works."

**What you already know (recall card, links to `#loft`):** You made puppet shows in the Story Loft. A show has scenes. Now meet poems and scripts.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. From book to stage (Act II, chapter 4: the first show)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** The librarian's book has a story about Dot's hat. The first show will put it on stage. Pip wants to know where the "saids" went.

**Short:**
> The hat story becomes a play. A script has names, lines and stage directions. The book's orange sky becomes an orange light.

**Longer:**
> The librarian reads chapter 3 aloud: Dot's hat, the wind, the brook. "That's our scene for the show," they say.
>
> Pip reads the script and frowns. "Where did all the 'saids' go?"
>
> "A script puts the name first," says the librarian. "DOT, then what Dot says."
>
> "And these bits in brackets?"
>
> "Stage directions. Nobody says them. They tell you what to do."
>
> Dot finds her first one. "Dot walks onto the bridge. I can do that."
>
> Puff is handed a big paper fan and told to be the wind. Puff takes this very seriously.
>
> "The book says the sky turned orange," says {guide}. "How do we show a sky?"
>
> The miller hangs an orange lamp over the painted footbridge. Everyone goes quiet.
>
> Pip writes in his notebook: BOOK TELLS. STAGE SHOWS.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> A script puts each speaker's name first, so it needs no "said" and no quotation marks. Stage directions are often printed in brackets or in slanted letters. Actors do them; nobody reads them out.

### Scene 2. Does a poem have to rhyme? (Summer: Market Fair and the Show)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** The summer show has a poem between every scene. Pip says one of them is broken. It doesn't rhyme.

**Short:**
> Pip thinks a poem must rhyme. Bramble says it doesn't, and she's right. Free verse doesn't have to rhyme. A haiku doesn't need them either.

**Longer:**
> Lanterns hang from the willows. The program is pinned to the hat stall, where Dot is not buying a hat.
>
> Between the scenes there are poems: a haiku about bees, a ballad about Dot's hat, and one about the footbridge at night.
>
> Pip reads the footbridge one twice. "This one's broken. Nothing rhymes."
>
> "It's not broken," says Bramble, from the front row, next to Ember. "It's free verse. It doesn't have to rhyme."
>
> "Then how do you know it's a poem?"
>
> "Look at the lines," says Bramble. "Somebody chose where each one stops."
>
> Pip counts the haiku on his claws. Five, seven, five. "This one doesn't rhyme either."
>
> "Told you," says Bramble.
>
> Ember claps for the haiku. Ember claps for everything.

**Keeper's note (A true thing from history):**
> Haiku come from Japan. Matsuo Bashō (1644 to 1694) wrote a famous one: *furu ike ya / kawazu tobikomu / mizu no oto*. An old pond; a frog jumps in; the sound of water. Japanese haiku count sound units called *on*: 5, 7 and 5. English syllables are often longer, so many English haiku use fewer.

### Scene 3. A summer's day (Summer: Market Fair and the Show, Keeper's Desk)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** The librarian ends the summer show with a poem over four hundred years old. Bramble counts the lines. She gets fourteen.

**Short:**
> The librarian reads a sonnet by Shakespeare. It has 14 lines. It compares someone to a summer's day, and says the poem will outlast summer.

**Longer:**
> The last lantern is lit. The librarian stands with a very old book. "Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?" they read.
>
> Bramble counts on her fingers, then borrows Pip's claws. "Fourteen lines."
>
> "A sonnet," says the librarian. "Fourteen lines, and a beat like a heart: da-DUM, five times a line."
>
> Dot listens for the rhymes. "Day and May. Shines and declines."
>
> "Real summers end," says the librarian. "Too hot, too short, the sun clouded over. Then line nine turns. This summer won't fade."
>
> "Whose summer?" asks Pip.
>
> "The person in the poem. For as long as people read it."
>
> Pip looks around at the crowd, all listening. "Well," he says. "It's working."

**Keeper's note (A true thing from history):**
> Shakespeare's sonnets were first printed together in 1609: 154 of them. This is Sonnet 18. The spelling follows a modern transcription that keeps old marks like *dimm'd*: the mark shows a left-out letter that is not said.

### Scene 4. A poem on the bridge (Winter: Lantern Night)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** On the longest night, lanterns line the footbridge. Before the almanac is read, someone says a poem by heart. Tonight, it's you.

**Short:**
> You say "Bed in Summer" by heart on the footbridge. Pip mouths along. When you get stuck, Dot whispers the next words.

**Longer:**
> Lanterns hang all along the footbridge. The fire on the green crackles. The librarian has the almanac under one arm.
>
> "First, a poem," they say. "By heart. Who's ready?"
>
> Everyone looks at you.
>
> You stand by the middle lantern. "In winter I get up at night," you begin.
>
> Pip mouths every word with you. He learned it too, just from listening.
>
> In the second stanza you stop. The birds. Then what?
>
> "Still hopping," Dot whispers, very quietly.
>
> You find it. You finish. "To have to go to bed by day?"
>
> Ember claps first. Ember always claps first.
>
> "In winter it's the other way round," says Pip. "We get to stay up for lanterns."

**Keeper's note (A true thing from history):**
> Robert Louis Stevenson printed "Bed in Summer" in *A Child's Garden of Verses* in 1885. Its first stanza sets winter beside summer: getting up in the dark, going to bed in daylight. Saying a poem by heart for others is called reciting.

### Scene 5. The traveling show (Act V: a traveling show to Vadoalto)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** Vadoalto has never seen a Willowbank show. The hat story must fit in a boat, and make sense to people who have never met Dot.

**Short:**
> The show goes up the river to Vadoalto. One lamp, a blue cloth river, a few words in Spanish. Nuria holds up the sign.

**Longer:**
> The whole show has to fit in one boat: one lamp, one blue cloth river, the paper fan, and Dot's hat, which Dot will not pack.
>
> "They've never met Dot," says the librarian. "So the show has to explain the hat."
>
> "Everyone understands a hat," says Dot.
>
> Pip adds one line at the start anyway: "This is Dot. She loves this hat."
>
> At Vadoalto's landing, Nuria holds up the sign:
>
> **Esta noche: el sombrero de Dot.** *(fluent reader)*
> Tonight: Dot's hat.
>
> "¡Bienvenidos!" she calls. *(fluent reader)* Welcome!
>
> The orange lamp comes on. Puff waves the fan. The hat flies. Vadoalto gasps, then laughs.
>
> Afterwards Nuria asks Dot where she bought it.
>
> "Willowbank," says Dot. "There's a whole stall."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> Changing a story for a new audience is called adapting it. You choose what to explain, what to cut and what to change. Here the show adds one line, so strangers meet Dot before her hat flies.

## The texts

### The hat story, three ways (A story from Willowbank, newly written)

**1. Prose: from a made-up Willowbank book, *Tales from the Footbridge***

**Chapter 3. The Hat Goes Sailing**
> The evening was warm, and the sky over Willow Brook had turned orange. Dot was crossing the footbridge with the last letters of the day when the wind came around the mill. It lifted her hat clean off her head.
>
> The hat spun once in the air. Then it landed on the brook, upside down, like a little boat, and sailed away.
>
> "My hat!" cried Dot.
>
> Pip ran along the bank, shouting at the hat to stop. It did not stop. It sailed all the way to the millpond, where the miller fished it out with her net.
>
> Dot put it back on. It dripped. "It needed a wash," she said.

**2. Play script: *The Hat Goes Sailing*, a play in one scene, made from chapter 3**

**Cast:** DOT, the postmaster · PIP, a fox · THE MILLER · THE WIND, played by Puff with a paper fan

**Scene 1. The footbridge. Evening.**

> *(An orange light. DOT walks onto the bridge with a bag of letters. PIP sits on the bank, eating an apple.)*
>
> DOT: Last letters of the day. Then supper.
>
> *(THE WIND rushes in, waving the fan.)*
>
> PIP *(looking up)*: Dot, hold on to your hat!
>
> *(Too late. The hat flies off and lands on the blue cloth river.)*
>
> DOT *(crying out)*: My hat!
>
> *(THE WIND blows the hat slowly across the stage. PIP runs along the bank.)*
>
> PIP: Stop, hat! Stop!
>
> *(THE MILLER steps in with a net and scoops up the hat.)*
>
> THE MILLER: Is this anybody's boat?
>
> *(DOT puts the hat on. It drips.)*
>
> DOT: It needed a wash.
>
> *(The lights go down.)*

**3. Poem: *The Ballad of Dot's Hat***

> When Dot came out across the bridge
> With letters, late in day,
> The wind came round the miller's wall
> And took her hat away.
>
> It spun up high, it spun around,
> It landed on the brook,
> And sailed off like a little boat
> While Dot could only look.
>
> Then Pip ran down along the bank
> And shouted, loud and clear,
> "Come back!" The hat would never stop
> Until the mill was near.
>
> The miller fished it with her net:
> "Is this a boat, or hat?"
> Dot put it on. It dripped and dripped.
> "A bath! It needed that."

### Poems from long ago

**Bed in Summer** (A poem from long ago). By Robert Louis Stevenson, from *A Child's Garden of Verses* (1885).
> In winter I get up at night
> And dress by yellow candle-light.
> In summer quite the other way,
> I have to go to bed by day.
>
> I have to go to bed and see
> The birds still hopping on the tree,
> Or hear the grown-up people's feet
> Still going past me in the street.
>
> And does it not seem hard to you,
> When all the sky is clear and blue,
> And I should like so much to play,
> To have to go to bed by day?

**When I Heard the Learn'd Astronomer** (A poem from long ago; Sapling). By Walt Whitman, from *Leaves of Grass* (first printed in *Drum-Taps*, 1865).
> When I heard the learn'd astronomer,
> When the proofs, the figures, were ranged in columns before me,
> When I was shown the charts and diagrams, to add, divide, and measure them,
> When I sitting heard the astronomer where he lectured with much applause in the lecture-room,
> How soon unaccountable I became tired and sick,
> Till rising and gliding out I wander'd off by myself,
> In the mystical moist night-air, and from time to time,
> Look'd up in perfect silence at the stars.

**Word help:** *learn'd* means learned, someone who has studied a lot. An *astronomer* studies stars. *Unaccountable* here means without knowing why.

**Sonnet 18** (A poem from long ago; Keeper's Desk). By William Shakespeare, first printed in 1609.
> Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?
> Thou art more lovely and more temperate:
> Rough winds do shake the darling buds of May,
> And summer's lease hath all too short a date:
> Sometime too hot the eye of heaven shines,
> And often is his gold complexion dimm'd,
> And every fair from fair sometime declines,
> By chance, or nature's changing course untrimm'd:
> But thy eternal summer shall not fade,
> Nor lose possession of that fair thou ow'st,
> Nor shall death brag thou wander'st in his shade,
> When in eternal lines to time thou grow'st,
> So long as men can breathe, or eyes can see,
> So long lives this, and this gives life to thee.

**Word help:** *thee, thou, thy* are old words for you and your. *Temperate* means mild, never too much. *Summer's lease* is summer's borrowed time. *The eye of heaven* is the sun. *Fair* means beauty, or a beautiful thing. *Ow'st* means own. *Eternal lines* are the lines of this poem.

**Get Up and Bar the Door, first two stanzas** (A poem from long ago; Keeper's Desk). A Scottish ballad, first printed in 1776. This text is from Francis James Child's *English and Scottish Ballads* (1858).
> It fell about the Martinmas time,
> And a gay time it was than,
> That our gudewife had puddings to mak,
> And she boil'd them in the pan.
>
> The wind blew cauld frae east and north,
> And blew into the floor;
> Quoth our gudeman to our gudewife,
> "Get up and bar the door."

**Word help:** *Martinmas* is a feast day in November. *Than* is Scots for then. *Gudewife* and *gudeman* mean the woman and man of the house. *Mak* is make. *Cauld frae* is cold from. *Quoth* means said. To *bar* a door is to shut it with a wooden bar.

**What happens next (A story people told):** The two make a deal: whoever speaks first must bar the door. At midnight two strangers walk in and eat the puddings. Nobody speaks. At last the strangers' plans make the husband jump up and shout. He spoke first, so his wife tells him to bar the door.

**Shakespeare's stage direction** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk). In *The Winter's Tale* (first printed in 1623), act 3, scene 3, one direction reads in full: *Exit, pursued by a bear.*

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer; worked-example steps appear one tap at a time. Cards are **A true thing about language** unless marked.

**Card 1. Rhyme**
> Words rhyme when they end with the same sound. Two rhyming lines side by side make a rhyming couplet.

Worked example: *quick, stick.*
1. Say quick. Say stick.
2. Both end in "ick."
3. "A fox is quick, / jumping on a fallen stick": a rhyming couplet.

**Card 2. Claps and beat**
> Clap a word: each clap is one syllable. Many poems have a steady beat, like footsteps, with strong and soft claps.

Worked example: *jumping on a fallen stick.*
1. Jump-ing: two claps.
2. On, a, stick: one each. Fall-en: two.
3. Seven claps in all.

**Card 3. A pattern poem**
> A pattern poem repeats a shape. Keep the shape, change the words. It's a good way to start your own poem.

Worked example: *What is quick?*
1. Ask: What is quick?
2. Answer in the pattern: A fox is quick.
3. Add a rhyming line: jumping on a fallen stick.

**Card 4. Learning by heart**
> Learn a poem a few lines at a time. Say it, hide a word, say it again. Then say it to someone.

Worked example: *Bed in Summer, line 1.*
1. Say: In winter I get up at night.
2. Hide "night." Say it again.
3. Hide "winter" too. Say it again.

**Card 5. Saying a poem well**
> Let your voice follow the meaning. Slow down for a quiet line. Breathe at a period, not always at a line's end.

Worked example: *Bed in Summer, last stanza.*
1. It is one long question.
2. Keep going through the commas.
3. Let your voice rise at "by day?"

**Card 6. Haiku**
> A haiku is a short poem about one moment, often with a season clue. In English, many poets use three lines of 5, 7 and 5 syllables. Others use fewer.

Worked example: *Bees in the bean rows.*
1. Three lines.
2. Claps: 5, 7, 5.
3. Season clue: bees in the bean rows mean summer.

**Card 7. Free verse**
> Free verse has no set rhyme and no steady beat. Its lines can be any length. Where a line stops still matters.

Worked example: *The Footbridge at Night.*
1. Last words: creaks, itself, night, moon, going. No rhymes.
2. Lines are long and short.
3. "all night," alone on a line makes you wait.

**Card 8. Ballad**
> A ballad is a story told as a song or poem. It often has four-line stanzas, and lines 2 and 4 rhyme.

Worked example: *The Ballad of Dot's Hat, stanza 2.*
1. It tells part of a story: the hat lands and sails.
2. Four lines.
3. Brook and look rhyme: lines 2 and 4.

**Card 9. Poem, play, prose**
> A poem is set out in lines, often in stanzas. A play script has a cast, speakers' names and stage directions. Prose runs on in sentences and paragraphs.

Worked example: *the hat story, three ways.*
1. Prose: chapter 3, five paragraphs.
2. Script: a cast list, DOT: and PIP:, directions in brackets.
3. Poem: four stanzas, rhymes in lines 2 and 4.

**Card 10. From book to stage**
> A show can keep a book's details, change them or add new ones. Light, set and sound can show what a book tells in words.

Worked example: *the orange sky.*
1. Book: "the sky ... had turned orange."
2. Script: *(An orange light.)*
3. The stage kept the detail and showed it with light.

**Card 11. Pictures and words**
> Pictures can change how the same words feel. Warm colors can feel cheerful. Dark colors can feel lonely or worried.

Worked example: *stanza 2, two ways.*
1. Same words: the hat sails off.
2. Warm orange picture: a funny adventure.
3. Dark blue picture: the hat feels far away.

**Card 12. Sonnet (Keeper's Desk)**
> A sonnet is a 14-line poem. Most of Shakespeare's sonnets rhyme ABAB CDCD EFEF GG: three groups of four lines, then a couplet.

Worked example: *Sonnet 18, lines 1 to 4.*
1. Day: A. Temperate: B.
2. May rhymes with day: A.
3. Date pairs loosely with temperate: B. ABAB.

**Card 13. Meter (Keeper's Desk)**
> Meter is a poem's beat pattern. Da-DUM five times a line is iambic pentameter. Ballads often go four beats, then three.

Worked example: *Sonnet 18, line 3.*
1. Rough WINDS do SHAKE the DAR-ling BUDS of MAY.
2. Ten syllables.
3. Five da-DUM pairs.

**Card 14. Run-on lines and pauses (Keeper's Desk)**
> When a sentence runs past the end of a line, that is enjambment. A pause in the middle of a line, often at a comma, is a caesura.

Worked example: *two lines.*
1. "the brook keeps talking to itself / all night": the sentence runs on.
2. "So long as men can breathe, or eyes can see": a pause at the comma.
3. Run-on, then pause.

**Card 15. Sound effects (Keeper's Desk)**
> Alliteration repeats first sounds. Assonance repeats vowel sounds. Onomatopoeia is a word that sounds like what it means.

Worked example: *three poems.*
1. Alliteration: "mystical moist" (Whitman).
2. Assonance: "shake" and "May" (Shakespeare).
3. Onomatopoeia: "creaks" (the footbridge poem).

**Card 16. Staging (Keeper's Desk)**
> A director chooses the set, the lights, the sound and where actors stand. Two stagings of one script can make an audience feel different things.

Worked example: *the hat flies.*
1. Script: *(Too late. The hat flies off ...)*
2. Dot's staging: bright light, quick whistling. Funny.
3. Bramble's staging: light fades to blue, wind sound. Worried.

**Card 17. Performing (Keeper's Desk)**
> Performers use volume, pace, pauses, stillness and silence. Sometimes a stage direction says how. Often the performer decides.

Worked example: *My hat!*
1. Direction: *(crying out)*. Loud and sudden.
2. Next: the hat drifts. Stand still, watch it.
3. A short silence before "Stop, hat!"

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Poem:** writing set out in lines, often with a beat or rhymes.
- **Line:** one row of words in a poem.
- **Stanza:** a group of lines in a poem, set apart by a gap.
- **Rhyme:** words that end with the same sound, like quick and stick.
- **Rhyming couplet:** two lines in a row that rhyme.
- **Syllable:** one beat of a word. Clap it: jump-ing has two.
- **Beat:** the steady pulse you can clap or tap in a poem.
- **Pattern poem:** a poem that repeats a shape, changing some words.
- **Haiku:** a short poem about one moment. In English, often three lines of 5, 7 and 5 syllables, but not always.
- **Season clue:** a word that tells the time of year, like bees or snow.
- **Free verse:** a poem with no set rhyme and no steady beat.
- **Ballad:** a story told as a song or poem, often in four-line stanzas.
- **Narrative poem:** a poem that tells a story.
- **Sonnet:** a poem of 14 lines with a set rhyme pattern.
- **Rhyme scheme:** the pattern of rhymes, written with letters like ABAB.
- **Meter:** the beat pattern of a poem's lines.
- **Iambic pentameter:** da-DUM five times in a line: ten syllables.
- **Turn:** the place where a poem changes direction, often with "but."
- **Enjambment:** a sentence running past the end of a line.
- **Caesura:** a pause in the middle of a line.
- **Alliteration:** words close together that start with the same sound.
- **Assonance:** words close together with the same vowel sound.
- **Onomatopoeia:** a word that sounds like what it means, like creak.
- **Prose:** ordinary writing in sentences and paragraphs.
- **Paragraph:** a group of sentences about one thing, starting on a new line.
- **Chapter:** one part of a book.
- **Play script:** the written words of a play, for actors.
- **Cast list:** the list of characters at the start of a script.
- **Stage direction:** words in a script that tell actors what to do. Nobody says them aloud.
- **Scene:** one part of a play, usually in one place and time.
- **Set:** the things on stage that show where the play happens.
- **Staging:** how a show is put on: set, lights, sound, places.
- **Director:** the person who makes the staging choices.
- **Slanted letters:** letters that lean to the right, often used for stage directions.
- **Brackets:** the marks ( ) around extra words.
- **Recite:** to say a poem aloud, usually by heart.
- **By heart:** remembered, without looking.
- **Volume:** how loud or soft.
- **Pace:** how fast or slow.
- **Mood:** the feeling a poem, picture or scene gives.
- **Caption:** words that go with a picture.
- **Adapt:** to change a story to suit a new form or audience.
- **Audience:** the people watching or listening.
- **Postmaster:** the person who runs a post office.
- **Millpond:** the pond that feeds a water mill.
- **Program:** a list of what happens in a show, in order.
- **Exit:** a stage direction meaning a character leaves the stage.
- **Pursued:** chased.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** reading, writing (on paper) and performing poems and scripts, from rhyme and claps to the sonnet and staging; the teach cards list each step. A pattern poem's rhyme or near-rhyme is the child's own and is never graded. UK schools call slanted letters "italics," and the end mark a "full stop"; this pack uses US terms in child text.

**About speaking and writing:** reciting and performing happen out loud, to you. The garden never records a voice and never stores the child's own poem. If you help with a forgotten word, that is ordinary rehearsal help, like a prompter in a theater.

**About the texts:** the hat story, the ballad, the haiku and the two new poems were written for this pack. The old poems and the stage direction are public domain, each dated where it appears; sources are in the audit. In *The Winter's Tale* the bear chases a character off stage; the pack quotes only the direction. In the full ballad of *Get Up and Bar the Door*, the strangers plan to shave the husband with hot pudding water and kiss his wife; the pack says only that their plans make him shout. Child's 1858 notes quote an earlier writer, William Stenhouse, saying the ballad gave a scene to a London stage show, *No Song, No Supper*, in 1790: a ballad turned into a play.

**What it does not claim:** 5, 7 and 5 is a common convention for English haiku, not a rule, and the pack says so. Stresses in a line can be read more than one way; the pack counts syllables and teaches da-DUM as the usual pattern, not a rule for every line. The Spanish lines are newly written and need a fluent reader.
