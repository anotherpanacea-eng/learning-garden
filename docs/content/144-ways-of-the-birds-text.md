## Teach cards

**Teach 1: What migration is.** (A true thing about birds)
> Migration is a journey animals make every year. They go, and then they come back.
- Worked example, one step per tap:
  1. A swallow raises chicks in Willowbank in summer.
  2. In autumn it flies south to Africa.
  3. In spring it flies back north.
  4. Away and back, every year: that's migration.

**Teach 2: Why birds go.**
> Birds go where the food is. In winter, many kinds of food run out up north.
- Worked example:
  1. A swallow eats flying insects.
  2. In a Willowbank summer, the air is full of them.
  3. In winter, flying insects almost vanish here.
  4. In Africa in our winter, there are insects. So the swallow goes.

**Teach 3: Soaring birds and the sea.**
> Warm air rises over sunny land. Soaring birds ride it. Over the sea, there's little rising air.
- Worked example:
  1. A stork circles up in rising warm air.
  2. It glides to the next rising air.
  3. Over the sea, the rising air fades.
  4. So storks cross where the sea is narrowest.

**Teach 4: Using a key.**
> A key names a bird by asking questions about what you can see.
- Worked example, a crane:
  1. Neck and legs stretched out? Yes.
  2. Calling loudly, in a line or V? Yes.
  3. The key says: common crane.
  4. Add one mark on the crane row.

**Teach 5: Not only birds.** (A true thing about animals)
> Whales, salmon and butterflies migrate too. Millions of insects cross one pass in these mountains.
- Detail card: The painted lady that flies south is not the one that comes back. Its great-grandchildren might be. The trip takes several generations.

**Teach 6: Staying or going.**
> A dormouse hibernates. It stays and sleeps through winter. A stork migrates. It travels.

**Teach 7: Finding the way.** (A true thing about birds)
> Birds use the Sun, the stars, Earth's magnetism and landmarks. Many use several at once.
- Detail card: Birds don't plan a trip like we do. They are born with the urge to go, and shorter days set it off. How the magnetic sense works is still being studied.

**Teach 8: What is a bird?**
> Birds have feathers, beaks and backbones. They lay eggs with hard shells. Some birds can't fly.
- Detail card: A bat flies but has fur, so it's a mammal. A butterfly flies but has six legs, so it's an insect.

**Swallow over open sea:** Swallows flap the whole way. They cross the sea, and even the Sahara.
**Honey buzzard (older):** On past the desert and the savanna, to the edges of the rainforest.

**Compare card:** Stork: over the pass and the Strait. Swallow: can take the sea. Why are their ways different?
- After a choice: Storks soar on rising air, which is weak over the sea. Swallows flap, so the sea is no wall for them.

**1.2 (Sprout)** Neck stretched out. Silent. Circling. Which bird?

- B. Black kite `[skipped-step: ignores "neck stretched out"]` · Feedback: A kite's neck is short. Look at step 1 again.
- C. White stork ✓ · Feedback: Yes. Neck out, silent, circling: a white stork.
- Hint: Use the key. Step 1, then step 2.
- Explanation: The key splits on neck shape, then on calling.

**1.3 (Sprout)** Which bird passed most on the short morning?
- A. White stork ✓ · Feedback: Yes. Four storks, more than any other bird.
- B. Honey buzzard `[first-seen: picks a bird seen early or often, not counted]` · Feedback: Count the honey buzzard marks: three. Storks have more.
- C. Black kite `[smallest-bar: reads the shortest bar]` · Feedback: Black kites have the shortest row, two. Look for the longest.

**1.4 (Seedling)** How many birds were counted on the September morning in all?
- Answer: 120.
- Steps: 36 + 24 = 60. 15 + 45 = 60. 60 + 60 = 120.
- Hint: Add two numbers at a time.
- Explanation: Pairing numbers that make round totals makes adding easier.

**1.5 (Seedling)** How many more honey buzzards than white storks?
- Answer: 21.
- Steps: 36 honey buzzards. 15 white storks. 36 − 15 = 21.
- Hint: "How many more" means find the difference.

**1.6 (Seedling)** The chart goes up in 5s. How many steps high is the swallow bar?

- C. 9 ✓ · Feedback: Yes. Nine fives make 45.
- Hint: Count up in fives: 5, 10, 15…
- Explanation: The scale tells you what each step is worth. 45 ÷ 5 = 9.

**1.7 (Sapling)** 72 cranes. How many crossed gates, and how many extra marks?

### Set 2. Long ways

**2.1 (Seedling)** A stork flies 200 km on each of 3 good days. How far is that?
- Answer: 600 km.
- Steps: 200 + 200 + 200 = 600. Or 3 × 200 = 600.
- Hint: Add one day's distance three times.
- Explanation: Tracked storks averaged about 200 to 300 km a day on migration, sometimes over 500. Bad days are shorter.

**2.2 (Seedling)** Which way would a white stork most likely take?
- A. Straight across the open sea, the shortest way `[soars-over-sea]` · Feedback: Rising warm air is weak over the sea. Storks avoid long sea crossings.
- B. Stay in the valley, out of the wind `[cold-only]` · Feedback: Staying doesn't solve the food problem. Storks head south.
- C. Over the pass and across the narrow Strait ✓ · Feedback: Yes. That way keeps the stork over land almost all the time.
- Hint: Where can a soaring bird find rising warm air?
- Explanation: Soaring birds stay over land and cross the sea where it is narrowest.

**2.3 (Seedling)** Why do storks avoid flying over wide seas?
- A. There's little rising warm air over the sea. ✓ · Feedback: Yes. Without it, a stork must flap, and that tires it fast.
- B. Storks can't flap their wings at all. `[cannot-flap]` · Feedback: Storks can flap. It just costs far more energy than soaring.
- C. Storks don't know the sea is there. `[just-know]` · Feedback: Birds use landmarks like coasts. The trouble is the air over the water.
- Hint: Think about what lifts a soaring bird.
- Explanation: Soaring saves energy only where warm air rises, mostly over sunny land.

**2.4 (Sapling)** How many good days from the pass to the Strait?
- Given (simplified map): pass to Strait 900 km. A good day: 300 km, or 200 km.
- Answer: 3 days at 300 km. 4 and a half days at 200 km. So about 3 to 5 good days.
- Steps: 900 ÷ 300 = 3. 900 ÷ 200 = 4.5.
- Hint: How many 300s make 900?
- Explanation: Real trips take longer, because birds wait out rain and wind. The map distance is a rounded model number.

**2.5 (Sapling)** About how many times longer is the sea crossing than the Strait?
- Given: sea crossing on the map 600 km. Strait about 14 km.
- A. About 4 times `[place-value-slip]` · Feedback: 14 × 4 is only 56. Try much bigger.
- B. About 40 times ✓ · Feedback: Yes. 600 ÷ 14 is about 43. That's a long way without rising air.
- C. About 400 times `[place-value-slip]` · Feedback: 14 × 400 is 5,600. That's too big.
- Hint: 14 × 10 = 140. How many 140s near 600?
- Explanation: 14 × 40 = 560, close to 600. Books give 13 or 14 km for the Strait; either way it's about 40 times. The 600 km is a model number.

**2.6 (Sapling)** A tern's year of flying: about how many times around Earth?
- Given: a tern flies about 70,000 km a year. Earth is 40,075 km around.
- A. About 17 times `[digit-shuffle]` · Feedback: 17 trips would be over 680,000 km. Far too many.
- B. About 7 times `[place-value-slip]` · Feedback: 7 trips would be about 280,000 km. Still too many.
- C. Less than 2 times ✓ · Feedback: Yes. 70,000 ÷ 40,075 is about 1.75. Nearly twice around!
- Hint: Two trips around would be about 80,000 km.
- Explanation: Tracked terns from Greenland and Iceland averaged about 70,000 km a year. Dutch terns flew about 90,000 km, over twice around.

**2.7 (Keeper's Desk)** In a lifetime, how many times the Moon's distance could a tern fly?
- Given: about 30 years, about 70,000 km a year. Moon about 384,400 km away.
- Answer: About 5 and a half times (more than 5, fewer than 6). That's more than two trips there and back.
- Steps: 30 × 70,000 = 2,100,000 km. 2,100,000 ÷ 384,400 ≈ 5.5. Two round trips are about 1,537,600 km; three would be about 2,306,400 km.
- Hint: First find the lifetime distance.
- Explanation: The oldest known Arctic tern was 34. Not every tern lives 30 years, so this is a "could be" figure, not a fact about one bird.

- Explanation: Each place gives birds something different: food, a hard crossing, or a winter home.

**3.6 (Sapling)** How did storks come to have long, broad wings for soaring?
- A. Each stork's wings grew bigger because it needed them. `[lifetime-adapt]` · Feedback: One bird can't grow new wings by needing them.
- B. Storks practiced soaring until their wings stretched out. `[use-stretch]` · Feedback: Practice doesn't change wing shape. Chicks inherit their wings.
- C. Over many generations, storks with suited wings raised more young. ✓ · Feedback: Yes. That's how features build up over a very long time.
- Hint: Think about parents, chicks, and many, many years.
- Explanation: Body features like wing shape come about over many generations, not in one bird's life.

**3.7 (Sapling)** Where do wild penguins live?
- A. In the Arctic, with the polar bears. `[penguins-north]` · Feedback: Polar bears are in the north. Penguins live in the south.
- B. In the Southern Hemisphere, including Antarctica. ✓ · Feedback: Yes. No wild penguins live in the Arctic.
- C. Near both poles, wherever there is ice. `[penguins-both-poles]` · Feedback: Seals live near both. Penguins live only in the south.
- Hint: Penguins and polar bears never meet in the wild.
- Explanation: The Arctic is an ocean ringed by land; Antarctica is a continent ringed by ocean. Penguins live in the south.

### Set 4. Keeper's Desk: how do we know?

**4.1** Willowbank counted 72 cranes. Vadoalto counted 64 that morning. What's the best explanation?
- A. Vadoalto's counter made a mistake and missed eight. `[blame-error]` · Feedback: Possible, but nothing here shows it. Look for another reason.
- B. Eight cranes turned around and went back north. `[story-not-evidence]` · Feedback: Nothing in either tally shows that. It's a guess.
- C. They watched from different places, so each saw different birds. ✓ · Feedback: Yes. "Two eyes see half the sky." Each viewpoint misses some birds.
- Hint: Were the two counters standing in the same spot?
- Explanation: Counts depend on where you stand. Real watch points use the same place and method so counts can be compared over years.

**4.2** Setup: two counts on 25 October, 72 cranes one year and 9 the next.
True, or not true from this record?
- "Fewer cranes passed the Notch on that day the next year." True from this record.
- "There are fewer cranes in Europe now." Not from this record.
- "A change in wind sent the cranes another way." Not from this record (possible, but not shown).
- Misconception tested: `[one-day-trend]`.
- Hint: What does the record actually measure?
- Explanation: Two single days can't show a trend. Birds may have crossed on another day, or elsewhere.

**4.3** Setup: made-up season totals for cranes at the Notch: 5,200, then 4,900, then 5,400, then 5,100.
What can you say?
- A. Cranes are rising fast. `[overclaim]` · Feedback: Numbers went down and up. The highest and lowest are only 500 apart.
- B. No clear rise or fall here in these seasons. ✓ · Feedback: Yes. Careful, and it says exactly where.
- C. Four seasons tell us nothing at all. `[underclaim]` · Feedback: They do tell us something about this pass. Just not the whole story.
- Hint: Is there a clear direction, up or down?
- Explanation: Whole seasons counted the same way are much better evidence than single days. Even so, one pass can't speak for every crane.
- **Say why (grown-up reads):** What else would you want to know before saying cranes are doing well?

**4.4** A stork ringed in Germany is found in Spain one winter. What does that show?
- A. That stork was in both places. ✓ · Feedback: Yes. One ring, two places, one bird.
- B. All storks spend the winter in Spain. `[one-to-all]` · Feedback: One bird can't speak for all storks. Many still go to Africa.
- C. The stork flew straight there without stopping. `[route-overclaim]` · Feedback: A ring shows two places. It can't show the route between.
- Hint: A ring is read only where the bird is found.
- Explanation: Rings show start and end points. Tracking tags show whole routes. Counts show how many pass.

## Key terms

- **Migration:** a journey animals make every year, away and back again.
- **Migrant:** an animal that migrates.
- **Hibernate:** to stay in one place and sleep through winter.
- **Thermal:** a column of warm air rising from sunny ground.
- **Soar:** to rise and glide on moving air, hardly flapping.
- **Pass:** a low place in a mountain ridge where it is easier to cross.
- **Strait:** a narrow strip of sea between two pieces of land.
- **Key:** a set of questions that helps you name something by what you see.
- **Tally:** marks you make to count, crossed in fives.
- **Vertebrate:** an animal with a backbone.
- **Adaptation:** a body feature or behavior that helps an animal live where it does.
- **Habitat:** the place where an animal lives and finds what it needs.
- **Ringing:** putting a small numbered ring on a bird's leg to learn where it goes.

## Off-screen invitations

- With a grown-up, watch the sky on a clear autumn day. Do you see birds flying in a line or a V? Which way are they going?
- Make your own tally of birds you see from a window for ten minutes. Which kind did you see most?
- Look at a bird and a bat in a book. Find three differences.

Watch birds from a distance. Never touch or disturb nests, eggs or chicks. Off-screen invitations record nothing.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** migration as a yearly round trip driven by food, daylight for raising young and nesting places; why soaring birds funnel over mountain passes and narrow straits; how birds navigate (with what is still unknown named); five habitats birds pass through; birds as one vertebrate group, with bats and butterflies as fliers that are not birds; counting with tallies, a table, a bar chart and a key; and what one day's count cannot show.

**What it does not claim:** the High Notch, its counts and Nuria's tally are made up. The map is simplified and its distances are rounded model numbers. Real routes vary with wind and weather. It does not teach why the poles have midnight Sun (Earth's tilt), how the magnetic sense works, or full rainforest layer ecology. Wing shape is said to come about over generations; the pack does not model natural selection.

**Sources in brief:** Arctic tern tracking studies (Greenland, Iceland, Netherlands), satellite tracking of white storks, Organbidexka and Lindus count records, field guides, and NASA for Earth's circumference (40,075 km) and the Moon's average distance (384,400 km). Facts were checked from search excerpts; see `docs/source-audits/144-ways-of-the-birds.md` for the numbered list and what remains open.

**Off-screen activity safety:** outdoor birdwatching is adult-led. Stay on paths, keep away from nests, and never approach or handle wild birds or bats.
