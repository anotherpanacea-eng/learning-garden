## b1. The low spot

**Card:** Keep everything dry in a spring flood. You have 3 coins.

**Worked first test (first visit only):** one step per tap.
1. "Let's test the bank as it is, before we build anything. Press Test."
2. "The water came up 2 boards. Look along the bank. Where did it get through?"
3. "The low corner is only 1 board high. That's where the water came over."
4. "What could you add there? Try it, and test again."

**Goal met:** Everything stayed dry in the spring flood. A small fix in the right place.

## b2. A bigger flood

**Card:** A big flood is coming in the model. Keep the homes lane, the bridge road and the mill path dry. Then choose what else to protect. You have 5 coins.

**After the first test:** The big flood got through in more places. You can't protect everything with 5 coins. What matters most to you?

**Goal met:** The homes lane, the bridge road and the mill path all stayed dry. Now: what did you choose to protect, and what got wet?

**Keep my design** opens the claim stems (below).

## b3. The wearing bank

**Card:** Three strips of bank, all the same, on the same slope. One is bare soil. One has sandbags. One has grass with willow roots. We'll pour the same water on each, three times.

**Fair test line:** Same slope, same soil, same pour, same height. Only one thing is different: what's on the bank.

**Pour button:** Pour {n} of 3

**What happens:**

| Pour | Bare soil | Sandbags | Grass and willow roots |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | The water cuts a little groove. Muddy water runs off. | The bags hold the water back. | Water runs over the grass. The soil stays put. The water runs off fairly clear. |
| 2 | The groove is deeper. More soil washes away. | The bags still hold. | The same as before. The roots hold the soil. |
| 3 | A gully. Lots of muddy water. | The water goes over the top, and one bag slips down the slope. | The same again. The soil is still there. |

`[check]` These are made-up animation results for comparing these three model strips. They are not observations from a real tray experiment, and other materials or conditions can behave differently. A science reviewer must check this illustration before build.

**Record prompt, after each pour:** What did you see on each strip?
**Stems:** I saw the water… · I saw the soil… · I saw the bags… · I saw the grass…

**Last question:** Which strip would you use where?
- On a slope above the homes lane, where water must not come through: · bare soil · sandbags · grass and roots
- On the meadow bank, where water can come over, but the soil should stay: · bare soil · sandbags · grass and roots
- By the bridge, for a few days while a flood passes: · bare soil · sandbags · grass and roots

**After answering:**
> Sandbags hold water back for a while. In these authored animations, the planted strip lets water over and loses less soil. Roots can reduce erosion, but they do not guarantee that a bank will stay put. Plants help against wind wearing soil away, too. Each one does a different job, so the best choice depends on the place. `[check]`

## b4. The low fields (Act IV)

**Card:** At the town meeting, there are three plans for the low fields. Here they are on the model. Test each one with all three floods. Then make the design you would choose.

**Plan cards:**

| Plan | Builds | Speaker's line |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Wall | Sandbags on the meadow, the top of the orchard, the mill path and the homes lane. A wall and sandbags at the low corner. | Bramble: "The orchard families have looked after those trees for a hundred years. A wall keeps every tree and every field. Why should they give up land?" |
| The Sponge | Room for the brook at the meadow and the low corner. Sandbags on the mill path and the homes lane. | Moss: "Let the low ground be wet again, like the old map. It's what it wants to be. The reeds from Vadoalto would love it." |
| Some of Each | Room for the brook at the low corner only. Sandbags on the meadow, the mill path and the homes lane. | The miller: "Give the brook a little room where it's already wet, and build where we must. Neither side gets everything." |

**Load buttons:** Try the Wall · Try the Sponge · Try Some of Each · Start from nothing

**Comparison table (fills in as the child tests):**
- Heading: What each plan did
- Columns: Plan · Coins · Land given · Spring flood · Big flood · Great flood
- Cells: wet sections and fictional downstream-impact score for Les Saules, as tested

**After all three plans are tested in all three floods:**
> Every plan kept the homes, the bridge road and the mill path dry in the big flood. They did it in different ways, with different costs. In the great flood, they were different again. No plan was best at everything.

**What if? (older, after the comparison):**
> Try the What if? flood with any design. Can any design keep the homes, the bridge road and the mill path all dry?
- After testing: Not with 8 coins. A big enough flood gets through somewhere. Planning makes floods less harmful. It can't make them go away. `[check]`

**Make your own:** You can change any plan, or start from nothing. When you've tested it, keep it with your reasons.

## Grown-up notes

- The model is deliberately simple: each section either holds or lets water through, room for the brook lowers the water downstream, and raised banks receive a unitless downstream-impact score. Real floods depend on volume, speed, ground, timing and much more.
- The three plans match the town meeting in Act IV. The model shows that each has real costs and real benefits; it never picks a winner, and nor should anyone at the meeting.
- The claim stems ask for evidence from tests the child ran, and a worry. The writing is for a grown-up to read and talk about, never graded.
