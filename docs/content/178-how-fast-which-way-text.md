# How Fast, Which Way: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 178](../specs/178-how-fast-which-way.md). Use as written, after independent spec review and a physics subject check. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion and is never gendered; `{courier}` is the explorer's courier. Pip is he/him, Dot she/her, Bramble she/her (with her magpie), Léon he/him, Hob Pennick he/him, the librarian they/them. Real-world facts are in the [source audit](../source-audits/178-how-fast-which-way.md); items still marked `[check]` stay off screen until cleared. Nothing here is scored or timed.

## Labels used on every card

- **A story from Willowbank**: the town, its couriers, boats, letters and every character are made up.
- **A true thing about science**: checked science, explained simply.
- **A true thing from history**: something people really did or wrote.
- **A made-up model**: a simple game rule, shown on the card, that is not real measurement.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. The line on the wall (Act III, chapter 3: the route by the high ford)

**Hook:** {courier} carries the first letter to Vadoalto. Dot chalks the trip on the wall as a line.

**Short:**
> {courier} walks to Vadoalto. Dot chalks a line: time across, distance up. "It climbs because the path climbs," says Bramble. The line disagrees.

**Longer:**
> {courier} sets off for Vadoalto with the first letter. Dot wants to know how the trip went, so she draws it on the post office wall. Hours go along the bottom. Distance from Willowbank goes up the side.
>
> "First hour, six kilometers," says {courier}. "Then an hour at the high ford. Hob made tea. Then the steep path, three hours for the last six."
>
> Dot's chalk goes up steeply, then flat, then up gently.
>
> Bramble squints. "That gentle bit is wrong. The path there is the steepest. The line should climb like a hill."
>
> "The line isn't the path," says {guide}. "It shows how far, by when."
>
> Pip points at the flat bit. "That's the tea."
>
> "So the gentle part means slow," says Bramble slowly. "Because the path was steep." She frowns at the wall. "Fine. The line wins."

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> Twelve kilometers in five hours is 2.4 kilometers each hour on average. That counts the hour of tea. Leave the tea out and you get 3: the walking speed, not the whole trip's. Real couriers vary; this trip is made up.

### Scene 2. Pip is not moving (Act III, chapter 1: the floating market)

**Hook:** Vadoalto's traders float down to market fast. Going home upstream takes twice as long. Pip rides along.

**Short:**
> Pip sits on the traders' barge. "I'm not moving," he says. The bank slides past. "Compared with what?" asks {guide}.

**Longer:**
> The traders from Vadoalto row down Willow Brook with apples, reeds and a crate of cheese. They are early. "The brook helps us down," says the oldest trader. "Going home, it pushes back."
>
> Pip climbs aboard to guard the cheese. He sits very still.
>
> "You're moving," says Dot, from the bank.
>
> "I'm not," says Pip. "I'm sitting. The cheese isn't moving either."
>
> "You're both right," says {guide}. "Compared with the barge, you're still. Compared with the bank, you're going downstream."
>
> "Which one is true?"
>
> "Both. You just have to say compared with what."
>
> Pip thinks about this. Then he walks to the front of the barge, to be nearer the market and, by chance, the cheese.

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> The traders row 6 km/h through the water. The brook flows at 2. Downstream they make 8 km/h past the bank; upstream, only 4. Twelve kilometers take 1.5 hours down and 3 hours back.

### Scene 3. A letter from the ferry (Act III, chapter 4: letters up and down the river)

**Hook:** Léon writes from his ferry at Les Saules. He has a puzzle about a rope and a barge.

**Short:**
> Léon writes: "Bonjour des Saules! (Hello from Les Saules!) A barge glides on when its pulls are even. Why doesn't it stop?"

**Longer** (French line for a fluent reader's check):
> *Bonjour des Saules !* (Hello from Les Saules!)
>
> Dear friends in Willowbank,
>
> From my ferry deck, your bank slides past and I stand still. My cousin on the bank says the opposite. We argue every morning.
>
> Here is a better puzzle. A barge is towed up our river by a rope. The rope pulls forward. The water pushes back just as hard. My cousin says the barge must stop. But it keeps gliding, the same speed, all morning.
>
> Then the rope goes slack. The barge is still going upstream. Which way is it changing?
>
> Write back. I am always slightly damp.
>
> Léon

## Teach cards

Each card is under 30 words. Worked steps appear one tap at a time.

**T1. Words for motion** (A true thing about science)
> Distance is how far. Speed is how far in each hour, or each minute. Direction is which way.
Worked example: 1. The cart rolls 30 cm each second. 2. That is its speed: 30 cm per second.

**T2. Fast is not speeding up** (A true thing about science)
> Speeding up means speed is changing. A fast cart at the same speed is not speeding up. Steady means the same distance in each equal time.
Worked example: 1. Ferry: 30 m, 30 m, 30 m each minute. 2. Same each time. 3. Steady, not speeding up.

**T3. Test a design** (A true thing about science)
> A push or a ramp starts the cart. Set a goal you can see. Predict. Try. Mark where it stopped. Change one thing.
Worked example: 1. Goal: the basket. 2. It stopped short. 3. Change one thing: one more block.

**T4. Patterns help you predict** (A true thing about science)
> Roll each height three times. Look for the pattern. Use it to predict a height you haven't tried. Say "about".
Worked example: 1. One block: about 30 cm. 2. Two blocks: about 60 cm. 3. Each block adds about 30 cm.

**T5. Read the edges first** (A true thing about science)
> A distance-time graph has time along the bottom. Distance from the start goes up the side. Read both before the line.
Worked example: 1. Bottom: hours. 2. Side: kilometers from Willowbank. 3. A point at (1, 6): one hour, six kilometers.

**T6. Flat, steep, down** (A true thing about science)
> Flat means stopped. Steeper means faster. Sloping down means coming back. The line is a record, not a picture of hills.
Worked example: 1. Hour 1 to 2: flat. 2. Distance didn't change. 3. The courier stopped.

**T7. How fast, in numbers** (A true thing about science)
> 6 km/h means 6 km in each hour. Speed = distance ÷ time.
Worked example (double number line): 1. 6 km in 1 hour. 2. After the ford: 6 km in 3 hours. 3. 6 ÷ 3 = 2 km/h.

**T8. Average speed counts the stops** (A true thing about science)
> Average speed = total distance ÷ total time. Stops count as time.
Worked example: 1. Total distance: 12 km. 2. Total time: 5 hours, tea included. 3. 12 ÷ 5 = 2.4 km/h.

**T9. Compared with what?** (A true thing about science)
> Every motion is measured compared with something. Pip is still compared with the barge. He moves compared with the bank. Both are true.
Worked example: 1. The barge drifts at 2 km/h. Pick the viewer: the bank. 2. Pip goes downstream at 2 km/h. 3. Now pick the barge: Pip is still.

**T10. Adding and taking away speeds** (A true thing about science)
> Walking with the barge: add. Walking against it: take away. Two boats side by side, same speed: still to each other.
Worked example: 1. Barge: 5 km/h. 2. Pip walks forward: 3 km/h. 3. Compared with the bank: 5 + 3 = 8 km/h.

**T11. Add the arrows into one** (A true thing about science)
> Same way: add. Opposite ways: take the smaller from the bigger. The one arrow left, the resultant, replaces them all.
Worked example: 1. 50 N and 30 N forward: 80 N. 2. 80 N forward, 20 N back: 60 N forward.

**T12. What the resultant does** (A true thing about science)
> Zero resultant: no change. Still stays still; moving keeps a steady speed. A resultant points the way the motion changes.
Worked example: 1. Rope 250 N forward, water 250 N back. 2. Resultant: zero. 3. The barge glides on, steady.

## Real-world and history cards

**H1. Galileo's ship** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> In a book printed in 1632, Galileo imagined a closed cabin on a smoothly moving ship. Flies, fish, drips: nothing would show the ship was moving.
Keeper's note:
> In the *Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems*, a character called Salviati describes the cabin. Salviati asks readers to picture it. The book does not report anyone trying it on a real ship. The idea is the Ferry Deck's: inside the cabin, you can only say "still compared with the cabin".

**H2. Knots on a rope** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> Sailors threw a wooden board off the back of a ship on a knotted rope. They counted knots running out while a small sandglass emptied.
Keeper's note:
> The count gave speed in knots: nautical miles per hour. The oldest known printed description is in an English book from 1574. Later accounts give knots about 14.4 m apart and a 28-second glass. Today one knot is exactly 1.852 km/h. The rope measured speed through the water, not past the land.

**H3. Ferries pushed by the river** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> In Basel, Switzerland, four small ferries cross the Rhine with no motor. Each hangs from a cable. The ferry keeper angles the boat, and the current pushes it across.
Keeper's note:
> The cable pulls one way and the water pushes another. Together they act like one push across the river. Here the two arrows are at an angle, so we draw the resultant instead of adding numbers.

## Bench (a): The Letter-Cart Ramp

**Model card** (A made-up model):
> Each block adds about 30 cm of roll. Real carts lose a little on the ramp.

**Buttons:** Add a block · Take a block · Turning board · Predict · Roll · Try again
**After a roll:** Look at the marker. Did it reach the basket?

**Guided item:** The cart stopped before the basket.
- Step 1: Was it short, or too far? *(Short.)*
- Step 2: What one thing could go farther? *(One more block.)*
- Step 3: Roll again and look at the marker.

**Pattern mode opening:** Roll each height three times. Then predict five blocks.
**Table headers:** Blocks · Try 1 · Try 2 · Try 3
**Prediction prompt:** Place your band where five blocks will stop.
**After the reveal:** Your band was made from your pattern. That's a prediction, not a guess.

## Bench (b): The Word Wall

**Opening:** Each word names a kind of motion. Sort the pictures.
**Sort labels:** Shows it · Doesn't

| --- | --- |

| speed | How far it goes in each hour, minute or second. |
| direction | Which way it goes. |
| stationary | Not moving, compared with what you're looking from. |

**Guided item:** The ferry crosses fast, the same speed all the way. Is it speeding up?
- Step 1: Is its speed changing? *(No.)*

## Bench (c): The Trip Line

**Guided item:** How fast was the courier's first hour?
- Step 1: Find the first part of the line.
- Step 2: How far? *(6 km.)*
- Step 3: How long? *(1 hour.)*
- Step 4: 6 km in 1 hour is 6 km/h.

## Bench (d): The Ferry Deck

**Model card:** A simplified picture: one straight river, steady speeds, no wind.
**Viewpoint buttons:** Watch from the bank · Watch from the barge · Watch from the other boat
**Speed tag pattern:** "[thing]: [number] km/h, compared with you."

**Guided item:** Pip sits on a barge drifting at 2 km/h. Is he moving?
- Step 1: Watch from the bank. *(Pip goes by at 2 km/h.)*
- Step 2: Watch from the barge. *(Pip stays put.)*
- Step 3: Both are true. Say compared with what.

## Bench (e): The Tow Line

**Model card:** A simplified picture: forces along one line, arrow lengths to scale.
**Buttons:** Add an arrow · Combine · Predict: speeds up / keeps steady / slows down · Show the change

**Guided item:** The rope pulls 300 N forward. The water pushes 200 N back.
- Step 1: Opposite ways, so take away: 300 − 200.
- Step 2: 100 N, pointing forward.
- Step 3: One forward arrow replaces both. The barge speeds up.

### Set C. How fast, compared with what? (Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**C1 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: The traders' barge goes 8 km each hour. How far in 1.5 hours?
Answer: 12 km.
Worked steps (double number line): 1. 1 hour: 8 km. 2. Half an hour: 4 km. 3. 8 + 4 = 12 km.
Tests: 9.5 (adding 8 and 1.5); 8 (ignoring the half hour).
Hint: How far in half an hour?
Explanation: Distance = speed × time: 8 × 1.5 = 12.

**C2 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: Whole courier trip: 12 km in 5 hours, tea included. Average speed?
Answer: 2.4 km/h.
Worked steps: 1. Total distance: 12 km. 2. Total time: 1 + 1 + 3 = 5 hours. 3. 12 ÷ 5 = 2.4 km/h.
Tests: 3 (leaving out the hour of tea: 12 ÷ 4); 4 (the mean of 6 and 2).
Hint: Total distance ÷ total time. Does tea count?
Explanation: Stops are part of the trip's time, so they count.

**C3 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Pip sits on a barge drifting at 2 km/h. Is Pip moving?
- A. No. He is sitting still, so he isn't moving at all. *(tests: motion belongs to the object)*
- B. Compared with the bank, yes. Compared with the barge, no. *(right)*
- C. Only the bank's view is real. The barge view is a trick. *(tests: the ground is the true frame)*
Hint: Ask: compared with what?
Explanation: Both views are true. Each names what it compares with.
Feedback A: Still compared with the barge. What about the bank?
Feedback B: Yes. Name the viewer every time.
Feedback C: Neither view is a trick. Each compares with something different.

**C4 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: The barge goes 5 km/h. Pip walks forward at 3 km/h. Compared with the bank?
Answer: 8 km/h.
Worked steps: 1. Pip moves the same way as the barge. 2. Same way on top: add. 3. 5 + 3 = 8 km/h.
Tests: 2 (taking away when walking with the barge); 3 (forgetting the barge moves).
Hint: Is Pip walking with the barge or against it?
Explanation: Walking toward the front adds his speed to the barge's.

**C5 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: Traders row 6 km/h; the brook flows 2 km/h. Twelve km upstream takes how long?
Answer: 3 hours.
Worked steps: 1. Upstream, the brook pushes back: 6 − 2 = 4 km/h. 2. 12 ÷ 4 = 3 hours.
Tests: 1.5 hours (using the downstream speed, 8); 2 hours (ignoring the current, 12 ÷ 6).
Hint: Against the current: add or take away?
Explanation: Compared with the bank, they only make 4 km/h going home.

**C6 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: Down 12 km in 1.5 hours, back in 3. Round-trip average speed?
Answer: about 5.3 km/h (24 ÷ 4.5).
Worked steps: 1. Total distance: 12 + 12 = 24 km. 2. Total time: 1.5 + 3 = 4.5 hours. 3. 24 ÷ 4.5 ≈ 5.3 km/h.
Tests: 6 (the mean of 8 and 4); 8 (the downstream speed only).
Hint: Total distance ÷ total time.
Explanation: They spend longer at the slow speed, so the average is under 6.

### Set D. Adding forces, and how we know (Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**D1 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: The rope pulls 300 N forward. The water pushes 200 N back. Resultant?
Answer: 100 N forward.
Worked steps: 1. Opposite ways: take away. 2. 300 − 200 = 100 N. 3. It points the way of the bigger force: forward.
Tests: 500 N (adding opposite forces).
Hint: Opposite arrows cancel. How much is left?
Explanation: One 100 N forward arrow replaces both. The barge speeds up.

**D2 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Moving barge: rope 250 N forward, water 250 N back. What happens?
- A. It keeps going at a steady speed. *(right)*
- B. It stops, because nothing is left over. *(tests: zero resultant means stopped)*
- C. It slows down bit by bit. *(tests: motion needs a force to keep going)*
Hint: Zero resultant means no change.
Explanation: No change means a moving barge keeps its speed.
Feedback A: Yes. Zero resultant, steady speed.
Feedback B: Zero means no change. It was already moving.
Feedback C: Slowing is a change. A change needs a resultant.

**D3 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: Pip pulls 40 N, Bramble 60 N forward. Friction pushes 30 N back. Resultant?
Answer: 70 N forward.
Worked steps: 1. Same way: 40 + 60 = 100 N forward. 2. Opposite: 100 − 30 = 70 N. 3. It points forward.
Tests: 130 N (adding friction too); 100 N (leaving friction out).
Hint: Add the forward pulls first. Then take away the back push.
Explanation: Three arrows, one resultant: 70 N forward.

**D4 (Keeper's Desk, say why; spoken to a grown-up, not stored)**
Prompt: The rope goes slack. The barge still moves upstream. What happens next, and why?
Model answer for the grown-up: The water still pushes back, and nothing pulls forward. So the resultant points downstream. The barge keeps moving upstream but slows down.
Tests: the resultant must point the way it is going; no pull means no forces.
Hint: Which forces are left? Which way do they point?
Explanation: A resultant points the way the motion changes, not always the way it goes.

**D5 (Keeper's Desk, true / not true from this source)**
Source: card H1, Galileo's ship.
Prompt: Tap each sentence: true from this card, or not from this card.
- "Galileo wrote about a cabin on a moving ship." *(true from this card)*
- "In the closed cabin, nothing shows the smooth ship is moving." *(true from this card)*
- "Galileo says he tested this on a real ship." *(not from this card: it is a thought experiment)*
- "The cabin proves the ship is really still." *(not from this card: tests the true-frame idea)*
Hint: Read the card again. What does it say Galileo did?
Explanation: Imagining a test carefully can teach a lot. It is still different from doing it.

**D6 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: Six knots ran out: 6 knots. One knot is 1.852 km/h. About how fast?
Answer: about 11 km/h (6 × 1.852 = 11.112).
Worked steps: 1. Each knot counted is 1.852 km/h. 2. 6 × 1.852 = 11.112. 3. About 11 km/h.
Tests: 7.852 (adding instead of multiplying); 6 (forgetting to change units).
Hint: Six lots of 1.852.
Explanation: That's speed through the water. A current could make the ship faster or slower past the land.

## "I'm stuck" worked examples

Step 2 of the ladder: an easier case, then how to start this one.

- **Ramp items:** "Too short? Pip's ball stopped short, so he rolled it from higher up. Start: was yours short or long?"
- **Word items:** "A dropped apple speeds up. A rolling ball on grass slows down. Start: is your speed growing, shrinking or staying?"
- **Pattern items:** "Two cups hold 2 apples, four cups hold 4. Each cup adds 1. Start: what does each block add?"
- **Graph items:** "Pip stands by the bakery for ten minutes. His line is flat. Start: is your line flat, steep or sloping down?"
- **Speed items:** "Dot flies 4 m in 2 seconds: 4 ÷ 2 = 2 m each second. Start: how far, how long?"
- **Average items:** "Pip walks 2 km in 1 hour, rests 1 hour: 2 ÷ 2 = 1 km/h. Start: add up all the time."
- **Relative items:** "Two friends walk side by side. To each other, they're still. Start: is it with or against?"
- **Force items:** "Two friends pull the same way, 10 N each: 20 N. Start: same way or opposite?"

## Key terms (glossary)

- **distance**: how far something goes.
- **speed**: how far it goes in each hour, minute or second.
- **km/h**: kilometers in each hour.
- **direction**: which way it goes.
- **stationary**: not moving, compared with where you are looking from.
- **steady speed**: the same distance in each equal time.
- **speeding up (accelerating)**: the speed is growing.
- **slowing down (decelerating)**: the speed is shrinking.
- **distance-time graph**: a line showing how far, by when.
- **average speed**: total distance ÷ total time, stops included.
- **compared with what?**: the question every motion needs.
- **newton (N)**: a unit for measuring force.
- **resultant**: the one force that does the job of all the others.
- **knot**: a ship's speed, one nautical mile each hour.
- **pattern**: a change that repeats, which helps you predict.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** motion words with non-examples; testing a design against a goal; predicting from a repeated-measure pattern; reading and making distance-time graphs; speed and average speed (total distance ÷ total time); relative motion ("compared with what?") with adding and subtracting speeds; combining forces along one line into a resultant.

**What it does not claim:** these are made-up journeys and models, not real travel times or real carts. Forces at an angle are only drawn, not calculated. It does not teach velocity, acceleration as a number, or Newton's laws by name. A screen ramp rehearses the reasoning; real ramps teach measuring.
