## Story scenes (A story from Willowbank)

### Act I, *mend the village*: The broken vane

**Hook:** The storm snapped the station's wind vane. Pip has mended it. He's very sure how it works.

**Short:**
> The miller and Hob find the vane in the nettles. Pip fixes it. "The arrow points where the wind goes," he says. The leaves disagree.

**Longer:**
> The morning after the storm, the miller and Hob Pennick walk the mill fence. In the nettles they find the station's wind vane, snapped clean off its pole.
>
> "The old Keeper would want that back up," says the miller. Her cat sits on the flood post and supervises.
>
> Pip takes charge. He pushes the vane back onto its pin and gives it a spin. It wobbles, then settles, arrow pointing at the hills.
>
> "There," says Pip. "Wind's going to the hills."
>
> Hob licks a finger and holds it up. A leaf from the orchard tumbles past them, away from the hills, toward the brook.
>
> "Then why are the leaves going the other way?" asks Hob.
>
> Pip looks at the leaf. He looks at the arrow. "Maybe the leaves are wrong."
>
> "Let's ask the station," says {guide}.

**Keeper's note:**
> A vane's tail is bigger than its arrow. Wind pushes hardest on the big tail and swings it away, so the arrow ends up pointing into the wind. That's why winds are named for where they come from: a west wind blows from the west.

### Act II, *a garden across the seasons*, and the Act II ending: Two records side by side

**Hook:** Page 2 is dry. It's the old Keeper's flood ledger. The librarian thinks it has a partner.

**Short:**
> The librarian lays the flood ledger beside the old Keeper's rain book. Big floods, and the rain before them. "We should keep reading," says Dot.

**Longer:**
> Page 2 comes free on the bakery windowsill: the old Keeper's flood ledger, a column of years and how high the brook rose at the mill's flood post.
>
> The librarian frowns at it, then goes to the station and comes back with a damp notebook from inside the white box. *Rain, Willowbank, by the year.*
>
> They lay the two side by side on the café table. A rain total for every year. A flood height for only some of them.
>
> "So it doesn't flood every wet year," says Dot.
>
> "And some floods came in years that weren't very wet at all," says the librarian. "A whole year's rain can hide a few very wet days."
>
> Pip has found something else in the notebook: *Read the gauge every morning. Empty it after. Never before.*
>
> "Then we'll read it every morning," says Dot. "So next time, we'll know."

**Keeper's note:**
> A flood depends on how much rain falls and how fast, how wet the ground already is, and where the rain lands. That's why a yearly total and a flood height don't always match. The two books together are a better record than either alone.

### Act III, *letters up and down the river*: Rain up there

**Hook:** Nuria's letter says it rained in Vadoalto all night. In Willowbank it barely drizzled. So why is the flood post rising?

**Short:**
> Rain all night in Vadoalto, writes Nuria. Hardly a drop here, yet the brook rises by afternoon. The old Keeper wrote: *Rain up there is the brook down here, later.*

**Longer:**
> The courier brings Nuria's letter at breakfast. *Llovió toda la noche.* "It rained all night," she writes. "Our flood post went up a hand's width."
>
> Willowbank's rain gauge holds two millimeters. Hardly anything.
>
> But by afternoon the miller calls from the mill: the brook is up on the flood post, brown and quick.
>
> "It didn't rain here," says Pip. "Where did the water come from?"
>
> {guide} opens the rain book. On the inside cover, in a spiky hand: *Rain up there is the brook down here, later.*
>
> "The rain fell on the hills upstream," says the miller. "It has to run down the slopes and along the brook before it gets to us. That takes time."
>
> "How long?" asks Pip.
>
> "Depends," says the miller. "On the ground, and how wet it already was."

**Keeper's note:**
> Rain that falls upstream reaches a river further down later, after it has run over and through the ground. The wait depends on the slope, the soil, the plants and how wet the ground already is. This story doesn't give a number of hours, because Willowbank's brook is made up.

### Act IV, *the old map and Hollis's account*: Two averages

**Hook:** Bramble has worked out two averages from Hollis's rain book. One is bigger. Is that enough?

**Short:**
> Bramble: "First ten years, 884 millimeters a year. Last ten, 912. It's getting wetter." Wren asks how much the years jump around.

**Longer:**
> At the Keeper's Desk, Bramble has Hollis's rain book open and a page of sums. Her magpie is sitting on the pencil.
>
> "The first ten years averaged 884 millimeters," she says. "The last ten averaged 912. It's getting wetter. That's the floods explained."
>
> Wren Halloway doesn't say she's wrong. She turns the book round. "How much does one year differ from the next?"
>
> Bramble looks. Year 12: 1,265. Year 15: 640. Year 27: 694. Year 28: 1,071.
>
> "Hundreds," she says, slowly. "Sometimes four hundred."
>
> "And your two averages are 28 apart," says Wren. "Could that happen by chance, in a valley where the years jump around this much?"
>
> Bramble chews the end of her ribbon. "Maybe. So I need more?"
>
> "More years, more places, or both," says Wren. "Your sums are right. The question is what they show."

**Keeper's note:**
> Comparing two averages is a good start. But when single years swing by hundreds of millimeters, a difference of 28 between two ten-year averages could come from chance alone. Scientists look for trends in long records from many stations, with statistics that measure how big the year-to-year swings are.

## Bench (a): Read the instruments

### Teach cards (True science)

**Thermometer**
> A thermometer measures how warm the air is. We use degrees Celsius, written °C. In the US, people often use °F.

**Thermometer, Sprout version**
> When it gets warmer, the line goes up. When it gets cooler, it goes down.

**The white box**
> The white box keeps sun and rain off the thermometer. Air flows through the slats. Now it measures the air.

**Rain gauge**
> A rain gauge catches rain. We measure how deep it is, in millimeters (mm). Read it, then empty it.

**Wind vane**
> The arrow points into the wind. So it points to where the wind comes from.

**Anemometer**
> The cups spin faster when the wind blows faster. Count the turns to compare.

**Barometer**
> A barometer measures air pressure. Falling pressure often comes before cloud and rain. It isn't a promise.

**Units card**
> 15 mm of rain and 15 °C are different things. Always read the unit.

### Worked example: reading between the marks

Revealed one tap at a time.
1. "The marks go up in 2s: 6, 8, 10."
2. "The top of the liquid is halfway between 8 and 10."
3. "Halfway between 8 and 10 is 9."
4. "So it's 9 °C."

**Eye-level tip:** Bring your eye level with the top of the liquid. Looking from above or below reads it wrong.

### Worked example: inches and millimeters (Sapling)

1. "1 inch is exactly 25.4 mm."
2. "So 10 inches is 10 × 25.4 = 254 mm."
3. "To go back, divide by 25.4."

### The placement bench

**Card:** Notes say where each instrument should stand. Try a spot and see.

**Gauge rule card (Sapling):** Keep the gauge far from trees and walls. A common rule: at least twice as far as the thing is tall.

## Bench (b): Water cycle loop

### Teach card (True science)

> Evaporation: liquid water becomes invisible water vapor. Condensation: vapor becomes tiny drops. Opposite arrows, one loop.

### Worked example: the puddle by the mill door

1. "Morning: a puddle by the mill door."
2. "The sun warms it. Water evaporates into the air as vapor. You can't see vapor."
3. "High up, the air is cooler. Vapor condenses into tiny drops: a cloud."
4. "Drops join and fall as rain. Some lands in the brook. Round it goes again."

### Step cards (drag onto the ring)

1. The sun warms the brook.
2. Some water evaporates into invisible vapor.
3. The air rises and cools.
4. Vapor condenses into tiny drops: clouds.
5. Drops join and fall as rain or snow.
6. Rain collects in the brook and the ground.

**Ring hint:** The ring has no start. Put the cards in order, going round.

**Ring complete:** That's the loop. No water was used up. It just changed form and place.

**Follow one drop:** Watch this drop go round. Faint means it's vapor. It's still there.

### Clouds card (True science)

> Clouds are tiny drops of water, or ice up high. Three kinds: cumulus (heaped), stratus (a gray layer), cirrus (thin wisps).

**Cloud Details (older):** Luke Howard gave clouds these Latin names in 1802 and 1803. Cumulus means heap. Stratus means spread out. Cirrus means a curl of hair.

### Roof or field?

**Card:** Rain falls on the mill roof and on the meadow. Where does each go next?

- **Roof:** It runs into the gutter, down the pipe and quickly to the brook.
- **Meadow:** Some soaks in. Some runs off, more slowly. Some evaporates later. Plants drink some.
- **Link:** Try this with trays at Rain Paths.

**Always shown:** A simplified picture. Real water takes many more paths.

## Bench (c): Weather or climate?

### Teach card (True science)

> Weather is what the air does on one day. Climate is the usual pattern over many years. Scientists often use 30.

### Worked example

1. Card: "It rained nearly every day in April."
2. "Is this about one time, or many years?"
3. "One month. That's weather, even if it's a lot of weather."
4. "Climate would need many Aprils."

### Contrast card: a cold day and a cold place

> By the sea, winters are often mild. One frosty morning there is weather. A place where winters are usually freezing has a cold climate.

## Bench (d): The long record (Made-up numbers)

**Heading:** The old rain book: 30 years

**Always shown:** Made-up numbers for a made-up village. They aren't real data for any place.

### Teach card (True science)

> The mean is the level you get if you share everything out evenly. Add them all, then divide by how many.

### Worked example: level the bars

1. "Three years: 800, 1,000 and 900 mm."
2. "Move 100 from the 1,000 bar onto the 800 bar."
3. "Now all three are 900. That's the mean."
4. "Check: 800 + 1,000 + 900 = 2,700. 2,700 ÷ 3 = 900."

**After leveling:** The mean doesn't have to be one of the numbers. It's the level they share.

**Median and range card:**
> Sort the numbers. The median is the middle one. The range is the biggest take away the smallest.

**Climate-change Keeper's note** (True science; older readers, bench (d) only):
> Scientists combining records from thousands of stations, ships and satellites found Earth's average surface was about 1.1 °C warmer in 2011 to 2020 than in 1850 to 1900. The IPCC says human activity has caused this warming. One village's rain book can't show a world trend by itself. And nothing in Willowbank's story says the storm was caused by climate change.

## Key terms

| Term | Child-friendly definition |
| --- | --- |
| temperature | How warm or cold something is. |
| thermometer | A tool that measures temperature. |
| degrees Celsius (°C) | The unit for temperature used in science and most countries. |
| degrees Fahrenheit (°F) | Another unit for temperature, used in the US. |
| rain gauge | A tube that catches rain so you can measure how deep it is. |
| millimeter (mm) | A tiny length. Ten of them make a centimeter. |
| wind vane | A pointer that shows where the wind comes from. |
| anemometer | Spinning cups that show how fast the wind blows. |
| barometer | A tool that measures air pressure. |
| air pressure | How hard the air presses down. Low pressure often brings clouds. |
| Stevenson screen | A white box with slatted sides that shades a thermometer. |
| evaporation | When liquid water turns into invisible water vapor. |
| water vapor | Water as a gas. You can't see it. |
| condensation | When water vapor turns back into tiny drops. |
| precipitation | Water falling from clouds: rain, snow, sleet or hail. |
| collection | Water gathering in rivers, lakes and the ground. |
| weather | What the air is doing at one time. |
| climate | The usual weather in a place over many years. |
| mean | The level you get if you share everything out evenly. |
| median | The middle number when you sort them. |
| range | The biggest number take away the smallest. |
| average | A number that describes what's typical. The mean is one kind. |

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** how weather instruments work and why they stand where they do; the units (°C with °F for US readers, mm of rain, hPa); the water cycle as a loop with no water lost; the difference between weather and climate; and the mean, median and range on a made-up 30-year record, including why one difference between two averages doesn't prove a trend.

**What it does not claim:** The rain record, every placement reading and every week of rain are made up for a made-up village. The bench makes no forecast and gives no safety advice. The brook's rise after upstream rain is described in words only, without hours. The climate-change note reports the IPCC's 2021 findings in brief; the game teaches neither the greenhouse effect nor its causes, and nothing links the fictional storm to climate change.

**Sources in brief:** WMO and NOAA NCEI on 30-year climate normals; NASA Climate Kids on weather and climate; the UK Met Office on measuring weather, thermometer screens and pressure; USGS Water Science School on condensation and the water cycle; NASA Glenn on how a wind vane turns; the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (2021) Summary for Policymakers. Full list in the source audit.

**Off-screen, adult-led:** Make a simple rain gauge from a straight-sided clear jar. Stand it on open ground, away from trees and walls, and measure the water's depth with a ruler each morning, then empty it. If you cut a plastic bottle to make a funnel, an adult does the cutting; cut edges can be sharp. Keep glass jars where they can't be knocked over. The game records nothing from this. To keep a real log, use Dot's Field Guide (spec 116), when it exists.

**Off-screen, anyone:** Watch leaves, smoke from a distant chimney, or washing on a line. Which way is the wind coming from? Check with a compass.
