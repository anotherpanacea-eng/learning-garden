## (a) The supper journey

### Teach

**Card 1:** Food travels through one long tube, from the mouth to the end. Each stop on the tube does a different job.

**Worked example, one tap per step: one bite of bread**
1. **Mouth.** Teeth cut and grind the bread. Spit, called saliva, wets it and starts breaking it down.
2. **Food pipe.** Muscles squeeze the soft bite down to the stomach. It doesn't just fall.
3. **Stomach.** A stretchy bag churns the bite with acid until it is like thick soup.
4. **Small intestine.** A long, coiled tube. Most of the useful parts pass through its walls into the blood.
5. **Large intestine.** It takes water back from what is left.
6. **Out.** What the body can't use leaves as waste, when a person uses the toilet.

**Card 2:** The small intestine is several meters long. It is folded up tight to fit inside.

**Fox note:** A fox's food goes on the same kind of journey, through a fox-sized tube.

### Guided item

**Prompt:** The bite has left the stomach. Where does it go next?
**Visible steps:**
1. The stomach churns food into thick soup.
2. Next, the useful parts must get into the blood.
3. Which stop takes food into the blood?

Choices: Large intestine · Small intestine (fits) · Food pipe

- Small intestine: Yes. This long tube takes most of the useful parts into the blood.
- Large intestine: That comes later. It takes back water from what is left.
- Food pipe: That stop came before the stomach. Look back along the tube.

### Practice: order the stops

**Tray prompt:** Put the stops in order, from first to last.
**Cards:** Mouth · Food pipe · Stomach · Small intestine · Large intestine · Out of the body as waste
**Place next button:** Place next
**Check button:** Check my order
**Per-marker feedback:** This stop fits here. / This stop belongs somewhere else on the tube. Try another card.
**When all fit:** Now tap the bread to follow it. Each stop shows its job.

## (b) Tooth match

### Teach

**Card 1:** A person has four shapes of teeth. Each shape suits a job.

**Worked example, one tap per step: incisor and molar**
1. Look at the front tooth. It has a thin, flat edge, like a little chisel.
2. A thin edge cuts. This is an **incisor**. It bites off a piece of apple.
3. Now a back tooth. It is wide, with bumps on top.
4. Wide and bumpy crushes and grinds. This is a **molar**.

**Card 2:** **Canines** are pointed, for gripping and tearing. **Premolars** sit between canines and molars. They crush and grind too.

**Card 3:** A person grows 20 baby teeth first. Later, adult teeth replace them, just once. A grown-up can have up to 32.

**Card 4:** The last four molars are wisdom teeth. Some grown-ups never get some or all of them.

### Guided item

**Prompt:** Match the canine to its job.
**Visible steps:**
1. Look at the canine's shape.
2. It is pointed at the tip.
3. A point can grip and tear.

Choices: Grip and tear (fits) · Cut and bite · Crush and grind
- Grip and tear: Yes. The point holds food while it tears.
- Cut and bite: That is the incisor's job. Its edge is thin and flat.
- Crush and grind: That needs a wide, bumpy top, like a molar.

### Practice: match all four

Incisor → Cut and bite · Canine → Grip and tear · Premolar → Crush and grind · Molar → Crush and grind

## (c) Pump and pipes

### Teach

**Card 1:** The heart is a muscle about the size of a fist. It sits in the middle of the chest.

**Card 2:** The heart squeezes again and again. It pumps blood through tubes, called blood vessels, all around the body.

**Card 3:** Blood carries oxygen and food to every part of the body. It carries waste gas away.

**Card 4:** The heart pumps blood. It doesn't make it. Blood cells are made in bone marrow, inside bones.

**Card 5, Diagram code:** Blood is always red. Bright red carries more oxygen. Dark red carries less. Some diagrams use blue as a code.

**Card 6 (Keeper's layer):** The heart has four rooms, called chambers. One side pumps blood to the lungs. The other side pumps to the body.

**Worked example, one tap per step: the red blood cell's first trip**
1. The cell leaves the heart, bright red, carrying oxygen.
2. It travels through blood vessels to the body.
3. It gives oxygen to the body's cells. It picks up waste gas.
4. Now darker red, it travels back to the heart.

### Guided item

**Prompt:** The cell is back at the heart. Where should it go to get oxygen?
**Visible steps:**
1. The cell gave away its oxygen in the body.
2. It needs fresh oxygen.
3. Oxygen comes in with air.
4. Where does air go?

Choices: The stomach · The brain · The lungs (fits)
- The lungs: Yes. In the lungs the blood picks up oxygen and drops waste gas.
- The stomach: Air doesn't go to the stomach. Breathed air goes to the lungs.
- The brain: The brain needs oxygen too, but blood gets oxygen in the lungs.

### Practice: follow the loop

**Step button:** Move the cell
**Stop captions:**
- Heart: The heart squeezes. Off you go.
- Lungs: Oxygen on. Waste gas off. The cell turns bright red.
- Body: Oxygen off to the cells. Waste gas on. The cell turns darker red.
**After one full loop:** Round and round, all day and all night.
**Keeper's layer prompt:** Follow the cell through both loops, in order.
**Keeper's layer captions:** Right side of the heart · Lungs loop · Left side of the heart · Body loop

### Breathing (teach cards on the same chart)

**Card 7:** Air goes in through the nose or mouth. It goes down the windpipe into two lungs.

**Card 8:** Lungs are spongy, full of tiny tubes and air pockets. They are not hollow balloons.

**Card 9:** Breathing in and breathing out are both air. Air breathed out has less oxygen and more waste gas.

**Card 10 (Sapling):** Under the lungs is a muscle called the diaphragm. It tightens and flattens, and air flows in.

**Card 11 (Keeper's):** Air breathed in is about 21% oxygen. Air breathed out still has about 16%.

**Card 12 (together):** Lungs load oxygen into the blood. The heart pumps it to the body. Cells use it and make waste gas.

**Card 13 (brain):** The brain sends messages along nerves. Some messages move muscles. Some keep breathing going without thinking.

**Fox note:** Foxes have hearts, lungs and blood vessels too. Theirs are fox-sized.

### Set A. The supper journey

**A1 (Sprout, order).** Put the bite's stops in order.
- Answer: Mouth, Food pipe, Stomach, Small intestine, Large intestine, Out.
- Tags: putting Out right after Stomach `straight-out`.
- Hint: Start where the bite goes in.
- Explanation: Food goes through one long tube. Each stop does one job before the next.

**A2 (Sprout, 3-choice).** Where does the bite go right after the mouth?
- Food pipe (fits): Yes. Muscles in the food pipe squeeze it down.
- Stomach `straight-out`: The stomach is next after that. Something carries the bite there.
- Windpipe `one-tube-air-food`: The windpipe carries air. Food goes down a different tube.
- Hint: Find the tube between the mouth and the stomach.
- Explanation: The food pipe joins the mouth to the stomach. The windpipe is a separate tube for air.

**A3 (Sprout, 3-choice).** What moves food down the food pipe?
- It just falls down `gravity-moves-food`: Food would still go down if a person lay flat. Something squeezes it.
- The stomach pulls it in `stomach-does-all`: The stomach waits at the bottom. The pipe's own walls do the work.
- Muscles squeeze it along (fits): Yes. Waves of muscle push the food down.
- Hint: Think of squeezing paste along a tube.
- Explanation: The food pipe's muscles squeeze in waves. That is why swallowing works lying down.

**A4 (Seedling, 3-choice).** Where do most nutrients pass into the blood?
- Stomach `stomach-does-all`: The stomach churns food into soup. Most of the taking-in happens later.
- Small intestine (fits): Yes. Its long walls let most useful parts into the blood.
- Large intestine `swap-intestines`: The large intestine mostly takes back water.
- Hint: Which stop is long and coiled?
- Explanation: The small intestine is long and its lining is folded, so lots of food can pass into the blood.

**A5 (Seedling, match).** Match each stop to its job.
- Answer: Mouth → cuts, grinds and wets food · Food pipe → squeezes food down · Stomach → churns food with acid · Small intestine → takes most nutrients into the blood · Large intestine → takes back water.
- Tags: stomach matched to "takes nutrients into the blood" `stomach-does-all`; large intestine matched to nutrients `swap-intestines`.
- Hint: Do the mouth first. What happens there?
- Explanation: Each stop has one main job. Together they break food down, take in what is useful and get rid of the rest.

**A6 (Seedling, true or not true).** "The stomach is where most food goes into the blood."
- Answer: Not true.
- Tag: choosing True `stomach-does-all`.
- Hint: What does the stomach do to food?
- Explanation: The stomach churns and mixes. Most food passes into the blood in the small intestine.

**A7 (Sapling, 3-choice).** Why is the small intestine coiled up?
- So a long tube fits inside (fits): Yes. It is several meters long, folded up tight.
- Because it is very short `short-gut`: It is one of the longest parts. Coiling helps it fit.
- So food can skip past it `straight-out`: Food goes all the way through it. That's where most is taken in.
- Hint: Think of a long garden hose put away in a box.
- Explanation: The small intestine is much longer than a person is tall. Coiling packs it in.

**A8 (Sapling, say why, read by a grown-up).** Say why a person chews bread before swallowing.
- Ideas to listen for: teeth break it into small pieces; saliva wets it and starts breaking it down; small, wet pieces are easier to swallow and digest.
- Hint: What do teeth and saliva each do?
- Explanation: Chewing starts digestion. Smaller pieces give the next stops less work.

### Set C. Body numbers (example rates, never anyone's own)

Every item here shows: **These are example numbers for a made-up grown-up. Real bodies vary.**

**C1 (Seedling, number).** An example grown-up heart beats 70 times a minute. How many beats in an hour?
- Answer: 4,200.
- Steps: 1. An hour is 60 minutes. 2. 70 beats each minute. 3. 70 × 60 = 4,200.
- Tag: answering 130 (adding 70 + 60) `add-not-multiply`.
- Hint: An hour has 60 minutes. Each one has 70 beats.
- Explanation: 70 beats, 60 times over, is 4,200. Resting grown-up hearts often beat 60 to 100 times a minute.

**C2 (Seedling, number).** A baby set has 20 teeth. A grown-up can have 32. How many more?
- Answer: 12.
- Steps: 1. Start at 20. 2. Count on to 32. 3. 32 − 20 = 12.
- Tag: answering 52 (adding) `add-not-subtract`.
- Hint: How many more is a take-away. Start with the bigger number.
- Explanation: 32 − 20 = 12. The extra teeth are 8 premolars, which baby sets don't have, and 4 more molars.

**C3 (Seedling, number).** Say a small intestine is 6 meters long. How many 1-meter rulers?
- Answer: 6.
- Steps: 1. Each ruler is 1 meter. 2. 6 meters ÷ 1 meter = 6 rulers.
- Hint: Lay rulers end to end. How many fit in 6 meters?
- Explanation: 6 rulers. Lengths vary, so 6 meters is a round example.

**C4 (Sapling, number).** 32 adult teeth are shared equally among 4 quarters of the mouth. How many each?
- Answer: 8.
- Steps: 1. 32 ÷ 4. 2. 4 × 8 = 32. 3. Check: 2 incisors + 1 canine + 2 premolars + 3 molars = 8.
- Tag: answering 128 (multiplying) `multiply-not-divide`.
- Hint: Share 32 into 4 equal groups.
- Explanation: Each quarter has 8 teeth: 2 + 1 + 2 + 3.

**C5 (Sapling, number).** A fox has about 42 teeth. A grown-up person can have 32. Find the difference.
- Answer: 10.
- Steps: 1. 42 − 32. 2. 42 − 30 = 12. 3. 12 − 2 = 10.
- Hint: Take away 30 first, then 2 more.
- Explanation: A fox has about 10 more teeth. It has more front teeth and premolars, and fewer molars.

**C6 (Sapling, number).** Small intestine 6 m, large intestine 1.5 m. How many times longer?
- Answer: 4.
- Steps: 1. How many 1.5s make 6? 2. 1.5 + 1.5 = 3. 3. 3 + 3 = 6, so four 1.5s. 4. 6 ÷ 1.5 = 4.
- Tag: answering 4.5 (subtracting) `difference-not-times`.
- Hint: Count how many 1.5 m pieces fit along 6 m.
- Explanation: About 4 times longer, using these round examples. "Small" means narrow here, not short.

**C7 (Keeper's Desk, number).** Say one heartbeat pushes out 70 mL, at 70 beats a minute. Liters per minute?
- Answer: 4.9 liters (4,900 mL).
- Steps: 1. 70 mL × 70 beats = 4,900 mL. 2. 1,000 mL = 1 liter. 3. 4,900 ÷ 1,000 = 4.9 liters.
- Tag: answering 140 (adding) `add-not-multiply`; answering 4,900 liters `unit-slip`.
- Hint: Multiply first. Then change mL into liters.
- Explanation: About 4.9 liters a minute. A grown-up has only about 5 liters of blood, so the same blood must go round and round. That was Harvey's kind of argument (set D).

### Set D. How blood was found to go round (Keeper's Desk)

**Source card 1, A true thing from history:**
> Galen was a Greek doctor who lived in the Roman Empire, about 1,800 years ago (129 to about 216 CE). He taught that blood was made in the liver from food, flowed out to the body, and was used up there. Doctors in many lands learned from his books for more than a thousand years.

**Source card 2, A true thing from history:**
> In 1628 an English doctor, William Harvey, published a short book in Latin, usually called *De Motu Cordis* (On the Motion of the Heart). He argued that the heart pumps the same blood around the body in a circle. He used experiments and counting. He showed that valves in veins only let blood flow toward the heart. He worked out that the heart pumps more blood in half an hour than a whole body holds, far too much to be made new from food.

**Source card 3, A true thing from history:**
> Harvey could not see how blood crossed from the smallest arteries to the smallest veins. In 1661 Marcello Malpighi saw tiny vessels, later called capillaries, in a frog's lung through a microscope. Centuries before Harvey, in the 1200s, the scholar Ibn al-Nafis had written that blood travels from one side of the heart to the other through the lungs. He studied in Damascus and worked in Cairo.

**D1 (3-choice).** What did Galen teach about blood?
- It goes round and round `present-back`: That was Harvey's idea, much later. Check card 1.
- The liver makes it; the body uses it up (fits): Yes. That is what card 1 says Galen taught.
- The heart makes new blood each beat `heart-makes-blood`: Card 1 names a different organ.
- Hint: Find the organ named in card 1.
- Explanation: Galen thought blood was made and used up. Harvey's evidence later changed that idea.

**D2 (true or not true from these cards).** Two statements.
- "Harvey published his book in 1628." Answer: True (card 2).
- "Harvey saw capillaries through a microscope." Answer: Not true from these cards. Card 3 says Malpighi saw them, in 1661. Tag `harvey-first-guess`.
- Hint: Look for who saw the tiny vessels, and when.
- Explanation: Harvey argued that blood must cross over. Malpighi later saw where: 33 years after the book.

**D3 (3-choice).** What evidence helped Harvey argue that blood goes round?
- Galen's old books had already said so `authority`: Galen taught something different. Harvey used his own tests.
- He watched it under a microscope `harvey-first-guess`: Card 3 says he couldn't see the smallest vessels.
- The heart pumps too much to be newly made (fits): Yes. He counted, and the amount was far too big.
- Hint: Card 2 mentions counting.
- Explanation: Counting and the valve experiments were his evidence. An amount too big to be made new must be going round.

**D4 (order).** Put these in time order, earliest first.
- Cards: Galen teaches in the Roman Empire · Ibn al-Nafis writes about blood through the lungs · Harvey's book is published · Malpighi sees capillaries.
- Answer: Galen (129 to about 216 CE) → Ibn al-Nafis (1200s) → Harvey (1628) → Malpighi (1661).
- Hint: Use the dates on the cards.
- Explanation: Ideas about blood changed over more than 1,400 years, as people tested and looked more closely.

**D5 (say why, read by a grown-up).** Why do you think Galen's idea lasted so long?
- Ideas to listen for: his books were respected and copied; it was hard to look inside a living body; nobody could see the tiny vessels; new tests and counting gave new evidence.
- Hint: What could people see and test then? What came later?
- Explanation: Old ideas can last when evidence is hard to get. Careful tests and new tools changed this one.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** where food goes in a person's body; tooth shapes and jobs, and how a fox's teeth differ; the heart pumping blood through vessels; breathing in and out as two mixtures of air; for older explorers, how breathing and circulation work together, how bodies are built from cells, and how evidence changed ideas about blood (William Harvey, 1628).

**What it does not claim:** the chart is a simplified picture, not a medical diagram; lengths and rates are round examples and real bodies vary. "Nutrients pass into the blood" is simplified (some digested fat travels first through the lymph system). This pack deliberately leaves out puberty, illness, medicines, vaccines and the immune system, tooth care, weight and calories. It never asks about the explorer's body, food or health, and it saves nothing about them.

**Food groups:** we use the NHS Eatwell Guide's five groups as one official guide, and present them without judging any food or meal. Other countries use other guides (for example USDA MyPlate in the United States).

**Off-screen card, optional: Feel a pulse (grown-up-led)**
> If you both like, a grown-up can show how to feel a pulse. Rest two fingers (not a thumb) gently on the inside of the wrist, below the thumb, or let a hand rest flat on the chest. Press lightly. Each little bump is the heart pushing blood along. Results vary from person to person and from minute to minute, so there is no right number, and no need to count or share one. It is a way to notice a heart at work, not a health check. Nothing is entered in the game. Skip it any time, and stop if it feels uncomfortable for anyone.
