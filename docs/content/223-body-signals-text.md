## Story scenes

### Scene 1. Nose first (Act I, chapter 1: find and feed)

**Short:** Pip finds the bakery with his nose. He finds the miller's cat with his ears. "My nose and ears do the finding," he says.

**Longer:** After the storm, the lanes were full of fallen branches. Pip could not see the bakery at all.

He lifted his nose. "Bread," he said. "That way."

A little bell jingled behind the mill. "And that's the miller's cat," said Pip, turning his big ears.

{guide} laughed. "A nose smells and ears hear. What else noticed the storm?"

"My fur felt the wind," said Pip. "My eyes saw the mess."

"Skin feels. Eyes see," said {guide}. "Let's find all five."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** Many animals have these senses too, but not all of them. An earthworm has no eyes or ears. Its skin can tell light from dark, and it feels touch.

### Scene 2. Name tags for the Traveler (Act II, chapter 4: the first show)

**Short:** Dot paints six bone tags for the show's program. The magpie flies off with one. Which bone is missing its name?

**Longer:** The librarian's jointed wooden puppet, the Traveler, stood on the workbench in the Story Loft. It was ready for the first show.

"The program should name its bones," said Dot, adjusting her hat. She painted six tags: skull, ribcage, spine, humerus, pelvis, femur.

"Femur sounds like a sneeze," said Pip.

"It's the thigh bone," said Dot. "Every tag gets a hard name and an easy name."

The magpie swooped, snatched a tag and landed on the rafters.

"Now which bone is nameless?" asked {guide}.

Dot tapped her hard red back. "Mine's on the outside, by the way."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about science):** A giraffe's neck has seven bones. So does a person's. The giraffe's are just much longer.

### Scene 3. The thistle on the high ford path (Act III, chapter 3: the route by the high ford)

**Short:** On the old path, the courier steps on a thistle. The foot jumps up first. "Ouch" comes after. How?

**Longer:** The courier followed the faded ribbons up toward the high ford. The path was narrow and the grass grew long.

A thistle hid in the grass. The courier's foot lifted off it in a flash.

A moment later came the "ouch".

"That foot moved before anyone said a word," said Pip.

"The message didn't wait for the brain," said {guide}. "It turned around in the spinal cord, like a shortcut."

The courier rubbed the boot and looked at the map. "And choosing the next ribbon? That I have to think about."

"That message goes all the way up," said {guide}.

### Scene 4. A tenth of a brain? (Act IV, chapter 3: the newspaper's two explanations)

**Short:** The *Gazette* puzzle corner says people use only a tenth of their brains. The librarian frowns. "Let's check."

**Longer:** The *Willowbank Gazette* had a new puzzle corner. Under two arrow pictures, it said: "People only use one tenth of their brains!"

"Then I have lots to spare," said Bramble.

The librarian set down a book of old drawings. "These were drawn by a scientist in Spain more than a century ago. Each little tree is one brain cell."

"There are gaps between them," said Dot.

"Tiny gaps. And scientists can now watch which parts of a brain are busy. Nearly all of it does something."

Bramble studied the arrow picture. "This top line is longer. Anyone can see that."

{guide} handed her a ruler.

### Scene 5. Sweet bread (Act II, chapter 3: Ember's birthday)

**Short:** In the fold-out book, a person chews a bread roll for a long time. It starts to taste a little sweet. Why?

**Longer:** The café was full for Ember's birthday. The baker's rolls were still warm.

The librarian opened *A Person's Body* to a new page. "It says here a person who chews bread for a long while finds it tastes a little sweet."

"Bread isn't sweet," said Ember.

"Bread is mostly starch," said the baker. "Starch is a long chain of sugars, all joined up."

"So something snips the chain," said {guide}. "Something in the spit."

"Enzymes," read the librarian. "Tiny helpers. And the same helper snips again and again."

### Scene 6. The miller's sluice (Act V, a better flood post)

**Short:** The miller keeps her mill pond inside a band. Too high, she opens the sluice. Too low, she closes it.

**Longer:** The miller walked {guide} along the mill pond's edge. Two painted marks showed on the post: a high mark and a low mark.

"Too high, and the wheel floods," she said. "Too low, and it stops."

"So you watch it?"

"All day. If it creeps up, I open the sluice a little. If it creeps down, I close it a little. Never all or nothing."

{guide} looked at the fold-out book under their arm. "A body does that with sugar in the blood."

"Does it have a sluice?"

"It has a pancreas," said {guide}.

### Scene 7. The parcel sprint (Summer Market Fair)

**Short:** Pip wins the parcel sprint. Then he pants and pants, long after he stops. The baker's dough rises by itself.

**Longer:** The parcel sprint ran from the post office across the green. Pip ran fastest. Then he flopped down and panted.

"You've stopped running," said Dot. "Why are you still puffing?"

"My legs feel heavy," said Pip. "Like full sacks."

"Those leg muscles ran short of oxygen," said {guide}. "They got some energy another way, a quick way. Now the panting pays back the oxygen."

Behind the stall, the baker lifted a cloth. The dough had doubled.

"Hardly any air gets inside my dough," said the baker. "My yeast doesn't mind."

## Teach cards

Each worked example reveals one step per tap.

**T1. Body parts** (A true thing about science). A body has a head, a neck, arms, hands, legs and feet. Pip has them too, with fur and a tail.
Worked example: (1) Tap the drawn person's head. (2) Tap Pip's head. (3) Both have a head.

**T2. Five senses.** Eyes see. Ears hear. The nose smells. The tongue tastes. The skin feels touch.
Worked example: (1) The cat's bell rings. (2) It is a sound. (3) Ears hear sounds.

**T3. How the parts work.** Light comes into the eyes. Eyes send out no beams. Skin feels all over the body. The tongue does most of the tasting.
Worked example: (1) The lantern shines. (2) Its light goes into the eyes. (3) Now the lantern is seen.

**T4. Bones have three jobs** (recap). Bones hold a body up. They protect soft parts inside. With muscles, they move.
Worked example: (1) The skull covers the brain. (2) It keeps the brain safe. (3) Its job: protection.

**T5. Two names, one bone.** Each bone has a science name and an everyday name. The femur is the thigh bone. The humerus is the upper-arm bone.
Worked example: (1) Read the tag: femur. (2) Turn it over: thigh bone. (3) Hang it on the thigh.

**T6. Inside or outside.** A person's skeleton is inside. Dot's hard skeleton is on the outside. An earthworm has no hard skeleton at all.
Worked example: (1) A crab. (2) Its hard outer case is its skeleton. (3) It goes on the outside shelf.

**T7. Two parts of one system.** The brain and spinal cord are the central nervous system. Nerves branch from them to every part. Messages go in from the senses and out to muscles.
Worked example: (1) The apple is tossed. (2) Eyes send a message in. (3) The brain decides. (4) Arm muscles catch.

**T8. In and out.** Sensory nerves carry messages in to the spinal cord and brain. Motor nerves carry messages out to muscles. Nerves carry signals, not blood.
Worked example: (1) The thistle pricks. (2) A sensory nerve carries it in. (3) A motor nerve carries the answer out.

**T9. The shortcut.** In a reflex, the message turns around in the spinal cord. The foot moves first. The brain feels "ouch" after.
Worked example: (1) Thistle. (2) Message in to the spinal cord. (3) Straight back out: foot lifts. (4) Then "ouch" reaches the brain.

**T10. A neuron** (Sapling). A neuron is a nerve cell. Its branches pick up signals. Its long axon carries an electrical signal. Myelin wraps it and speeds it.
Worked example: (1) Branches pick up a signal. (2) It runs along the axon. (3) It reaches the end.

**T11. Across the gap.** Neurons don't touch. At the gap, the synapse, a chemical messenger crosses. It can start a new signal in the next neuron.
Worked example: (1) The signal reaches the gap. (2) Chemical messengers cross. (3) The next neuron fires.

**T12. A brain map.** Different parts do different jobs: seeing at the back, hearing at the sides, planning at the front. Most tasks use many parts together.
Worked example: (1) The magpie flashes past. (2) The back of the brain handles seeing. (3) The front plans: duck!

**T13. Practice changes connections.** Connections used again and again grow stronger and faster. This happens at any age.
Worked example: (1) Dot rings a new bell pattern. (2) She practices every morning. (3) The link thickens. (4) It gets easier.

**T14. The brain builds what we see.** Eyes take in light. The brain makes sense of it. Sometimes it builds a picture that a ruler disagrees with.
Worked example: (1) Two arrow lines. (2) Most people see the top one as longer. (3) The ruler says they match.

**T15. Follow the food** (Sapling). Mouth, food pipe, stomach, small intestine, large intestine. The liver, gallbladder and pancreas help from the side.
Worked example: (1) A bite of bread. (2) Mouth, food pipe, stomach. (3) Small intestine: taken into blood. (4) Large intestine: water back.

**T16. Squash and snip.** Physical digestion breaks food into smaller pieces. Chemical digestion changes big molecules into small ones, using enzymes.
Worked example: (1) Teeth chew: squash. (2) Stomach churns: squash. (3) Amylase cuts starch into short sugar chains: snip.

**T17. Enzymes are helpers.** An enzyme speeds up a change and is not used up. It is not alive. Small molecules can pass into the blood; big ones can't.
Worked example: (1) Amylase cuts a starch chain. (2) It cuts again, and again. (3) It is still whole.

**T18. Holding steady** (Keeper's Desk). A sensor notices a change. A control center sends a signal. An effector pushes the level back toward the set point.
Worked example: (1) The mill pond rises. (2) The miller sees it. (3) She opens the sluice. (4) The pond falls back.

**T19. The sugar seesaw.** Sugar in the blood rises: the pancreas sends insulin, and the level falls. It falls: the pancreas sends glucagon, and the level rises.
Worked example: (1) Supper is digested. (2) Blood sugar rises. (3) Insulin helps cells take sugar in. (4) The level falls back.

**T20. Two message systems.** Nerves are fast and short. Hormones travel in the blood from glands. They are slower and last longer. The sugar loop needs no nerve message.
Worked example: (1) Thistle: a nerve message, in a flash. (2) Supper: insulin, over a longer while.

**T21. Short of oxygen.** Usually: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water, plus energy. In a sprint, muscles also break glucose down without oxygen, into lactic acid.
Worked example: (1) Jog: oxygen keeps up. (2) Sprint: it can't. (3) Some glucose becomes lactic acid.

**T22. Less energy, and a debt.** Without oxygen, each glucose releases much less energy. Afterward, fast breathing brings extra oxygen. Some of it helps clear the lactic acid.
Worked example: (1) Pip stops. (2) He keeps panting. (3) The extra oxygen pays back the debt.

**T23. Yeast without oxygen.** Yeast in dough breaks sugar down without oxygen. It makes carbon dioxide and ethanol. The gas puffs up the dough.
Worked example: (1) Yeast in dough. (2) Bubbles of carbon dioxide. (3) The dough rises. (4) Most of the ethanol bakes off in the oven.

## History cards

**Aristotle's five (A true thing from history).** Long ago in Greece, a thinker named Aristotle wrote down five senses: sight, hearing, smell, taste and touch. Scientists now count more, like balance.

**Ibn al-Haytham (A true thing from history).** About a thousand years ago, in Basra and Cairo, Ibn al-Haytham showed that light comes into the eyes. He said the mind also judges what it sees. He explained why the Moon looks bigger near the horizon.

**Vesalius's bones (A true thing from history).** In 1543, Andreas Vesalius printed a great book of the human body, drawn from real skeletons. He showed a person's lower jaw is one bone. An old teacher, Galen, had said two, from studying animals.

**Cajal and Golgi (A true thing from history).** Camillo Golgi in Italy found a stain that showed nerve cells. Santiago Ramón y Cajal in Spain used it and drew each cell separate, with gaps. Golgi thought they joined in one net. In 1906 they shared a Nobel Prize, still disagreeing. Cajal's view won out.

**The taxi drivers (A true thing about science, Keeper's Desk).** In 2000, scientists scanned the brains of London taxi drivers, who learn thousands of streets. The back part of one memory area was larger, more so in drivers who had worked longer. It matches, but doesn't prove, practice changing the brain.

**The first enzyme (A true thing from history).** In 1833, two French chemists, Anselme Payen and Jean-François Persoz, drew a substance out of sprouted barley. It turned starch into sugar. They called it diastase. It was the first enzyme found.

**Inner steadiness (A true thing from history).** Claude Bernard, in France, wrote that living things keep a steady world inside them. In 1926 Walter Cannon, in the United States, named it homeostasis.

**The oxygen debt (A true thing from history).** A. V. Hill in Britain studied runners' muscles. He named the oxygen debt. He and Otto Meyerhof in Germany shared the 1922 Nobel Prize. Later scientists changed many of their ideas.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Taste buds:** tiny parts, mostly on the tongue, that sense tastes.
- **Feelers:** an insect's antennae, used to smell and touch.
- **Sense:** a way a body notices the world: sight, hearing, smell, taste or touch.
- **Skeleton:** the hard frame that holds a body up, protects it and helps it move.
- **Skull:** the head bones, a case around the brain.
- **Spine:** the backbone, a stack of small bones called **vertebrae**.
- **Ribcage:** the curved rib bones around the heart and lungs.
- **Pelvis:** the hip bones, joining the spine to the legs.
- **Femur:** the thigh bone, the longest bone.
- **Humerus:** the upper-arm bone.
- **Exoskeleton:** a hard skeleton on the outside, like a ladybug's or a crab's.
- **Central nervous system:** the brain and the spinal cord.
- **Spinal cord:** a thick bundle of nerve cells inside the spine.
- **Nerve:** a bundle of long nerve cells that carries signals.
- **Sensory nerve:** carries messages in from the senses.
- **Motor nerve:** carries messages out to muscles.
- **Reflex:** a fast action that happens without deciding.
- **Neuron:** a nerve cell.
- **Axon:** the long part of a neuron that carries its signal.
- **Myelin:** a fatty wrap around an axon that speeds the signal.
- **Synapse:** the tiny gap between two neurons.
- **Electrochemical:** electrical along the neuron, chemical across the gap.
- **Lobe:** one of the brain's big regions.
- **Limbic system:** parts deep in the brain linked to emotions and memory.
- **Neuroplasticity:** the brain changing its connections with use.
- **Illusion:** a picture the brain sees differently from what a ruler measures.
- **Liver, gallbladder, pancreas:** helper organs beside the food tube. The liver makes bile, the gallbladder stores it, the pancreas makes enzymes and hormones.
- **Starch, protein, fat:** big molecules in food, each cut by its own enzyme.
- **Digestion:** breaking food down so a body can take it in.
- **Physical digestion:** breaking food into smaller pieces.
- **Chemical digestion:** changing big molecules into small ones.
- **Molecule:** a tiny particle of a substance, far too small to see.
- **Enzyme:** a helper made by cells that speeds a change and isn't used up.
- **Amylase:** an enzyme that cuts starch into short sugar chains, mostly pairs.
- **Maltase:** an enzyme on the small intestine's wall that splits sugar pairs into single sugars.
- **Catalyst:** something that speeds a change without being used up.
- **Bile:** a liquid from the liver that splits fat into small drops.
- **Villi:** tiny finger shapes lining the small intestine.
- **Absorb:** to take in, here from the gut into the blood.
- **Homeostasis:** keeping the inside of a body steady.
- **Set point:** the level a body steers back toward.
- **Negative feedback:** a response that opposes a change.
- **Sensor, control center, effector:** the noticer, the decider and the doer in a loop.
- **Gland:** a body part that releases hormones.
- **Hormone:** a chemical messenger carried in the blood.
- **Insulin:** a hormone that lowers blood sugar.
- **Glucagon:** a hormone that raises blood sugar.
- **Glucose:** a sugar that cells use for energy.
- **Aerobic respiration:** releasing energy from glucose using oxygen.
- **Anaerobic respiration:** releasing a little energy from glucose without oxygen.
- **Lactic acid:** what muscles make from glucose without oxygen.
- **Oxygen debt:** the extra oxygen a body uses after hard effort.
- **Sluice:** a gate that lets water out of a pond.
- **Yeast:** a tiny fungus bakers use; it makes gas that raises dough.
- **Ethanol:** a chemical yeast makes along with the gas. Most of it bakes off in the oven.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** naming outside parts and matching each sense to its part, on a drawn person and animals; six major bones by science name, everyday name and job, and inside versus outside skeletons; the central nervous system and nerves, with the spinal reflex shortcut; how neurons signal, a brain map by job, practice changing connections and illusions as evidence; physical and chemical digestion and enzymes as catalysts; homeostasis as negative feedback, with insulin and glucagon, and nerves versus hormones; anaerobic respiration, lactic acid and the oxygen debt, compared with aerobic respiration and with yeast.

**What it does not claim:** the benches are made-up models with stated rules. The sugar dial is not a reading and measures nobody. The sprint tokens are not real energy units; textbooks give roughly 30 to 38 units of usable energy (ATP) per glucose aerobically and 2 anaerobically. In the body, lactic acid is mostly present as lactate. It is linked to the burn of hard effort but is not its only cause (acid build-up and other changes matter too), and it does not cause next-day soreness. Scientists now call the oxygen debt EPOC: the extra oxygen also refills muscle energy stores, and clearing lactic acid is only one part. Ethanol is named only as something yeast makes in bread dough; nothing here is about drinks. Nothing here is advice about health, food, sugar, exercise or the brain. Puberty, illness and medicine are left to families.

**Every body here is a drawing, a wooden model or an animal.** No screen asks a child to point to, feel, taste, measure or describe their own body, and nothing about anyone's body is stored.
