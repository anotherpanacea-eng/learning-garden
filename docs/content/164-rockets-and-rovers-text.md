# Rockets, Rovers and the Long Way Up: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 164](../specs/164-rockets-and-rovers.md). Use as written, after independent review and a subject check. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion and is never gendered. Pip is a fox (he/him), Dot a ladybug (she/her), Bramble a girl witch (she/her) with a magpie, Ember a pocket-dragon. The librarian is they/them. Lines marked `[check]` need a subject check before build. Nothing here is scored.

**Labels used on every card:** *A story from Willowbank* (fiction) and *A true thing from history* or *A true thing about space* (checked fact). Letters from Nuria and Léon are fiction; the astronauts they mention are real.

## The story

### Scene 1. How would a dragon get up there? *(A story from Willowbank)*

**Short:**
> Ember wants to go to space. "Flap harder," says Bramble. "Rockets push on the air." Her magpie lets go of a balloon. It shoots off across the room.

**Longer:**
> Ember has been reading the star chart upside down for an hour. "I want to go up there," Ember says. "How?"
>
> "Flap harder," says Bramble. "Rockets push on the air. Everyone knows that."
>
> Pip is more worried about lunch. "What do astronauts eat? Do they have pies?"
>
> The librarian ties a string from the bookcase to the window and threads a straw on it. "Let's find out how a rocket pushes first," they say. "Pies after."
>
> Bramble's magpie has found a balloon. It blows, badly, then lets go. The balloon whizzes away from the magpie, spluttering, and bumps the window.
>
> "Air came out the back," says {guide}. "And the balloon went the other way."
>
> "Into the air," says Bramble. "Which it pushed on."
>
> "Maybe," says the librarian. "There's a way to check."

**Keeper's note:**
> Bramble is half right that something is pushed. The question is what. A rocket pushes its own gas out, and the gas pushes back on the rocket. Nothing else is needed, which is why rockets work in space.

### Scene 2. The letter that took three days *(A story from Willowbank)*

**Short:**
> Dot sorts a letter from Hollis, three days from the coast. "Hollis always sent the whole plan at once," she says. "No time for questions."

**Longer:**
> Dot is sorting the morning post on the library steps. A letter from the coast has Hollis's spiky writing on it.
>
> "Three days to get here," she says. "Three days for an answer to go back. So Hollis never wrote, *Shall I mend the gate?* Hollis wrote, *Mend the gate, then the fence, and if it rains, leave the fence.*"
>
> "That's a plan with an if in it," says {guide}.
>
> The librarian looks up from a picture of a rover on red sand. "That's how people drive a robot on Mars," they say. "A message there takes minutes. So you send the whole day's plan, with the ifs, and the rover does it alone."
>
> Pip looks at the sand tray in the corner. "Can I be the rover?"
>
> "You can write the plan," says Dot. "The rover is the one that listens."

**Keeper's note:**
> On the roof you saw how long a message takes to reach Mars. Here the question is what that delay changes: who decides, and when. Dot's post road is fiction. The rover's problem is real.

### Scene 3. Two letters from over the hills *(A story from Willowbank, with a true thing from history)*

**Short:**
> Nuria writes about Pedro Duque, the first Spanish astronaut. Léon writes about Claudie Haigneré, the first French woman in space. Léon says his came first.

**Longer:**
> Two letters arrive on the same day, which Dot says never happens.
>
> Nuria writes from Vadoalto: *Our library has a picture of Pedro Duque. He was the first astronaut from Spain. He flew on the Space Shuttle in 1998.*
>
> Léon writes from Les Saules: *Ours has Claudie Haigneré. She was the first French woman in space, in 1996. She did science on the Mir station. That is two years earlier.*
>
> Bramble counts on her fingers. "1996 is before 1998. Léon wins."
>
> "It isn't a race between towns," says {guide}. "But you could put them both on the strip."
>
> The librarian hands over two cards. "Put them where they go. Then look at who else is up there."

**Keeper's note:**
> The letters are made up. Both astronauts are real, and both facts are checked. The space age had people from many countries: the first person in space and the first woman were Soviet, and the International Space Station is shared by several space agencies.

## Teach cards

### Balloon Line

**Teach 1 (A true thing about space):** A balloon pushes air out of its neck. The air pushes the balloon the other way.
*Worked example, tap by tap:* 1. The neck points left. 2. Air rushes out to the left. 3. The balloon goes right.

**Teach 2:** A rocket does the same with hot gas. It throws gas down, and the gas pushes it up.

**Teach 3:** A rocket doesn't need air to push on. It pushes on its own gas.
*Worked example:* 1. Switch on **No air**. 2. Let go. 3. The balloon still goes, a little farther.

**Teach 4 (Sapling):** To lift off, the push up must be bigger than the rocket's weight. Remember the force arrows from the hoist?

**Teach 5 (Keeper's Desk):** Every push is between two things. Each pushes the other: same size, opposite ways.
*Worked example:* 1. Two skaters push apart. 2. Both meters read the same. 3. The smaller skater speeds away faster.

**Teach 6 (Keeper's Desk):** A pair never cancels, because its two forces act on different things.
*Worked example:* 1. A book sits on a table. 2. Gravity pulls the book down; the table pushes it up. Both act on the book. 3. They balance, but they aren't a pair. 4. The book's weight's partner: the book pulling Earth up.

**Model line (always shown):** A simplified picture: real rockets burn fuel to make hot gas, and the No air room is pretend. The arrows show which way each push goes.

**Arrow labels:** *The balloon pushes the gas.* · *The gas pushes the balloon.*

### Rocket history *(A true thing from history)*

**History card 1:** Rockets were first made in China, with gunpowder, by the 1200s. Historians still argue about the first exact date.

**History card 2:** In 1926 Robert Goddard flew the first rocket that burned liquid fuel. It rose about 12 meters, on his aunt's farm.

**History card 3 (Sapling):** Scientists in Russia, the USA and Germany worked out rocket science on their own, at about the same time.

### Station Day

**Teach 7 (A true thing about space):** Astronauts are people with a job. Many were pilots, scientists, doctors or engineers first. Then they train for years.

**Teach 8:** On the space station, everything floats. Water makes wobbly balls.

**Teach 9:** They float because they and the station fall around Earth together. Gravity is still there.
*Worked example:* 1. Gravity keeps the Moon going around Earth. 2. It keeps the station going around too. 3. Everything inside falls with the station, so nothing lands.

**Hotspot lines (one sentence each, Sprout-friendly):**
- **Water blob:** Water floats in a wobbly ball. Astronauts drink from pouches with straws.
- **Food pouch:** Food comes in pouches and packets, so crumbs don't float away.
- **Sleeping bag:** You sleep in a bag tied to the wall. There's no up or down.
- **Treadmill:** Astronauts exercise about two hours every day, held down by straps, to keep bones and muscles strong.
- **Recycling unit:** Almost all the water is cleaned and used again, even from sweat and breath.
- **Window:** The station goes around Earth about every 90 minutes. That's about 16 sunrises a day.
- **Spacesuit:** Outside there is no air. The suit gives air and keeps you safe.

**Lift-flap:** *Why does everything float?* → They are all falling around Earth together. Nothing ever reaches the floor.

### The Long Way Up *(A true thing from history)*

**Teach 10:** The first person went to space in 1961. On the Long Line, the space age is short and recent.

**Decade strip cards (tap for details):**
- **1926** Goddard's liquid-fuel rocket flies (USA).
- **1957** Sputnik 1, the first satellite, launched by the Soviet Union.
- **1961** Yuri Gagarin, the first person in space (Soviet Union).
- **1962** Katherine Johnson checks the orbit math by hand for John Glenn's flight. Glenn asked for her.
- **1963** Valentina Tereshkova, the first woman in space. She went around Earth 48 times.
- **1969** Apollo 11. Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin walk on the Moon; Michael Collins orbits.
- **1990** The Hubble Space Telescope goes up on the Space Shuttle.
- **1996** Claudie Haigneré, the first French woman in space.
- **1998** Pedro Duque, the first Spanish astronaut. John Glenn flew again on that trip.
- **2000** The first crew moves into the International Space Station. Crews took turns there, with no gap, for decades.
- **2021** Perseverance (USA) and Zhurong (China) land on Mars. No person had ever been there.
- **2021** The James Webb Space Telescope launches from French Guiana.

**Katherine Johnson details:** Katherine Johnson was a NASA mathematician. She worked out paths for spacecraft, and helped with paths for Apollo.

**Hubble details:** Hubble's mirror came out a tiny bit wrong. In 1993 astronauts fitted parts to fix the view.

**Long Line link (when spec 133 is built):** *Send to the Long Line.* → *The whole space age is shorter than one box.*

**Keeper's note:** In April 2026, Artemis II carried four astronauts around the Moon and home, one from Canada. Nobody has lived on the Moon.

### Rover Yard

**Teach 11 (A true thing about space):** Robots go where people can't yet. Mars is far, cold, with air we can't breathe.

**Teach 12:** A rover has wheels for rocks, cameras for eyes, and an arm with a drill.

**Teach 13:** Rovers look for signs that water flowed on Mars long ago. Where there was water, life might once have been possible.

**Teach 14:** Messages take minutes to reach Mars. So you send a whole plan, and the rover does it alone.
*Worked example:* 1. Write: forward, forward, drill. 2. Press **Send**. 3. Wait while the message travels. 4. The rover drives and drills.

**Rover cards:**
- **Sojourner (1997):** The first Mars rover. About as big as a microwave oven.
- **Curiosity (2012):** As big as a car. It climbed partway up a mountain in a crater.
- **Perseverance (2021):** As big as a car. It packs rock samples in tubes.
- **Zhurong (2021):** China's first Mars rover. It looked for signs of ice under the ground.

**Yard lines:** *Message on its way…* · *Plan done.* · *My cameras see a rock. I stopped. Send a new plan.* · *Soft sand ahead. I stopped.*

**Model line:** A made-up map and a squeezed delay. A real message to Mars takes 3 to 22 minutes one way, and real rovers drive slowly, then stop and look.

### Light Bucket

**Teach 15 (A true thing about space):** A telescope is a light bucket. A bigger mirror catches more light.
*Worked example:* 1. Two buckets stand in the rain. 2. One is twice as wide. 3. It fills four times as fast.

**Teach 16:** More light lets you see fainter, farther things. Making things bigger comes second.

**Teach 17:** Air wobbles starlight. That's why stars twinkle. Above the air, the view is sharp.

**Teach 18:** Space telescopes go up to get above the air. Getting closer hardly helps at all.

**Telescope cards:**
- **Hubble (1990):** Its mirror is 2.4 meters across. It has photographed galaxies far away.
- **Webb (2021):** Its mirror is 6.5 meters, in 18 pieces, folded to fit. It sees infrared, light our eyes can't see.
- **Deep field:** Every smudge in this picture is a whole galaxy, full of stars.

**Model line:** A simplified picture: the pictures are drawn, and real mirrors bring light to a point.

### Mission Board *(Keeper's Desk)*

**Teach 19:** NASA lists five big hazards for long trips: radiation, being shut in, distance, gravity, and a sealed home.

**Hazard cards:** **Radiation:** Away from Earth, there's more harmful radiation. · **Shut in together:** A small crew, a small space, for years. · **Distance:** Help is far away, and messages are slow. · **Gravity:** Bones and muscles weaken month by month without exercise. · **Sealed home:** Air, water and warmth all come from machines.

**Fix cards (each with a cost):** Thick shielding *(heavy)* · Exercise machines *(space and time)* · Recycle water *(machines can break)* · Carry spare parts *(heavy)* · Train crews to fix things alone *(years of training)* · A faster trip *(more fuel)* · Private calls home, recorded *(no live chat)* · Grow some food *(slow, uncertain)*

**Are we alone? (A true thing from history), three cards:**
1. In 1950, Enrico Fermi wondered at lunch: if the galaxy is old and huge, where is everybody?
2. In 1960, Frank Drake listened to two nearby stars with a radio telescope. He heard nothing.
3. No search since has found a sure signal. Only a tiny part of the sky has been searched. The question is still open.

## Problem sets

Each item: prompt, choices or answer, tags for the misconception each wrong choice tests, hint, explanation. Say-why answers are spoken to a grown-up and never stored.

### Set A. Pushes and rockets

**A1 · Sprout · 3-choice**
Air rushes out of a balloon's back. Where does the balloon go?
- Backward, with the air. *Tag: `same-way`.* Feedback: Look again. The air goes back; the balloon goes the other way.
- Away from the air. ✓ Feedback: Yes. Air out one way, balloon the other.
- Straight down to the floor. *Tag: `just-falls`.* Feedback: It does fall at the end. First, it zooms forward.
Hint: Watch the neck. Explanation: The balloon pushes the air out, and the air pushes the balloon away.

**A2 · Seedling · 3-choice**
Why can a rocket fly in space, where there is no air?
- Space itself pulls rockets up and away from Earth. *Tag: `space-pulls`.* Feedback: Nothing pulls it up. Earth's gravity pulls it down.
- It pushes hard against the air around it. *Tag: `push-air`.* Feedback: There's no air in space to push on. Try the No air switch.
- It pushes gas out; the gas pushes back. ✓ Feedback: Yes. It pushes on its own gas.
Hint: What did the balloon do in the No air room? Explanation: A rocket carries what it pushes on. That's why it works in space.

**A3 · Seedling · 3-choice**
A rocket's gas shoots straight down. Which way does the rocket go?
- Up. ✓ Feedback: Yes. Gas down, rocket up.
- Down, with the gas. *Tag: `same-way`.* Feedback: The gas goes down. The rocket goes the opposite way.
- Sideways. *Tag: `guess-direction`.* Feedback: Look at the gas. The rocket goes straight the other way.
Hint: Think of the balloon's neck. Explanation: The push on the rocket is opposite to the way the gas goes.

**A4 · Sapling · say why (read with a grown-up)**
Bramble says rockets push on the air. Use the No air room to answer her.
Look for: the balloon still moved with no air; it pushes on its own gas. Tag if missing: `push-air`.
Hint: What changed when the air was gone? Explanation: If rockets needed air, the balloon would stop in the No air room. It didn't.

**A5 · Keeper's Desk · 3-choice**
A big skater and a small skater push apart. Which meter reads more?
- The big skater's meter. *Tag: `bigger-pushes-harder`.* Feedback: The meters match. Being bigger changes how fast you move, not the push.
- The small skater's meter. *Tag: `smaller-feels-more`.* Feedback: The small one moves faster, but the pushes match.
- Both read the same. ✓ Feedback: Yes. Same size, opposite ways, on two different skaters.
Hint: It's one push between two people. Explanation: The forces are equal. The smaller skater speeds up more because there is less of them to move.

**A6 · Keeper's Desk · 3-choice**
A book lies on a table. What is the partner of the book's weight?
- The table pushing up on the book. *Tag: `same-body`.* Feedback: That one acts on the book too. A pair acts on two different things.
- The book pulling Earth up. ✓ Feedback: Yes. Earth pulls the book; the book pulls Earth. Same type, two bodies.
- Nothing. The forces cancel. *Tag: `pair-cancels`.* Feedback: Forces on the book balance. Pair partners act on different things, so they can't cancel.
Hint: Swap the two things: Earth pulls the book, so… Explanation: The weight is Earth's pull on the book. Its partner is the book's pull on Earth.

### Set B. People in space

**B1 · Sprout · 3-choice**
Outside the station, astronauts wear spacesuits. What is there none of, outside?
- Air. ✓ Feedback: Yes. The suit gives air to breathe.
- Light. *Tag: `dark-space`.* Feedback: The Sun shines brightly up there. The missing thing is air.
- Gravity. *Tag: `no-gravity`.* Feedback: Gravity is still there. It keeps the station going around Earth.
Hint: What do you breathe? Explanation: Inside the station there is air. Outside there isn't, so the suit carries it.

**B2 · Sprout · 3-choice**
Where have astronauts lived for months at a time?
- On a base on the Moon. *Tag: `moon-now`.* Feedback: People have walked on the Moon. Nobody has lived there.
- On a space station. ✓ Feedback: Yes. A space station goes around Earth.
- In a camp on Mars. *Tag: `people-on-mars`.* Feedback: No person has been to Mars yet. Robots have.
Hint: Look at the model hanging from the ceiling. Explanation: From 2000, crews took turns living on the International Space Station, with no gap for decades.

**B3 · Seedling · 3-choice**
Why does a drop of water float on the station?
- There's no gravity up in space at all. *Tag: `no-gravity`.* Feedback: Gravity is still there. It keeps the station circling Earth.
- The station spins it up. *Tag: `spin-floats`.* Feedback: The station doesn't spin things up. Think about falling.
- They fall around Earth together. ✓ Feedback: Yes. They fall together, so it never lands.
Hint: Open the lift-flap. Explanation: Everything inside falls at the same rate as the station, so nothing reaches the floor.

**B4 · Seedling · number with steps**
The station goes around Earth every 90 minutes. How many times in a day?
Answer: 16.
Steps: 1. A day is 24 hours. 2. 24 × 60 = 1,440 minutes. 3. 1,440 ÷ 90 = 16.
Tag: forgetting to change hours to minutes (`unit-slip`).
Hint: Change hours to minutes first. Explanation: That's 16 sunrises a day, which is why the crew sleeps by the clock.

**B5 · Sapling · order the cards**
Put these in order: Apollo 11 · Gagarin · Sputnik · Tereshkova.
Answer: Sputnik (1957), Gagarin (1961), Tereshkova (1963), Apollo 11 (1969).
Tag: putting Apollo 11 first because it is famous (`usa-first`).
Hint: Which came first, a machine or a person? Explanation: The first three were Soviet firsts. The Moon landing came 12 years after Sputnik.

**B6 · Sapling · number with steps**
Given: Tereshkova went around Earth 48 times in about 71 hours.
About how long was one trip around?
Answer: about 89 minutes.
Steps: 1. 71 hours × 60 = 4,260 minutes. 2. 4,260 ÷ 48 ≈ 89 minutes.
Tag: dividing hours and calling it minutes (`unit-slip`).
Hint: Make it minutes, then share it out. Explanation: About an hour and a half, close to the station's 90 minutes today. Low orbits take about that long.

**B7 · Keeper's Desk · 3-choice**
One box is 100 years. How long is Sputnik (1957) to Webb (2021)?
- Less than one box. ✓ Feedback: Yes. 64 years is shorter than one box.
- About 6 boxes in a row. *Tag: `decade-box`.* Feedback: That's counting tens of years. Each box is 100 years.
- 64 boxes. *Tag: `year-box`.* Feedback: That would be one box per year. Each box is 100 years.
Hint: 2021 − 1957 first. Explanation: 64 years is shorter than one box. On a scaled line the whole space age is a thin sliver.

### Set C. Robots and telescopes

**C1 · Seedling · 3-choice**
Why do we send rovers to Mars instead of people, for now?
- People already live there. *Tag: `people-on-mars`.* Feedback: No person has been to Mars yet.
- It's very far, with air we can't breathe. ✓ Feedback: Yes. A robot can go where we can't safely.
- Robots cost less, and that's the only reason. *Tag: `cheaper-only`.* Feedback: Cost matters, but the big reason is danger.
Hint: Think about air and distance. Explanation: Mars is far and cold, its air is mostly carbon dioxide, and radiation is strong.

**C2 · Seedling · 3-choice**
Why put a telescope like Hubble up above the air?
- To get much closer to the faraway stars. *Tag: `closer-to-stars`.* Feedback: It's barely closer at all. The air is the problem.
- To make things look bigger. *Tag: `zoom-only`.* Feedback: Size isn't why. The air wobbles the light.
- The air wobbles and blurs the light. ✓ Feedback: Yes. Above the air, the view holds still.
Hint: Why do stars twinkle? Explanation: Moving air bends starlight. A telescope above it sees sharply.

**C3 · Sapling · 3-choice**
A bucket is twice as wide as another. How much more rain does it catch?
- Twice as much. *Tag: `width-not-area`.* Feedback: The opening is twice as wide and twice as long. Count the squares.
- Four times as much. ✓ Feedback: Yes. 2 × 2 = 4 times the opening.
- The same, since rain is rain. *Tag: `same-catch`.* Feedback: A bigger opening catches more drops.
Hint: Draw both openings on squared paper. Explanation: A mirror twice as wide catches four times the light. That's why telescope mirrors are big.

**C4 · Sapling · 3-choice (plan)**
Given: a rock is four squares ahead of the rover.
Which plan stops beside it, then drills?
- Forward 3, Drill. ✓ Feedback: Yes. Three squares puts it right beside the rock.
- Forward 4, Drill. *Tag: `overshoot`.* Feedback: Square 4 is the rock. The cameras would stop it with nothing done.
- Turn left, Forward 3, Drill. *Tag: `turn-slip`.* Feedback: Turning first points it away from the rock.
Hint: Count to the square before the rock. Explanation: A plan has to be right before you send it, because fixing it takes another message.

**C5 · Sapling · number with steps**
Given: Mars is 150 million km away. Radio goes about 300,000 km each second.
How long for a question and its answer?
Answer: 1,000 seconds, about 17 minutes.
Steps: 1. One way: 150,000,000 ÷ 300,000 = 500 seconds. 2. There and back: 500 × 2 = 1,000 seconds. 3. 1,000 ÷ 60 ≈ 17 minutes.
Tag: answering one way only (`one-way`).
Hint: The answer has to travel back too. Explanation: About 17 minutes before you hear anything. That's why rovers stop themselves when they see danger.

**C6 · Keeper's Desk · number with steps**
Given: treat both mirrors as circles. Webb's is 6.5 m wide; Hubble's is 2.4 m.
Webb's area is about how many times Hubble's?
Answer: about 7.3 times.
Steps: 1. Width ratio: 6.5 ÷ 2.4 ≈ 2.71. 2. Area grows with width × width. 3. 2.71 × 2.71 ≈ 7.3.
Tag: stopping at 2.7 (`width-not-area`).
Hint: Like the buckets: square the width ratio. Explanation: The circle model says about 7 times. NASA says about 6, because Webb's mirror isn't a full circle.

### Set D. The long way to Mars

**D1 · Sapling · 3-choice**
Why can't Earth talk a Mars crew through a problem, second by second?
- Radios don't work out in deep space. *Tag: `no-radio`.* Feedback: They do. Rovers send pictures home by radio.
- Mars is far too cold for radios to work. *Tag: `cold-radio`.* Feedback: Rovers' radios work there. The trouble is time.
- Each answer takes minutes to arrive. ✓ Feedback: Yes. The crew must often decide alone.
Hint: Compare the Moon: about 2.6 seconds there and back. Explanation: To Mars and back takes about 6 to 45 minutes. A Mars trip is much more than a long Moon trip.

**D2 · Keeper's Desk · number with steps**
Perseverance launched on 30 July 2020 and landed on 18 February 2021. How many days?
Answer: 203 days, about 6.7 months.
Steps: 1. The rest of July: 1 day. 2. August to January: 31 + 30 + 31 + 30 + 31 + 31 = 184. 3. February: 18. 4. 1 + 184 + 18 = 203.
Tag: counting months as 30 days each (`month-slip`).
Hint: Count whole months, then the ends. Explanation: Over six months, one way. A crew would need a way home too.

**D3 · Keeper's Desk · number with steps**
Given: 4 crew each use about 4 liters a day.
Given: 98 of every 100 liters are recycled.
In 30 days, how much water is lost?
Answer: 480 liters used; about 9.6 liters lost.
Steps: 1. 4 × 4 = 16 liters a day. 2. 16 × 30 = 480 liters. 3. 2 of every 100 is lost: 480 ÷ 100 × 2 = 9.6.
Tag: taking 98% as the amount lost (`percent-flip`).
Hint: Lost is the 2 out of 100. Explanation: Recycling turns 480 liters into less than 10 to replace. That's why the station's system matters for Mars.

**D4 · Keeper's Desk · true or not shown, then say why**
Given: in 1960 Drake listened to two stars for about 150 hours. He heard nothing.
Which statements does this show?
- The search found no signal from those two stars. **True.**
- No one is out there anywhere. **Not shown.** *Tag: `silence-proves`.* Feedback: Two stars, a few months, a few radio channels. The rest wasn't searched.
- Searching is pointless. **Not shown.** *Tag: `null-pointless`.* Feedback: A careful "nothing here" tells us where not to look next.
Say why (read with a grown-up): Give two different answers to Fermi's question. What would help tell them apart?
Look for: two ideas (for example, life is rare; or others exist but are far or quiet) and some evidence that could help. Tag if one idea is treated as settled: `solved`.
Hint: "No sign yet" and "nothing there" are different. Explanation: It's an open question. Scientists keep searching, and keep both ideas in mind.

## Project page (Act V, *The Long Way Up*)

## Key terms

- **Astronaut:** a trained person who travels to space. Russian astronauts are called cosmonauts.
- **Rocket:** a machine that flies by pushing hot gas out of its back.
- **Satellite:** something that goes around a planet. The Moon is natural; Sputnik was made by people.
- **Orbit:** the path of something going around a planet or star.
- **Space station:** a home and lab that goes around Earth, where astronauts live.
- **Rover:** a robot car that explores another world.
- **Telescope:** a tool that gathers faint light from far away, so we can see it.
- **Mirror (telescope):** a curved mirror that catches light and brings it to a point.
- **Galaxy:** a huge group of stars. Our Sun is one star in the Milky Way.
- **Force pair (Keeper's Desk):** two equal, opposite pushes or pulls between two things.
- **Radiation:** energy from space that can harm living things over time.
- **Signal:** a message carried by radio or light.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** how a rocket moves (gas out one way, rocket the other; no air needed), with Newton's third law at the oldest depth; life on the space station (floating as falling together, water recycling, sleep, exercise); a short timeline of space firsts by people from several countries, including Valentina Tereshkova, Katherine Johnson, Claudie Haigneré and Pedro Duque; why we send rovers and why they drive by plan; how telescopes gather light and why some go above the air; and, at Keeper's Desk, the hazards of a trip to Mars and the open question of other life.

**What it does not claim:** it does not teach orbital math or the second law formally. The No air room and the Rover Yard are made-up models. Disasters and losses in spaceflight are left out on purpose. Willowbank, its people and the letters are fiction.

**Sources in brief:** NASA Glenn Research Center (rocket principles), NASA Human Research Program (five hazards), ESA (Webb, Haigneré, Duque), Britannica (Project Ozma, Haigneré), and others listed in the source audit.

**Balloon-rocket safety (adult-led):** an adult blows up and handles balloons. Uninflated or burst balloon pieces are a choking hazard for young children; pick up every piece at once. Check for latex allergies, or use latex-free balloons. Keep the string at chest height, away from faces and stairs, and never across a doorway.
