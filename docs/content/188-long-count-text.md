# The Long Count: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 188](../specs/188-long-count.md). Use as written, after independent spec review and a math check. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion and is never gendered; `{courier}` is the explorer's courier. Pip is he/him, Dot she/her, Bramble she/her (with her magpie), Wren Halloway she/her, Nuria she/her, the librarian they/them. Before the end of Act III nobody names the old Keeper (Hollis rule). Real-world facts are in the [source audit](../source-audits/188-long-count.md); items still marked `[check]` stay off screen until cleared. Nothing here is scored or timed. Numbers use commas between groups of three digits, American style, and number words use no "and" for whole numbers.

## Labels used on every card

- **A story from Willowbank**: the town, its ledger, its counts and every character are made up.
- **A true thing about math**: checked math, explained simply.
- **A true thing from history**: something people really did or wrote.
- **A made-up model**: a simple game rule, shown on the card, that is not a real count.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. A thousand more (Act II, chapter 2: a garden across the seasons)

**Hook:** A new crate of seeds arrives at the seed store. Wren must change one number in her ledger.

**Short:**
> A crate of one thousand seeds arrives. Wren's ledger says 4,562. "Which digit changes?" she asks. Pip says all of them. Pip is mistaken.

**Longer:**
> The seed store smells of dust and sunflowers. Wren has counted every packet twice. Her ledger says 4,562 seeds.
>
> Then the cart comes up the lane with one more crate. The label says ONE THOUSAND SEEDS.
>
> "Now I have to count them all again," groans Pip.
>
> "No," says Wren. "A thousand more only changes the thousands." She rubs out the 4 and writes a 5. "5,562. The hundreds, tens and ones haven't moved."
>
> "What if the thousands were already a 9?" asks Bramble.
>
> Wren smiles. "Then it rolls over, like a cart wheel. 9,562 and a thousand more is 10,562. The ten-thousands get a 1."
>
> Pip counts the new crate anyway, to be sure. He gets to forty and stops.

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> Adding 1000 changes the thousands digit, unless that digit is 9. Then it rolls over to 0 and the next place goes up by one: 9,500 + 1000 = 10,500. Counting on in 10,000s or 100,000s works the same way, one place at a time.

### Scene 2. The bricks Pip read (Act II ending: A Town of Our Own)

**Hook:** The town's brick count for rebuilding is in the ledger. Pip reads it out loud, and reads it short.

**Short:**
> "Three hundred forty-eight bricks," reads Pip. "That's not enough." Wren points at the 0. "That zero is holding the hundreds. Read it again."

**Longer:**
> The town has stacked its bricks behind the café, ready to rebuild the wall by the bridge. Wren has written the count in her ledger.
>
> Pip reads it out to everyone. "Three hundred forty-eight bricks. Oh no. We need far more than that."
>
> The baker drops his spoon.
>
> "Look again," says Wren. "Four digits: 3,048. The 3 is in the thousands place."
>
> "But there's no hundreds," says Pip.
>
> "There are zero hundreds," says {guide}. "The 0 holds the place, so the 3 stays a thousands digit."
>
> Pip reads it slowly. "Three thousand forty-eight." He looks at the stack. "That's much better."
>
> The baker picks up his spoon.

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> A zero in the middle of a number does a job. It keeps every other digit in its proper place. Take it out and 3,048 shrinks to 348.

### Scene 3. Nuria's tally (Act III, chapter 2: the seed exchange)

**Hook:** Nuria brings Vadoalto's count of reed stems. It has six digits and a comma.

**Short:**
> Nuria's count of reeds: 403,072. "Read up to the comma first," she says. "Cuatrocientos tres mil setenta y dos!" Bramble tries it in English.

**Longer** (Spanish line for a fluent reader's check):
> Nuria unrolls a long paper on the library table. "Vadoalto counted its reed stems this spring," she says. "Every bundle, every stem."
>
> The number says 403,072.
>
> Pip squints. "Four, zero, three, zero, seven, two."
>
> "That's digits, not a number," says Bramble. "How do you say it?"
>
> "*Cuatrocientos tres mil setenta y dos,*" says Nuria. "In English, start with the group before the comma."
>
> Bramble tries. "Four hundred three. Then the comma says thousand. Four hundred three thousand."
>
> "And after the comma?"
>
> "Seventy-two." Bramble grins. "Four hundred three thousand seventy-two."
>
> Wren writes it in the ledger under Willowbank's seeds. "Bigger than ours," she says. "Much bigger. Which place tells you that first?"

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> Commas split a big number into groups of three: ones, thousands, millions. Read each group as a small number, then say its name. 7,045,200 is seven million, forty-five thousand, two hundred.

### Scene 4. The headline (Act V, path 2: The Willowbank Gazette)

**Hook:** The librarian wants a headline about seeds swapped across the valley. Bramble wants every digit.

**Short:**
> Seeds swapped this year: 132,603. "Put all six digits in the headline," says Bramble. "Who will remember that?" asks the librarian.

**Longer:**
> The librarian spreads the Gazette's front page on the desk. "Seeds swapped this year, all along the river," they say. "Willowbank, Vadoalto, Les Saules. Wren added it up."
>
> Wren reads her total. "132,603."
>
> "Print exactly that," says Bramble. "Every digit. It's true."
>
> "It is true," says the librarian. "But a headline is for remembering. Nobody remembers six digits over breakfast."
>
> "About 130,000," says Pip. "That's easy to say."
>
> "Then put the exact number in the small print," says Bramble, "for people who care."
>
> "That," says the librarian, "is exactly what newspapers do."
>
> The magpie steals the pencil. The headline stays.

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> Round to the place that fits the job. A headline needs a number people remember. A ledger needs every digit. Both can be on the same page.

## Teach cards

Each card is under 30 words. Worked steps appear one tap at a time.

**T1. Count by one place** (A true thing about math)
> Count on in thousands and only the thousands digit changes. The hundreds, tens and ones stay still.
Worked example: 1. Start at 4,562. 2. One thousand more: 5,562. 3. One thousand less: 3,562.

**T2. When a 9 rolls over** (A true thing about math)
> If the digit you change is 9, it rolls over to 0. The next place to the left goes up by one.
Worked example: 1. 9,500 + 1000. 2. The 9 rolls over to 0. 3. A new ten-thousand appears: 10,500.

**T3. Bigger steps, same idea** (A true thing about math)
> Count in 10,000s and the ten-thousands digit changes. Count in 100,000s and the hundred-thousands digit changes.
Worked example: 1. 462,000. 2. In 10,000s: 472,000, 482,000, 492,000. 3. Roll over: 502,000.

**T4. Where it sits on the line** (A true thing about math)
> On a line from 0 to 10,000, the middle is 5,000. Use the middle and the thousands to place a number.
Worked example: 1. Find 4,900. 2. It is just before 5,000. 3. 4,100 sits much nearer 4,000.

**T5. Commas make groups** (A true thing about math)
> A comma splits digits into groups of three. Read each group, then its name: million, thousand.
Worked example: 1. 4,302,561. 2. Four million. 3. Three hundred two thousand. 4. Five hundred sixty-one.

**T6. Zeros hold places** (A true thing about math)
> A zero means "none in this place". It keeps the other digits where they belong.
Worked example: 1. 3,048. 2. Thousands: 3. Hundreds: 0. 3. Three thousand forty-eight, not three hundred forty-eight.

**T7. Compare from the left** (A true thing about math)
> Line up the places. Start at the left. The first place that differs tells you which is bigger.
Worked example: 1. 548,301 and 543,801. 2. Hundred-thousands: same. Ten-thousands: same. 3. Thousands: 8 beats 3.

**T8. The nearest post** (A true thing about math)
> To round, find the two posts on either side. Mark the halfway point. Which post is nearer?
Worked example: 1. Round 4,367 to the nearest 100. 2. Posts: 4,300 and 4,400. 3. Halfway: 4,350. 4. 4,367 is past it: 4,400.

**T9. Halfway goes up, and decide once** (A true thing about math)
> Exactly halfway rounds up. Round in one step, straight to the place asked. Never round, then round again.
Worked example: 1. 4,450 to the nearest 1000. 2. Posts: 4,000 and 5,000. 3. Halfway: 4,500. 4. 4,450 is below: 4,000.

**T10. Ten times the place to its right** (A true thing about math)
> Each place is worth ten times the place to its right. Slide a digit one place left: ten times bigger.
Worked example: 1. The 3 in 300 is worth 300. 2. Slide left: 3,000. 3. 3,000 ÷ 300 = 10.

**T11. Slide right, through the point** (A true thing about math)
> Slide a digit one place right: one tenth as big. It keeps going past the decimal point.
Worked example: 1. 4. 2. Slide right: 0.4. 3. Again: 0.04, one tenth of 0.4.

**T12. Digits move, the point stays** (A true thing about math)
> Multiply by 100 and every digit slides two places left. The decimal point stays still. Empty places fill with zeros.
Worked example: 1. 3.4 × 100. 2. The 3 slides to hundreds, the 4 to tens. 3. Fill the ones: 340.

**T13. Powers of ten** (A true thing about math)
> 10³ means three tens multiplied: 10 × 10 × 10 = 1000. The small number counts the tens.
Worked example: 1. 10² = 10 × 10 = 100. 2. 10³ = 1000. 3. 10⁴ = 10,000.

**T14. Thousandths** (A true thing about math)
> After tenths and hundredths come thousandths. Read 5.062 as "five and sixty-two thousandths".
Worked example: 1. Compare 0.45 and 0.405. 2. Write 0.45 as 0.450. 3. 450 thousandths beats 405: 0.45 is bigger.

**T15. Useful "about"** (A true thing about math)
> To estimate, round each number to its first digit or two. Work with those. Your answer is "about".
Worked example: 1. 48 sacks of 312 seeds. 2. About 50 × 300. 3. About 15,000 seeds.

**T16. A rounded number is a range** (A true thing about math)
> "4,000 to the nearest thousand" could be any count from 3,500 up to, but not including, 4,500.
Worked example: 1. Posts either side: halfway to 3,000 is 3,500. 2. Halfway to 5,000 is 4,500. 3. 4,500 itself rounds up.

**T17. Big numbers, short** (A true thing about math)
> Standard form writes one digit before the point, times a power of ten. 4,500,000 = 4.5 × 10⁶.
Worked example: 1. 4,500,000. 2. Slide the digits until 4.5 is left. 3. That took six places: 4.5 × 10⁶.

## Keeper's Desk curiosity card

**K1. Is there a biggest number?** (A true thing about math; Keeper's Desk; nothing is asked or saved)
> Counting numbers start 1, 2, 3 and never stop. Give any number, and you can add 1. So there is no biggest number.
Keeper's note:
> Mathematicians call the counting numbers the *natural numbers*. Add zero and the negatives (the Flood Post's numbers below zero) and you get the *integers*. That list never stops in either direction. "Infinity" is a name for "never stops", with no last number to reach.

## Real-world and history cards

**H1. Knots that count** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> In the Andes of South America, Inca record keepers tied knots on cords to keep counts. Where a knot sat told its place: ones, tens, hundreds, thousands.
Keeper's note:
> A khipu has a main cord with many cords hanging from it. On each hanging cord, the cluster nearest the main cord is the biggest place. The ones are farthest down. A blank space meant zero. A researcher named Leslie Leland Locke is usually credited with showing, in 1912, that these knots work in tens, like our digits. On some khipu, one cord holds the total of others. Not every khipu is numbers; scholars still study the rest.

**H2. Counting the sand** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> Over 2,000 years ago, Archimedes bounded the sand filling his model universe. A myriad meant ten thousand. He built bigger numbers.
Keeper's note:
> The Greek word for ten thousand was a *myriad*. Archimedes took a myriad of myriads (100,000,000) as a new unit and kept building. His book, *The Sand Reckoner*, was written for King Gelon of Syracuse. He proved an upper bound: the count in his model was smaller than a huge number, rather than an exact count of grains.

### Set A. Counting on and back (Sprout and Seedling)

**A1 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: One more big crate arrives. Which shelf grows?
- A. The loose seeds grow by one. *(tests: changing the wrong place)*
- B. The big crates grow by one. *(right)*
- C. Every shelf grows by one. *(tests: every digit changes)*
Hint: What arrived: a seed, a box or a crate?
Explanation: A big crate joins the big crates. The other shelves stay the same.
Feedback A: A whole crate came, not one seed. Look at the crate shelf.
Feedback B: Yes. Only the crate shelf changes.
Feedback C: Only one crate came. Did the boxes change?

**A2 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which pile has more seeds: three big crates, or nine small boxes?
- A. The three big crates. *(right)*
- B. The nine small boxes, because there are more things. *(tests: counting things, not their size)*
- C. You can't tell without counting every seed. *(tests: withholding more than the pictures require)*
Hint: Open one crate. How many boxes fit inside?
Explanation: One big crate holds ten small boxes. Three crates beat nine boxes.
Feedback A: Yes. Each crate holds more than all nine boxes.
Feedback B: More things, but smaller things. What is inside a crate?
Feedback C: The picture shows what a crate holds. That is enough to tell.

**A3 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: The ledger says 6,215. What is 1000 less?
- A. 6,115 *(tests: changing the hundreds digit)*
- B. 6,205 *(tests: confusing 1000 with 10)*
- C. 5,215 *(right)*
Hint: Which digit counts thousands?
Explanation: Only the thousands digit changes: 6 becomes 5. So 5,215.
Feedback A: That is 100 less. Find the thousands digit.
Feedback B: That is 10 less. Look for the thousands.
Feedback C: Yes. The 6 thousands became 5 thousands.

**A4 (Seedling, number)**
Prompt: 9,500 seeds, then 1000 more arrive. How many now?
Answer: 10,500.
Worked steps: 1. The thousands digit is 9. 2. 9 rolls over to 0. 3. The ten-thousands go up by one: 10,500.
Tests: 9,600 (adding 100); 0,500 or 500 (rolling over and losing the new place).
Hint: The 9 is full. Where does the extra thousand go?
Explanation: A 9 that rolls over pushes a 1 into the next place left.

**A5 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Count on in 10s: 3,980, 3,990, ... What comes next?
- A. 3,1000 *(tests: writing ten tens inside one place)*
- B. 4,000 *(right)*
- C. 3,900 *(tests: counting back by mistake)*
Hint: Nine tens and one more ten make what?
Explanation: Ten tens make a hundred, and ten hundreds make a thousand. 3,990 + 10 = 4,000.
Feedback A: Each place holds one digit. Ten tens roll over.
Feedback B: Yes. Two places rolled over at once.
Feedback C: That goes down. Counting on goes up.

**A6 (Seedling, number)**
Prompt: Count on in 10,000s: 462,000, 472,000, 482,000. Next three?
Answer: 492,000, 502,000, 512,000.
Worked steps: 1. Each step adds 1 to the ten-thousands digit. 2. 482,000 → 492,000. 3. The 9 rolls over: 502,000, then 512,000.
Tests: 493,000 (stepping by 1000); 402,000 (rolling over and losing the hundred-thousands step).
Hint: Which digit changes each time? What happens after 9?
Explanation: After 492,000 the ten-thousands roll over and the hundred-thousands become 5.

Feedback C: That is the nearest 100. The nearest 1000 is asked.

### Set D. Sliding digits (Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**D1 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: The 3 in 3,000 and the 3 in 300. How do they compare?
- A. The first is worth 10 more. *(tests: thinking of places as adding, not multiplying)*
- B. They are worth the same: both are 3. *(tests: face value only)*
- C. The first is worth 10 times as much. *(right)*
Hint: 3,000 ÷ 300 = ?
Explanation: One place left is ten times as much: 3,000 is 10 × 300.
Feedback A: 300 + 10 is 310. How many 300s make 3,000?
Feedback B: Same digit, different place. The place sets its worth.
Feedback C: Yes. Ten times, one place apart.

**D2 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: What is 3.4 × 100?
- A. 3.400 *(tests: "add zeros" on a decimal)*
- B. 340 *(right)*
- C. 34 *(tests: sliding one place instead of two)*
Hint: × 100 slides each digit how many places?
Explanation: Two places left: the 3 goes to hundreds, the 4 to tens. 340.
Feedback A: 3.400 is still 3.4. The digits have to move.
Feedback B: Yes. Digits moved two places; the point stayed.
Feedback C: That is × 10. One more slide.

**D3 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: What is 10³?
- A. 1000 *(right)*
- B. 30 *(tests: reading 10³ as 10 × 3)*
- C. 13 *(tests: reading 10³ as 10 + 3)*
Hint: The small 3 counts how many tens are multiplied.
Explanation: 10³ = 10 × 10 × 10 = 1000.
Feedback A: Yes. Three tens multiplied.
Feedback B: The small 3 means three tens multiplied, not 10 × 3.
Feedback C: It means multiplying tens, not adding 3.

**D4 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: The 4 in 0.04 and the 4 in 0.4. Which is true?
- A. 0.04 is bigger, because it is longer. *(tests: longer decimal means bigger)*
- B. 0.04 is 0.36 more than 0.4. *(tests: additive gap; also the wrong way round)*
- C. 0.04 is one tenth of 0.4. *(right)*
Hint: Slide the 4 in 0.4 one place right.
Explanation: One place right is one tenth as much: 0.4 ÷ 10 = 0.04.
Feedback A: Longer doesn't mean bigger. Compare the places.
Feedback B: 0.04 is smaller. Places differ by ten times.
Feedback C: Yes. One place right, one tenth as much.

**D5 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which is bigger: 0.372 or 0.38?
- A. 0.38, because 380 thousandths beats 372. *(right)*
- B. 0.372, because it has more digits. *(tests: longer decimal means bigger)*
- C. 0.372, because 372 is bigger than 38. *(tests: reading decimals as whole numbers)*
Hint: Write 0.38 with three decimal places.
Explanation: 0.38 = 0.380. Compare tenths first: both 3. Hundredths: 8 beats 7.
Feedback A: Yes. Same tenths, then 8 hundredths beats 7.
Feedback B: More digits doesn't mean bigger. Compare tenths, then hundredths.
Feedback C: These are thousandths and hundredths. Write 0.38 as 0.380.

**D6 (Keeper's Desk, match)**
Prompt: Match each number to its words or form.
- 5.062 → five and sixty-two thousandths
- 5.62 → five and sixty-two hundredths
- 2.56 × 10³ → 2,560
- 3.4 × 10² → 340
Tests: 5.062 matched to "five and sixty-two hundredths" (losing the 0); 2.56 × 10³ matched to 2.56000 (adding zeros).

## Key terms (glossary)

- **digit**: one of the ten signs 0 to 9.
- **place**: where a digit sits, which sets what it is worth.
- **place value**: what a digit is worth because of its place.
- **thousand**: ten hundreds, 1,000.
- **ten thousand**: ten thousands, 10,000.
- **million**: a thousand thousands, 1,000,000.
- **group (period)**: three places read together: ones, thousands, millions.
- **expanded form**: a number written as the sum of its places, like 400,000 + 3,000 + 70 + 2.
- **placeholder zero**: a 0 that holds an empty place.
- **round**: replace a number with the nearest post for the place you chose.
- **estimate**: a sensible "about" answer, found with rounded numbers.
- **power of ten**: tens multiplied together, like 10³ = 1000.
- **thousandth**: one part of a whole cut into 1000 equal parts, 0.001.
- **standard form**: one digit before the point, times a power of ten.
- **range**: every value a rounded number could have come from.
- **natural numbers**: the counting numbers 1, 2, 3, and on forever.
- **integers**: the counting numbers, zero and the negative numbers.
- **khipu**: Inca knotted cords used to keep counts and records.
- **myriad**: the Greek word for ten thousand.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** counting on and back in steps of 10 to 100,000 with roll-overs; 1000 more or less; reading, writing and comparing whole numbers to ten million using comma groups; placing four-digit numbers on a line; rounding to 10, 100, 1000 and up to the nearest million, with the halfway rule and no double rounding; estimating with rounded numbers and the range a rounded number stands for; each place worth ten times the place to its right, through the decimal point; multiplying and dividing by 10, 100 and 1000 by sliding digits; powers of ten (10³ = 1000); decimals to thousandths; standard form for large numbers.

**What it does not claim:** the town's counts are made up. Negative numbers belong to the Flood Post pack (spec 189). Standard form here covers large numbers only. Rational and irrational numbers are not taught. Written multiplication is in a separate pack (spec 191); here multiplying is only for estimating and sliding digits.

**Sources in brief:** khipu knot values via museum, encyclopedia and history-of-mathematics summaries of Leslie Leland Locke's 1912 paper and later work; *The Sand Reckoner* via encyclopedia and university physics summaries. Place-value, rounding and powers-of-ten conventions are standard school mathematics. Full list in the source audit.
