## Story scenes (A story from Willowbank)

### Act II, *the Market Fair*: The stall that wanted to fly

**Hook:** The baker's new stall has a canvas roof. The wind has opinions about it.

**Short:**
> A gust lifts the baker's stall roof. Hob grabs a pole. "Tie it down," says the orchard keeper. "Then test it before next fair."

**Longer:**
> Fair morning. The baker's new stall stands by the bridge, with a striped canvas roof. Pip has found the bun tray.
>
> A gust comes down the valley. The canvas puffs up like a sail and the whole frame hops. Hob Pennick catches a pole. The buns stay put, mostly.
>
> "It wants to lift," says the orchard keeper. "Wind pulls a roof up."
>
> "Then we tie it down," says the baker.
>
> "How much?" asks Dot. "And how will we know it's enough?"
>
> The orchard keeper points at the hedge on the far side of the green. "My grandmother planted that so the wind would slow down before it reached the orchard. Ties and a hedge. Let's try them in the workshop first."

**Keeper's note:**
> Wind over a roof can pull up on it: engineers call this uplift. Straps tying the roof to its frame, all the way to the ground, help it stay on. Every design holds only up to some limit.

### Act III, *letters up and down the river*: A letter from the coast

**Hook:** A postcard from the sea, signed only "H." Every afternoon, the wind changes its mind. Why?

**Short:**
> A postcard signed "H.": *Mornings are still. By afternoon the wind blows in off the sea. At night it turns round.* "The Sun did it," says the librarian.

**Longer:**
> The courier brings a postcard with sand stuck to it. Spiky handwriting, signed only "H."
>
> *Dear Keeper (whoever you are now),*
>
> *The sea is very large and we get on well. Something for your sky book. Mornings are still. By afternoon, every sunny day, a cool wind blows in off the sea. At night it turns round. Why?*
>
> "Maybe the sea is breathing," says Pip.
>
> The librarian smiles. "Nearly. The Sun warms the beach faster than the water. Warm air rises over the land, and cooler air from the sea slides in underneath."
>
> "And at night?"
>
> "The land cools faster too," says {guide}. "So it turns round."
>
> "Who is H.?" asks Pip. Nobody knows. Dot pins the card to the map anyway.

**Keeper's note:**
> Land warms and cools faster than water. On a sunny day, air over the land warms and rises, and cooler sea air flows in: a sea breeze. At night it can reverse. The same idea, much bigger, drives winds between the Equator and the poles.

### Act V, *a Willowbank newspaper*: The forecast that "lied"

**Hook:** The *Gazette* said seven in ten for rain. It stayed dry. Bramble is cross.

**Short:**
> Bramble waves the *Gazette*. "It said rain! It stayed dry. The forecast lied." Wren asks what "seven in ten" means.

**Longer:**
> Bramble slaps the *Willowbank Gazette* on the café table. Her magpie lands on the sugar.
>
> "Weather column," she says. "Seventy percent chance of rain. I carried an umbrella all day. Dry as toast. The forecast lied."
>
> Wren Halloway turns the paper round. "What does seventy percent mean?"
>
> "That it'll rain," says Bramble.
>
> "It means that on days like this one, it usually rains," says Wren. "About seven times in ten. So about three times in ten, it doesn't."
>
> Bramble thinks. "So yesterday was one of the three."
>
> "Maybe. One dry day can't tell you if the forecast was good. Keep a list for a month, then check."
>
> Bramble opens her notebook. Her magpie takes a sugar lump.

**Keeper's note:**
> A chance of rain says how likely rain is. Forecasters check themselves as Wren suggests: over many forecasts, does it rain on about 7 in 10 of the "70%" days? One day can't show that.

### Act VI, *the long rain*: Faster than the water

**Hook:** The map shows rain over the hills for days. Then Nuria's runner arrives. The warning chain begins.

**Short:**
> The map shows rain on the hills. Nuria's runner brings Vadoalto's warning. Willowbank gets ready, then sends the courier on to Léon.

**Longer:**
> For three days the Forecast Room flags have shown rain over the hills. The barometer stays low.
>
> Then, at dawn, Vadoalto's runner arrives, wet to the ears, with a note from Nuria: *Our post is rising. Get ready.*
>
> Nobody panics. Everybody has a job. The miller sets the flood boards. Hob moves the hay to the loft. Pip carries the sky books upstairs, then comes back for the buns.
>
> Dot writes the warning again, in French, and hands it to the courier. "For Léon. Before the water gets there."
>
> "The warning can't stop the rain," says Bramble, watching the courier go.
>
> "No," says {guide}. "It gives people time."

**Keeper's note:**
> An early warning works as a chain: measure, warn, act. It protects people and what they can move, but it can't stop the water. Britain's first storm warnings, in 1861, worked this way: telegraph messages sent to ports ahead of a storm.

## Bench (a): The forecast chain

### Teach card (True science)
> A forecast is a chain. Measure the weather in many places. Send the readings to one place. Spot the pattern. Warn people.

**Worked example** (one step per tap):
1. Vadoalto's station reads a falling barometer and rain.
2. Nuria's letter carries the readings down to Willowbank.
3. The map shows rain moving down the valley.
4. Willowbank puts the flood boards ready.

### Tool cards (True science)
- **Weather station:** measures the air where it stands, like the one by the mill.
- **Weather balloon:** carries instruments up through the sky, measuring as it rises.
- **Radar:** bounces radio waves off rain to show where it's falling.
- **Satellite:** looks down from space and sees clouds from above.
- **Computer model:** uses all the readings to work out what the air will probably do.

### Did you know? (A true thing from history, three cards, one per tap)
> In 1854 a storm took days to cross Europe and battered ships in the Black Sea. The French astronomer Urbain Le Verrier showed it could have been tracked.
>
> He set up weather stations that sent reports to Paris by telegraph. A message could outrun a storm.
>
> In 1861 Robert FitzRoy began storm warnings to British ports. On 1 August *The Times* printed his first public forecast. He called them "forecasts."

### Today, around the world (True science)
> Twice a day, at the same moment, weather balloons go up from hundreds of places around the world.

### Chance card (True science)
> "70% chance of rain" means rain is likely. On 10 days like this, it would rain on about 7.

### Further ahead (True science, Sapling and up)
> Forecasts get less sure the further ahead they look. Tomorrow's is usually more reliable than next week's.

## Bench (b): The map table (Made-up model)

Line on every screen: *A made-up map of a made-up valley. Real weather maps use these same symbols.*

### Teach card (True science)
> A weather map is a picture of the weather at one time. A key tells you what each symbol means.

### Symbol key (tap each one)
- **Sun, cloud, rain, snow pictures:** the weather expected there.
- **Number in a circle:** the temperature, in °C.
- **Arrow:** wind. A map arrow shows where the wind is going: a west wind's arrow points east. (The station's vane is the opposite: it points to where the wind comes from.)
- **H:** high pressure. Often settled, dry weather.
- **L:** low pressure. Often cloud, wind and rain.
- **Thin gray lines with numbers (isobars):** join places with the same air pressure, in hPa.
- **Blue line with triangles:** a cold front. The triangles point the way it's moving.
- **Red line with half-circles:** a warm front. The half-circles point the way it's moving.

**Worked example: read one place** (one step per tap):
1. Find Les Saules on the map.
2. Its picture shows a cloud with rain.
3. Its circle says 14. So 14 °C.
4. "Les Saules: rain, 14 °C."

### Isobar card (True science, Sapling and up)
> Where isobars crowd close together, the wind is strong. Where they spread far apart, the wind is light.

### Moving map (Sapling and up)
The rain band is three squares from Willowbank. **Next 6 hours** moves it one square. Line: *The real weather can speed up, slow down or turn. This map moves at one steady speed.*

### Pip packs for the high ford (Sprout)
> Pip is walking to the high ford tomorrow. The forecast strip shows: cold morning, rain after lunch.

The child drags items into Pip's bag: warm layers, a rain cape, a sun hat, sandals. Each item says why it helps or doesn't:
- Layers: "Layers trap Pip's own warmth. He can take one off if he gets hot."
- Rain cape: "Keeps the rain off his fur."
- Sun hat: "Useful on a sunny day. Is tomorrow sunny?"
- Sandals: "Cold, wet toes at the ford. Try boots."

## Bench (c): The Sun's engine

### Teach card (True science)
> The Sun warms the Earth unevenly. Land warms faster than water. Warm air rises, and cooler air flows in. That moving air is wind.

### The trays (Made-up model)
Two trays in sunshine for one hour: one of sand, one of water. Both start at 20 °C. Tap **One hour of Sun**. The sand reads 30 °C, the water 22 °C. Line: *Made-up readings that show a real pattern.*

**Worked example** (one step per tap):
1. Sand: 20 °C to 30 °C. That's 10 degrees warmer.
2. Water: 20 °C to 22 °C. That's 2 degrees warmer.
3. Same Sun, same hour. The sand warmed five times as much.

### Sea breeze picture (True science)
> Day: the beach is warmer. Air over it rises. Cool sea air slides in. Night: the land cools first, and the breeze turns round.

### Pressure card (True science, Sapling and up)
> Warm, rising air leaves lower pressure underneath. Air flows from high pressure toward low pressure. That flow is wind.

### The flashlight (Keeper's Desk) (Made-up model, two cards)
> Shine a flashlight straight down: it lights one square. Tilt it: the same light spreads over two squares. Each square gets half.
>
> Near the Equator, sunlight comes in nearly straight. Near the poles it comes in low and spreads out.

Line: *A simplified picture. Real sunlight also depends on season and cloud.*

## Bench (d): Storm day, calmly

A storm over the far hills, seen from inside the café. Ember and Puff watch from the window seat with cocoa. The weather service's warning is pinned by the door.

**Built on:** the spark (spec 163's Switch Table) and the flash-to-boom count (spec 146's Thunder counter), both linked. This bench adds warnings: who sends them, the colors, who decides.

### Thunder card (True science, Sprout)
> Thunder is loud. But thunder is only a sound. It can't touch you.

### Who decides? card (all depths)
> Weather services watch storms and send warnings. Grown-ups use those warnings to decide what everyone does.

### Warning colors (True science, Seedling and up)
> France and Spain both use four warning colors: green, yellow, orange, red. Red is the strongest.

### How rare? card (True science, Sapling and up)
> Spain's weather service sets each place's warning levels partly by how rare that weather is there. Red is kept for the rarest.

### For grown-ups: storm safety
> Storm-safety advice belongs to your local weather service, not to this game: Météo-France (vigilance map), AEMET (Meteoalerta), the UK Met Office, the US National Weather Service, or your own country's service. The US National Weather Service says that if you can hear thunder, you are probably within striking distance and should get to a safe place. The game never tells a child where to go in a storm.

## Bench (e): Building for weather (Made-up model)

Line on every result: *A made-up model with simple rules. Real buildings are tested by engineers.*

### Teach card (True science)
> Each hazard needs its own fix. Straps fight wind lifting a roof. A lightning rod gives lightning a safe path down.

**Worked example: match a fix to a hazard** (one step per tap):
1. Hazard: wind lifting the stall roof.
2. What pulls up? The wind.
3. What holds down? Straps tied to the frame.
4. So: straps for wind.

### Fix cards
- **Roof straps:** tie the roof down to the frame. For wind.
- **Hedge windbreak:** slows the wind before it arrives. For wind. Takes years to grow.
- **Gutter and raised boards:** carry rain away and keep goods off wet ground. For heavy rain.
- **Lightning rod:** a metal rod joined to the ground. For lightning on tall buildings.
- **A warning:** a forecast or a runner. For people: it gives them time.

### A true thing from history
> Around 1750, Benjamin Franklin suggested pointed iron rods on rooftops, joined to the ground. In 1753 he printed instructions for putting them up.

### The stall test (Made-up model)
The stall roof holds a **gust number** of 2 on its own. Each pair of straps adds 1 (at most 2 pairs). The grown hedge adds 1. The gust dial goes from 1 to 5. If the gust is the same as the hold number or lower, the roof holds. If it's higher, the roof lifts. Budget: 4 coins. Straps 1 coin a pair, hedge 2, gutter 2.

**Result lines:** Holds: "The roof held at gust {n}. Try the dial higher." Lifts: "The roof lifted at gust {n}. It holds up to gust {hold}." Every result: "Every design has a limit. Find this one's."

- Hint: What did the test actually show?
- I'm stuck: Dot's bag held 10 letters. Does that prove it holds 100? Say only what you tested.
- Explanation: A test shows what happened in that test. A design reduces harm up to a limit; it never makes a stall storm-proof.

**E5 (Keeper's Desk, say why, read by a grown-up).** Nuria's warning didn't stop any rain. How did it still help?
- Said aloud to a grown-up. Nothing is saved.
- Points a grown-up might listen for: it gave time; people moved hay, flour and books; the flood boards went up; the warning went on to Les Saules; a warning protects people and things they can move, not buildings.
- Hint: Think about what people did with the extra time.
- I'm stuck: Dot's "rain later" note let Pip pack a cape. The rain still came. What did the note change?
- Explanation: A warning can't stop weather. It gives people time to act, which is why it's a real kind of protection.

## Key terms

| Term | Child-friendly definition |
| --- | --- |
| forecast | What the weather will probably do next. |
| radar | A tool that bounces radio waves off rain to find it. |
| satellite | A machine in space. Weather satellites look down at clouds. |
| computer model | A program that works out what the air will probably do next. |
| chance | How likely something is. 70% chance means about 7 times in 10. |
| weather map | A picture of the weather at one time, with a key. |
| high pressure (H) | Air pressing down harder. Often settled weather. |
| low pressure (L) | Air pressing down less. Often cloud and rain. |
| isobar | A line joining places with the same air pressure. |
| front | Where warmer air and colder air meet. Fronts often bring rain. |
| sea breeze | A wind blowing in from the sea on a sunny day. |
| thunder | The sound lightning makes. |
| hazard | Weather or nature that can cause harm, like strong wind. |
| uplift | Wind pulling up on a roof. |
| lightning rod | A metal rod, joined to the ground, that gives lightning a safe path. |
| warning | A message that gives people time to get ready. |
| limit | The most a design can hold before it fails. |

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** how a forecast is made and what its tools do; what a chance of rain means; reading a weather map's key, highs, lows, fronts and isobars; how uneven heating by the Sun makes wind; that thunder is only a sound and that weather services send warnings for grown-ups to act on; and choosing and judging designs against weather hazards, each with a limit.

**What it does not claim:** the valley map, the trays, the stall model and all their numbers are made up. The map moves weather at a steady speed, which real weather does not. The game gives no storm-safety rules of its own and no forecast for any real place: the storm-safety card points to your local weather service. Lightning's cause (spec 163) and the flash-to-boom count (spec 146) are linked, not taught; hurricanes and climate zones are left out.

**Sources in brief:** Met Office archive, Paris Observatory, NOAA and the US National Weather Service, NASA Earth Observatory, Météo-France, AEMET, Founders Online and UF/IFAS Extension. Full list in the source audit.

**Off-screen, adult-led:** On a sunny day, set a tray of dry sand or soil and a tray of water side by side in the sun, each with a thermometer, and read both after an hour. Use plastic trays and an outdoor thermometer, not glass; an adult handles any water near little ones. The game records nothing from this.

**Off-screen, anyone:** Read your national weather service's forecast together each morning for a week and see how often it matched.
