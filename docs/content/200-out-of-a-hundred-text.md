# Out of a Hundred: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 200](../specs/200-out-of-a-hundred.md). Use as written, after independent spec review and a math check. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion and is never gendered. Pip is he/him, Dot she/her, Bramble she/her (with her magpie), the librarian they/them, the orchard keeper she/her, the baker he/him, Ember the pocket-dragon. Real-world facts are in the [source audit](../source-audits/200-out-of-a-hundred.md); items still marked `[check]` stay off screen until cleared. Nothing here is scored or timed.

## Labels used on every card

- **A story from Willowbank**: the town, its Gazette, orchard, prices, fields and every character are made up.
- **A true thing about math**: checked math, explained simply.
- **A true thing from history**: something people really did or wrote.
- **A made-up model**: a simple game rule, shown on the card, that is not real measurement.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. Fifty percent of what? (Act IV, chapter 3: the newspaper's two explanations)

**Hook:** The librarian adds a numbers corner to the Gazette. Pip thinks 50% means 50 apples.

**Short:**
> The Gazette has a new corner: *Say it out of a hundred.* Pip reads "50% off apples" and asks for fifty apples.

**Longer:**
> The librarian pins a new sign over the Gazette's last column. *The Numbers Corner. Say it out of a hundred.*
>
> Pip reads the café's ad out loud. "Fifty percent off apples! Good. I'll have fifty apples."
>
> "Fifty percent is half," says the librarian. "Half of the price. Half of what you buy. Never fifty of anything."
>
> "Half of what, then?"
>
> "That's the question," they say. They chalk it on the wall in big letters: *Percent of what?*
>
> Dot looks at the sign. "Per cent. For each hundred."
>
> "So fifty for each hundred," says Pip slowly. "And if there are only ten apples?"
>
> "Then fifty percent is five."
>
> Pip looks at the basket. It has ten apples. He takes five and does not argue.

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> A percent is a share of a whole, measured out of every hundred. The same percent of a bigger whole is a bigger amount. Always ask: percent of what?

### Scene 2. Forty percent of the low fields (Act IV, chapter 4: the proposal for the low fields)

**Hook:** At the meeting, a line on the board says the Sponge plan "would flood 40% of the low fields." Bramble hears "nearly half the orchard."

**Short:**
> "Forty percent!" says Bramble. "Nearly half the orchard!" The orchard keeper counts. "Forty percent of the low fields. That's different."

**Longer:**
> The Sponge plan has a line in the librarian's best pen: *The marsh would flood 40% of the low fields.*
>
> Bramble jumps up. "Forty percent! That's nearly half the orchard under water!"
>
> The orchard keeper unrolls her map. "The orchard has eighty fields. Thirty of them are low fields. Forty percent of thirty is twelve."
>
> "Twelve fields," says {guide}. "Out of eighty in the whole orchard."
>
> The librarian works it on the board. "Twelve out of eighty. That's fifteen percent of the orchard."
>
> Bramble folds her arms. "Fifteen percent is still twelve fields of somebody's trees."
>
> "That's true," says the orchard keeper quietly. "They're mine."
>
> Nobody shouts after that. Both numbers go on the board, side by side, with what each one is a percent **of**.

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> Forty percent and fifteen percent describe the same twelve fields. They use different wholes. An honest claim says which whole it means. Neither number says which plan is best. The fields and plan are made up.

### Scene 3. More than a whole harvest (Act IV, chapter 2: a puzzling change at the bottom of the orchard)

**Hook:** The orchard keeper's harvest board says this year is 130% of last year. Ember thinks that is impossible.

**Short:**
> The harvest board says *130% of last year*. "You can't have more than all of it," says Ember. The orchard keeper smiles.

**Longer:**
> The orchard keeper chalks the harvest on the barn board. *Last year: 400 baskets. This year: 520 baskets.*
>
> Underneath, she writes: *130% of last year.*
>
> Ember flutters up to the board and taps it with a claw. "That can't be right. One hundred percent is everything. You can't have more than everything."
>
> "One hundred percent of **last year** is all of last year," says the orchard keeper. "Four hundred baskets. This year I got all of that, and more."
>
> {guide} draws one full square of a hundred, then a second square with thirty more shaded. "All of last year, and thirty percent again."
>
> Ember looks at the two squares for a long time. "So a hundred percent is just the whole you started with."
>
> "And you can go past it," says the orchard keeper. "In a good year."
>
> She does not say why the low corner gave less. Nobody knows that yet.

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> 520 out of 400 is 130 out of 100: 130%. The extra 120 baskets are 30% of last year. The harvest numbers are made up.

### Scene 4. The cider price that didn't come back (Act V, path 2: the Willowbank Gazette)

**Hook:** The baker raised the cider price by 10% in autumn and lowered it by 10% in winter. He is sure it is back where it started.

**Short:**
> Up ten percent, then down ten percent. "Back where it started," says the baker. The Gazette's numbers corner says: not quite.

**Longer:**
> The baker pins a note on the Gazette board. *Cider: up 10% for the harvest supper. Now down 10% again. Back to the old price!*
>
> The librarian frowns at it and fetches a pencil.
>
> "The old price was two dollars," they say. "Ten percent of two dollars is twenty cents. So it went up to two dollars twenty."
>
> "Then down ten percent," says the baker. "Twenty cents off. Two dollars."
>
> "Ten percent of two dollars twenty," says the librarian. "That's twenty-two cents. So the new price is one dollar ninety-eight."
>
> The baker stares at the note. "Two cents! Where did they go?"
>
> "The second ten percent was a percent of a bigger price," says {guide}.
>
> The baker crosses out *Back to the old price!* and writes *Two cents cheaper. Come quickly.* The Gazette prints it in the numbers corner.

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> Up 10% is × 1.1. Down 10% is × 0.9. Together, × 0.99. Each percent is a percent of the price at that moment. Prices are made up.

## Teach cards

Each card is under 30 words. Worked steps appear one tap at a time.

**T1. Out of every hundred** (A true thing about math)
> Percent means "out of every hundred". The sign % says percent. 40% means 40 out of every 100.
Worked example: 1. A square of 100 little squares. 2. Shade 40 of them. 3. That is 40%.

**T2. The whole is 100%** (A true thing about math)
> The whole thing is 100%. Half of it is 50%. None of it is 0%.
Worked example: 1. All 100 squares shaded: 100%. 2. Half the rows shaded: 50 squares, 50%. 3. Nothing shaded: 0%.

**T3. More than the whole** (A true thing about math)
> 100% is the whole you started with. You can go past it. 150% is one whole and half again.
Worked example: 1. Last year: 40 baskets. 2. 100% is 40, and 50% is 20. 3. 150% is 40 + 20 = 60 baskets.

**T4. Three names for one amount** (A true thing about math)
> One amount can have three names: a fraction, a decimal and a percent. 25 out of 100 is 25/100, 0.25 and 25%.
Worked example: 1. Shade 25 squares. 2. Fraction: 25/100, which is 1/4. 3. Decimal: 0.25. 4. Percent: 25%.

**T5. Small percents keep their zero** (A true thing about math)
> 9% is 9 hundredths. As a decimal that is 0.09. The zero holds the tenths place.
Worked example: 1. 9% = 9/100. 2. 9 hundredths: 0.09. 3. 0.9 would be 90%, ten times more.

**T6. Simplest form** (A true thing about math)
> Write the percent out of 100. Then divide top and bottom by the same number until you can't.
Worked example: 1. 40% = 40/100. 2. Divide both by 20. 3. 40% = 2/5.

**T7. Words for shares** (A true thing about math)
> A ratio compares part to part. A proportion compares a part to the whole. A percent is a proportion out of 100.
Worked example: 1. 1 cup juice to 4 cups water: ratio 1 : 4. 2. Whole: 5 cups. 3. Juice: 1 out of 5, 20%.

**T8. Percent of what?** (A true thing about math)
> A percent is always a percent of something. The same percent of a bigger whole is a bigger amount.
Worked example: 1. 50% of 20 apples: 10. 2. 50% of 200 apples: 100. 3. Same percent, different whole.

**T9. Build from 10% and 1%** (A true thing about math)
> 10% is the whole ÷ 10. 1% is the whole ÷ 100. 5% is half of 10%. Add pieces to build others.
Worked example: 1. 10% of 360 is 36. 2. 5% is half: 18. 3. 15% is 36 + 18 = 54.

**T10. Pick the handiest name** (A true thing about math)
> Choose the name that makes the work easy. 75% of 80? Think 3/4. Divide by 4, then times 3.
Worked example: 1. 80 ÷ 4 = 20. 2. 20 × 3 = 60. 3. Also: 25 × 16 = 25 × 4 × 4 = 100 × 4 = 400.

**T11. One amount as a percent of another** (A true thing about math)
> To say A as a percent of B, write A out of B. Then make it out of 100.
Worked example: 1. 12 fields out of 30. 2. 12/30 = 4/10. 3. 4/10 = 40/100 = 40%.

**T12. Going up by a percent** (A true thing about math)
> Up 10% means the whole plus 10% more. That is 110%, so times 1.1.
Worked example: 1. Cider: $2.00. 2. 10% of $2.00 is $0.20. 3. $2.00 + $0.20 = $2.20. Same as $2.00 × 1.1.

**T13. Going down by a percent** (A true thing about math)
> 25% off means 25% comes off and 75% is left. The discount and the new price are different numbers.
Worked example: 1. Ladder: $40. 2. Discount: 25% of $40 = $10. 3. New price: $40 − $10 = $30.

**T14. Up then down is not a round trip** (A true thing about math)
> Up 10% then down 10% doesn't get back. The second 10% is a percent of the new, bigger amount.
Worked example: 1. $2.00 up 10%: $2.20. 2. 10% of $2.20 is $0.22. 3. $2.20 − $0.22 = $1.98.

**T15. Back to the price before** (A true thing about math)
> 20% off leaves 80%: times 0.8. To find the old price, undo it: divide by 0.8.
Worked example: 1. Sale price $64. 2. $64 is 80% of the old price. 3. $64 ÷ 0.8 = $80.

**T16. Simple interest** (A true thing about math)
> Simple interest pays the same percent of the first amount every year. The percent never grows on itself.
Worked example: 1. $500 saved at 3% each year. 2. 3% of $500 = $15. 3. Four years: 4 × $15 = $60.

**T17. Read a claim honestly** (A true thing about math)
> Ask three things. Percent of what? How many is that? Was the starting number small?
Worked example: 1. "Sales up 50%!" 2. From 4 baskets to 6. 3. True, and only 2 more baskets.

## Real-world and history cards

**H1. One part in a hundred, in Rome** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> In ancient Rome, goods sold at public auction paid a tax of one part in every hundred. It was called the *centesima*, "the hundredth".
Keeper's note:
> The emperor Augustus used this tax's money to pay soldiers when they finished their service. Later, under the emperor Tiberius, the rate was cut to half of one percent: one part in every two hundred. A sale of 300 coins paid 3 coins at the first rate.

**H2. How % got its shape** (A true thing from history; Seedling and up)
> Italian merchants wrote *per cento*, "for each hundred". Writers shortened it. Over hundreds of years the short form slowly turned into the sign %.
Keeper's note:
> In an Italian arithmetic book from the 1400s, a writer shortened *per cento* to a p and a c with a little loop. Later writers kept squeezing it. The loop and the letters became the two small circles and the slanted line we write today.

## Problem sets

Eighteen three-choice items put the right answer at A six times, B six times and C six times.

### Set A. Out of every hundred (Sprout and Seedling)

**A1 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which square is half shaded?
- A. One row shaded, the rest empty. *(tests: any shading counts as half)*
- B. Half the rows shaded. *(right)*
- C. Every little square shaded. *(tests: half confused with all)*
Hint: Half means the shaded part and the empty part match.
Explanation: Half the rows shaded, half empty. That is half of the square.
Feedback A: One row is a small part. Is the empty part the same size?
Feedback B: Yes. Shaded and empty match.
Feedback C: That is all of it. Half leaves the same amount empty.

**A2 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Half the big basket or half the small basket. Which is more apples?
- A. Half the big basket. *(right)*
- B. The same. They are both half. *(tests: a share as a fixed amount)*
- C. You can never tell with baskets. *(tests: withholding when the picture shows both)*
Hint: Look at how big each whole basket is.
Explanation: Half of a bigger whole is more. Both are half; the wholes differ.
Feedback A: Yes. Half of more is more.
Feedback B: Both are half. Look at the wholes. Which half holds more?
Feedback C: The picture shows both baskets. You can see which is bigger.

**A3 (Sprout, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which picture shows more than all of one square?
- A. One square, all shaded. *(tests: all is the most there can be)*
- B. One square, nearly all shaded. *(tests: nearly read as more)*
- C. One full square and part of a second. *(right)*
Hint: All of one square is the whole. Is there more?
Explanation: One whole square and some more is more than the whole.
Feedback A: That is all of one square. Is anything extra?
Feedback B: Nearly all is a bit less than all.
Feedback C: Yes. A whole and some more.

**A4 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: 40 of the 100 squares are shaded. What percent is shaded?
- A. 40% *(right)*
- B. 60% *(tests: counting the empty squares)*
- C. 4% *(tests: counting rows as the percent)*
Hint: Percent means out of 100. How many of the 100 are shaded?
Explanation: 40 out of 100 is 40%.
Feedback A: Yes. 40 out of 100.
Feedback B: 60 squares are empty. The question asks about the shaded ones.
Feedback C: 4 rows of 10 is 40 squares. Count squares, not rows.

**A5 (Seedling, shade)**
Prompt: Shade 25% of the square.
Answer: 25 little squares (2 rows and 5 more).
Worked steps: 1. 25% is 25 out of 100. 2. Two rows make 20. 3. Five more make 25.
Tests: 4 squares (reading 25% as "1 in 25": 100 ÷ 25); 75 squares (shading what is left).
Hint: How many of the 100 squares is 25%?
Explanation: 25% is 25 squares. That is also one quarter of the square.

**A6 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: The apples filled the bin once, then half again. What percent of the bin?
- A. 100%. A bin can't hold more. *(tests: 100% as a ceiling)*
- B. 50%, the extra half. *(tests: counting only the extra)*
- C. 150% *(right)*
Hint: One full bin is 100%. Half a bin is 50%.
Explanation: 100% and 50% more make 150%.
Feedback A: The bin's worth is 100%. They picked more than that.
Feedback B: That's the extra part. Add the first full bin.
Feedback C: Yes. One whole and half again.

### Set C. The Harvest Board: a percent of an amount (Seedling and Sapling)

**C1 (Seedling, number)**
Prompt: 10% of 400 baskets. How many?

Tests: 4 (that is 1%); 390 (taking away 10).

- C. 3 apples *(tests: dividing by 20)*

Feedback A: 20% depends on the whole. 20% of 60 isn't 20.

- B. $10 *(tests: giving the discount as the price)*

Feedback A: 25% of $40 isn't $25. Find a quarter of $40.
Feedback B: $10 comes off. What is left to pay?
Feedback C: Yes. $10 off, $30 to pay.

**C5 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: 36% of 400 baskets. Build it from 10% and 1%.
Answer: 144 baskets.
Worked steps: 1. 10% is 40, so 30% is 120. 2. 1% is 4, so 6% is 24. 3. 120 + 24 = 144.
Tests: 36 (the percent as a count); 1440 (a place slip).

Explanation: 120 + 24 = 144. Check: a bit more than a third of 400.

**C6 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Which is more apples: 10% of 500, or 80% of 60?
- A. 80% of 60, because 80% is bigger. *(tests: a bigger percent always means more)*
- B. You can't tell without more facts. *(tests: withholding when both wholes are given)*
- C. 10% of 500. *(right)*
Hint: Work out both amounts.
Explanation: 10% of 500 is 50. 80% of 60 is 48. 50 is more.
Feedback A: Work it out: 80% of 60 is 48. 10% of 500?
Feedback B: Both wholes are given. You can find both amounts.
Feedback C: Yes. 50 beats 48. The whole matters.

### Set D. One amount as a percent of another (Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**D1 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: 12 of the 30 low fields would flood. What percent is that?
Answer: 40%.
Worked steps: 1. 12 out of 30: 12/30. 2. 12/30 = 4/10. 3. 4/10 = 40/100 = 40%.
Tests: 12% (the count as the percent); 250% (30 out of 12, the wrong whole).
Hint: Which number is the whole? Write the part out of it.
Explanation: 12 out of 30 is the same share as 40 out of 100.

**D2 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Pip kept 18 of 25 apples. Dot kept 70 of 100. Bigger share?
- A. Dot, because 70 is more than 18. *(tests: comparing counts with different wholes)*
- B. Pip: 72% is more than 70%. *(right)*
- C. You can't compare different totals. *(tests: withholding; percents make them comparable)*
Hint: Make Pip's share out of 100.
Explanation: 18/25 = 72/100. 72% beats 70%.
Feedback A: Dot has more apples. Who kept the bigger share of their own?
Feedback B: Yes. Out of 100 makes them fair to compare.
Feedback C: Percents are made for this. Turn both into out of 100.

**D3 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: Last year 400 baskets. This year 520. This year as a percent of last year?
Answer: 130%.
Worked steps: 1. 520 out of 400: 520/400. 2. Divide top and bottom by 4: 130/100. 3. 130%.
Tests: 30% (only the extra baskets); about 77% (400 out of 520, the wrong whole).
Hint: Last year is the whole. Write this year out of it.
Explanation: 520 is all of 400 and 30% more: 130%.

**D4 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: 12 fields would flood. The orchard has 80 fields. What percent of the orchard?
Answer: 15%.
Worked steps: 1. 12 out of 80: 12/80. 2. Divide by 4: 3/20. 3. 3/20 = 15/100 = 15%.
Tests: 40% (using the 30 low fields as the whole); 12% (the count as the percent).
Hint: This time the whole is the orchard.
Explanation: Same 12 fields, bigger whole, smaller percent: 15%.

**D5 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: Write 12.5% as a fraction in simplest form and as a decimal.
Answer: 1/8 and 0.125.
Worked steps: 1. 25% is 1/4. 2. 12.5% is half of that: 1/8. 3. 1 ÷ 8 = 0.125.
Tests: 1.25 (a place slip); 1/12.5 (the percent number as the bottom).
Hint: 12.5 is half of 25.
Explanation: Half of a quarter is an eighth: 12.5% = 1/8 = 0.125.

**D6 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Red to green apples is 3 : 2. What percent are red?
- A. 60% *(right)*
- B. 150%, because 3 ÷ 2 = 1.5. *(tests: reading part to part as part to whole)*
- C. 40% *(tests: giving the green share)*
Hint: Count the whole first: 3 + 2.
Explanation: Red is 3 out of 5. 3/5 = 60/100 = 60%.
Feedback A: Yes. 3 out of 5 is 60%.
Feedback B: 3 : 2 compares red with green. A percent needs the whole.
Feedback C: 40% is the green share, 2 out of 5.

### Set E. Going up and down (Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**E1 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: Cider costs $2.00. The price goes up 10%. New price?
Answer: $2.20.
Worked steps: 1. 10% of $2.00 is $0.20. 2. $2.00 + $0.20 = $2.20.
Tests: $2.10 (adding 10 cents); $0.20 (giving only the increase).
Hint: Find 10% of $2.00 first.
Explanation: The new price is the old price plus 10% of it.

**E2 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: A price goes up 15%. What do you multiply by?
- A. × 1.5 *(tests: 15% written as 0.5)*
- B. × 0.15 *(tests: finding only the increase)*
- C. × 1.15 *(right)*
Hint: Up 15% means 100% plus 15%.
Explanation: 115% is 1.15. Times 1.15 gives the new price.
Feedback A: × 1.5 is up 50%. 15% is 0.15.
Feedback B: × 0.15 finds the increase. Add the old price too.
Feedback C: Yes. 115% is 1.15.

**E3 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: $2.00 goes up 10%, then down 10%. Final price?
Answer: $1.98.
Worked steps: 1. Up 10%: $2.20. 2. 10% of $2.20 is $0.22. 3. $2.20 − $0.22 = $1.98.
Tests: $2.00 (thinking the changes cancel); $2.20 (stopping after one change).
Hint: The second 10% is 10% of $2.20.
Explanation: × 1.1 then × 0.9 is × 0.99. It ends two cents lower.

**E4 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: After 20% off, a ladder costs $64. What did it cost before?
Answer: $80.
Worked steps: 1. 20% off leaves 80%. 2. $64 is 80% of the old price. 3. $64 ÷ 0.8 = $80.
Tests: $76.80 (adding 20% of $64); $84 (adding 20 dollars).
Hint: $64 is which percent of the old price?
Explanation: Check: 20% of $80 is $16, and $80 − $16 = $64.

**E5 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: Dot's box pays 3% simple interest on $500. Interest after 4 years?
Answer: $60.
Worked steps: 1. 3% of $500 is $15. 2. Simple interest: $15 each year. 3. 4 × $15 = $60.
Tests: $15 (one year only); about $62.75 (interest on interest, which simple interest doesn't pay).
Hint: Find one year's interest first.
Explanation: Simple interest is the same $15 every year, from the first $500.

**E6 (Keeper's Desk, say why; spoken to a grown-up, not stored)**
Prompt: Why doesn't up 10% then down 10% get back to the start?
Model answer for the grown-up: The second 10% is taken from a bigger amount, so more comes off than went on. Together it is × 0.99.
Tests: percents add and cancel like plain numbers.
Hint: 10% of which price, each time?
Explanation: Each percent is a percent of the amount at that moment.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **percent (%)**: out of every hundred.
- **whole**: the full amount a percent is taken from. It is 100%.
- **fraction**: a part of a whole, written like 1/4.
- **decimal**: a number with a point, like 0.25.
- **equivalent**: different names for the same amount.
- **convert**: change to another name for the same amount.
- **simplest form**: a fraction that can't be divided down any more.
- **proportion**: a part compared with the whole.
- **ratio**: one part compared with another part.
- **discount**: how much comes off a price.
- **increase / decrease**: going up / going down.
- **multiplier**: the number you multiply by to make a change, like 1.1 for up 10%.
- **simple interest**: the same percent of the first amount, paid each year.
- **percentage point**: one step on the percent scale, like 10% to 11%.
