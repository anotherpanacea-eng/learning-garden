Companion to [spec 206](../specs/206-surveyors-triangle.md). Use as written, after independent spec review and a math check. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion and is never gendered; `{courier}` is the explorer's courier. Pip is he/him, Dot she/her, Bramble she/her (with her magpie), Wren Halloway she/her, Hob Pennick he/him, the librarian they/them, Hollis they/them (named only in the Act IV and Act VI scenes). ## Labels used on every card

- **A story from Willowbank**: the town, its banks, trees, paths and every character are made up.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. A bigger map for the wall (Act IV, chapter 1: the old map and Hollis's account)

**Hook:** Hollis's old map is small. Wren wants a copy big enough for the whole library to see.

**Short:**
> Hollis's map is 10 cm by 15 cm. Wren wants it three times bigger. Pip adds 20 cm to each side. The map looks squashed.

**Longer:**
> Hollis's old map is no bigger than a postcard: 10 centimeters by 15. Wren Halloway pins a big sheet on the library wall.
>
> "Three times bigger," she says. "So everyone can see the Sponge."
>
> Pip measures. "The short side goes from 10 to 30. That's 20 more. So I add 20 to the long side too. Thirty-five."
>
> He draws it. The brook looks fat. The marsh looks squashed. The ford has slid toward the mill.
>
> "That isn't Hollis's map any more," says Bramble. "It's a cousin."
>
> "Three times bigger means three times every length," says {guide}. "Not twenty more."
>
> Pip draws again: 30 by 45. The brook bends just where it should.
>
> Wren nods. "Same shape. Bigger. That's a copy you can trust."

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> Pip's first copy added 20 to each side. 30 ÷ 10 is 3, but 35 ÷ 15 is only about 2.3. The sides grew by different amounts of times, so the shape changed. A true copy uses one scale factor for every length.

### Scene 2. How high are the banks? (Act IV, chapter 4: the proposal for the low fields)

**Hook:** The Wall plan needs to know how high the old brook wall is, and how tall Hob's poplar stands. Nobody wants to climb.

**Short:**
> The meeting needs heights. Hob won't climb his poplar. Wren brings a stick, a tape and a clinometer from the workshop.

**Longer:**
> "How high is the old wall now?" asks the miller. "And how high would a new one be?"
>
> Nobody knows. Hob Pennick knows his poplar by the ford is "tall". He will not climb it to be more exact.
>
> Wren Halloway brings a stick, a tape and a straw taped to a protractor. "We measure on the ground," she says, "and let the triangles climb."
>
> On a sunny morning, Pip stands a one-meter stick by the poplar. Its shadow is two meters long. The poplar's shadow is eighteen.
>
> "Every shadow is twice as long as its thing," says Wren. "So the poplar is nine meters."
>
> On a cloudy afternoon there are no shadows. Bramble looks up the straw at the top of the old wall. "Twenty degrees," she reads.
>
> Wren opens a small book of numbers. "Then I can tell you the height."

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> Bramble stood 5 m from the wall. The table says the height rises 0.36 m for each meter across at 20°. 5 × 0.36 = 1.8 m above her eye. Her eye is 1.2 m up. 1.8 + 1.2 = 3 m. Real walls lean and real ground slopes; this one is made up.

### Scene 3. As the crow flies (Act VI, chapter 2: compare routes and keep a plan)

**Hook:** Dot's route plan needs the straight distance to the high ford and how wide the brook is there.

**Short:**
> The river path to the ford is 6 km. "And straight across?" asks Dot. The map says 4 km east, 3 km north.

**Longer:**
> Dot spreads the post office map under the honey jars. "The high ford," she says. "Six kilometers by the river path. But a crow flies straight. How far is that?"
>
> On the map grid, the ford is 4 kilometers east of the post office and 3 kilometers north.
>
> "Seven," says Pip. "Four and three."
>
> "That's walking east, then turning north," says {guide}. "The crow cuts the corner."
>
> Bramble draws squares on each side of the triangle. Sixteen and nine make twenty-five. "Five kilometers," she says. "The path wiggles one extra."
>
> Then {courier} asks how wide the brook is at the ford. Hob Pennick stands straight across from a post on the far side. He paces 20 meters along his bank, lays the clinometer flat like a protractor, and sights the post. "Thirty-five degrees," he says.
>
> Wren checks her table. "Fourteen meters. Wide, but you can wade it in summer."

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> 4 × 4 = 16 and 3 × 3 = 9. 16 + 9 = 25, and 5 × 5 = 25. For the brook: 20 m × 0.70 = 14 m. Real maps and real brooks are messier; these numbers are made up.

## Teach cards

Each card is under 30 words. Worked steps appear one tap at a time.

**T1. Same shape, new size** (A true thing about math)
> Similar shapes are the same shape at a different size. Every length is multiplied by the same number. Every angle stays the same.
Worked example: 1. A card is 2 by 3. 2. Multiply each side by 2. 3. The copy is 4 by 6, with the same square corners.

**T2. Find the scale factor first** (A true thing about math)
> The scale factor is new ÷ old, for one matching pair of sides. Then multiply every other side by it.
Worked example: 1. Short side: 10 cm becomes 30 cm. 2. 30 ÷ 10 = 3. 3. Long side: 15 × 3 = 45 cm.

**T3. Adding stretches the shape** (A true thing about math)
> Adding the same amount to each side usually changes the shape. Check by dividing each new side by its old side.
Worked example: 1. A 2 by 5 card, add 4: 6 by 9. 2. 6 ÷ 2 = 3, but 9 ÷ 5 = 1.8. 3. Different, so not similar. Times 3 gives 6 by 15.

**T4. Shrinking is scaling too** (A true thing about math)
> A smaller copy has a scale factor less than 1. You can divide every side by the same number instead.
Worked example: 1. A 12 by 9 picture. 2. One third the size: divide by 3. 3. The copy is 4 by 3.

**T5. Area grows faster** (A true thing about math)
> Double every length and the area is four times as big. Area grows by the scale factor times itself.
Worked example: 1. A 2 by 3 card has area 6. 2. Doubled: 4 by 6, area 24. 3. Four copies fit inside: 2 × 2 = 4.

**T6. Grow from a point** (A true thing about math)
> To enlarge from a center, multiply each corner's distance from the center by the scale factor. Count across and up from the center.
Worked example: 1. Center (1, 1). Corner (2, 2) is 1 across, 1 up. 2. Scale factor 2: 2 across, 2 up. 3. New corner: (3, 3).

**T7. Shadows at the same time** (A true thing about math)
> Sunlight arrives in parallel lines. At one moment, every upright thing and its shadow make the same-shaped triangle.
Worked example: 1. Stick: 1 m tall, 2 m shadow. 2. Each shadow is twice its height. 3. Poplar shadow 18 m, so 18 ÷ 2 = 9 m tall.

**T8. Find the hypotenuse first** (A true thing about math)
> In a right triangle, the hypotenuse is the side across from the right angle. It is always the longest side.
Worked example: 1. Find the square corner mark. 2. Look straight across it. 3. That side is the hypotenuse, whichever way the triangle is turned.

**T9. Squares on the sides** (A true thing about math)
> Draw a square on each side of a right triangle. The two small squares together fill the big one. This is Pythagoras' theorem.
Worked example: 1. Sides 3 and 4. 2. 3 × 3 = 9 and 4 × 4 = 16. 3. 9 + 16 = 25. 4. 5 × 5 = 25, so the hypotenuse is 5.

**T10. Finding a shorter side** (A true thing about math)
> If the hypotenuse is known, take away. Big square minus small square leaves the other small square. Then find its side.
Worked example: 1. Hypotenuse 13, one side 5. 2. 169 − 25 = 144. 3. 12 × 12 = 144, so the side is 12.

**T11. Straight across a grid** (A true thing about math)
> The straight line between two points is a hypotenuse. Count across, count up, then use the squares.
Worked example: 1. From (2, 1) to (8, 9). 2. Across 6, up 8. 3. 36 + 64 = 100. 4. 10 × 10 = 100, so 10.

**T12. Name the sides from the angle** (A true thing about math)
> Mark the angle you are using first. Opposite is across from it. Adjacent is next to it, but not the hypotenuse.
Worked example: 1. Angle at the ground, by your feet. 2. Opposite: the tree. 3. Adjacent: the ground. 4. Hypotenuse: your sight line.

**T13. Same angle, same ratio** (A true thing about math)
> Right triangles with the same angle are similar. So opposite ÷ adjacent is the same, whatever the size. This ratio is called the tangent.
Worked example: 1. Small triangle: up 3, across 4. 3 ÷ 4 = 0.75. 2. Big one: up 6, across 8. 6 ÷ 8 = 0.75. 3. Same angle, same ratio.

**T14. Height from the table** (A true thing about math)
> Height above your eye = distance × the tangent of your angle. Then add your eye height.
Worked example: 1. Angle 45°, 9 m from the tree, eye 1.5 m up. 2. Table: tangent 45° is 1.00. 3. 9 × 1.00 = 9. 4. 9 + 1.5 = 10.5 m.

**T15. Sine and cosine** (A true thing about math)
> Sine = opposite ÷ hypotenuse. Cosine = adjacent ÷ hypotenuse. Pick the ratio that uses the side you know and the side you want.
Worked example: 1. A 5 m ladder at 30° to the ground. 2. Want: height (opposite). Know: ladder (hypotenuse). 3. Sine. 4. 5 × 0.50 = 2.5 m.

**T16. Read the table backwards** (A true thing about math)
> To find an angle, work out the ratio first. Then find it in the table and read the angle beside it.
Worked example: 1. Up 3, across 3. 2. 3 ÷ 3 = 1.00. 3. The table shows tangent 1.00 at 45°. 4. The angle is 45°.

## Real-world and history cards

**H1. A pyramid and a shadow** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> Writers long after Thales, a Greek thinker, told how he measured an Egyptian pyramid. He used its shadow, never climbing it.
Keeper's note:
> One early report says he waited for the hour when a person's shadow is as long as the person is tall. Then the pyramid's shadow equals its height. A later writer, Plutarch, says he set up a stick and compared two triangles. Nobody wrote it down in Thales' own time, so we say "people reported".

**H2. The Sea Island book** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> In the year 263, Liu Hui wrote a Chinese book of surveying problems. The first one finds the height of an island's peak from the shore.
Keeper's note:
> Two poles, each 5 paces high, stand 1000 paces apart in a line with the island. From behind each pole you lie down and sight the peak over its top. The book's answer: the peak is 1255 paces high. Its title, *Sea Island Mathematical Manual*, comes from that first problem.

**H3. A table in a poem** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> Around the year 500, the Indian astronomer Aryabhata wrote a book in verse. One verse holds a table for sines.
Keeper's note:
> His table covers 24 angles, every 3 and three quarters degrees, up to 90°. Sines helped astronomers work out where the planets would be. Our Surveyor's Table does the same job with easier steps: every 5 degrees.

## Problem sets

### Set A. Same shape, scaled (Seedling and Sapling)

**A1 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: A card is 2 by 3. Which copy is the same shape?
- A. 4 by 6. *(right)*
- B. 4 by 5. *(tests: adding the same amount to each side)*
- C. 2 by 6. *(tests: scaling only one side)*
Hint: Was every side multiplied by the same number?
Explanation: 2 × 2 = 4 and 3 × 2 = 6. Both sides doubled.
Feedback A: Yes. Both sides times 2, so the same shape.
Feedback B: That adds 2 to each side. 4 is double 2, but 5 is not double 3.
Feedback C: Only one side grew. The copy is thinner than the card.

**A2 (Seedling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: A square corner is in a shape. The copy is twice as big. The corner?
- A. Doubles to 180°. *(tests: angles scale with lengths)*
- B. Grows a little with the shape. *(tests: angles grow with size)*
- C. Stays 90°. *(right)*
Hint: Does a bigger copy turn its corners?
Explanation: Scaling changes lengths. Angles stay the same, so the shape stays the same.
Feedback A: 180° is a straight line. The copy still has a square corner.
Feedback B: Lengths grow. The amount of turn at a corner does not.
Feedback C: Yes. Same shape, same angles.

**A3 (Seedling, number)**
Prompt: A card is 5 cm by 2 cm. Copy it times 3. Long side?
Answer: 15 cm.
Worked steps: 1. Scale factor 3. 2. Long side: 5 × 3 = 15 cm. 3. (Short side: 2 × 3 = 6 cm.)
Tests: 8 (adding 3 instead of multiplying); 6 (the short side).
Hint: Times 3 means three lots of 5.
Explanation: Every side is multiplied by 3.

**A4 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: A map is 10 by 15 cm. Copy's short side: 30 cm. Long side?
Answer: 45 cm.
Worked steps: 1. Scale factor: 30 ÷ 10 = 3. 2. Long side: 15 × 3 = 45 cm.
Tests: 35 (adding the 20 cm increase); 30 (copying the short side).
Hint: Find the scale factor first: new ÷ old.
Explanation: The short side grew 3 times, so the long side does too.

**A5 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: One side went from 4 cm to 8 cm. Pip says "similar". Enough?
- A. Yes. One pair doubled, so all of them did. *(tests: checking one pair only)*
- B. No. Check every pair of sides, and the angles. *(right)*
- C. No. Similar shapes must be the same size. *(tests: similar means identical)*
Hint: Could the other sides have grown differently?
Explanation: Every pair must share one scale factor, and the angles must match.
Feedback A: One pair can double while another doesn't. Check them all.
Feedback B: Yes. One scale factor for every pair, same angles.
Feedback C: Similar shapes can be any size. Same shape is what matters.

**A6 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: A triangle has sides 4, 6 and 8. Which triangle is similar?
- A. 8, 12, 16. *(right)*
- B. 6, 8, 10. *(tests: adding 2 to each side)*
- C. 8, 12, 10. *(tests: scaling only some sides)*
Hint: Divide each new side by its old side.
Explanation: 8 ÷ 4, 12 ÷ 6 and 16 ÷ 8 all make 2.
Feedback A: Yes. Every side times 2.
Feedback B: 6 ÷ 4 is 1.5, but 10 ÷ 8 is 1.25. Not one factor.
Feedback C: 10 ÷ 8 is not 2. That side didn't double.

**D1 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Diagram: the right angle is at the top right. The top side says 12, the upright side 5, the slanted side ?.
Prompt: Which side is the hypotenuse?
- A. The slanted side, across from the square corner mark. *(right)*
- B. The top side, because 12 is the biggest number. *(tests: judging by the numbers, not the position)*
- C. The upright side, next to the right angle. *(tests: naming a side that touches the right angle)*
Hint: Find the square mark. Look straight across from it.
Explanation: The hypotenuse is across from the right angle, however the triangle is turned.
Feedback A: Yes. Across from the right angle, so it's the longest, even unlabeled.
Feedback B: The biggest label isn't always the hypotenuse. Look for the right angle.
Feedback C: That side touches the right angle. The hypotenuse never does.

**D2 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: Sides 6 and 8 meet at the right angle. How long is the hypotenuse?
Answer: 10.
Worked steps: 1. 6 × 6 = 36. 2. 8 × 8 = 64. 3. 36 + 64 = 100. 4. 10 × 10 = 100, so 10.
Tests: 14 (adding the sides); 100 (forgetting to find the side of the square).
Hint: Square each side, add, then find the side.
Explanation: The two small squares fill the big one: 100. Its side is 10.

**D3 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: Hypotenuse 13. One shorter side 5. The other shorter side?
Answer: 12.
Worked steps: 1. 13 × 13 = 169. 2. 5 × 5 = 25. 3. 169 − 25 = 144. 4. 12 × 12 = 144, so 12.
Tests: about 13.9 (adding when the hypotenuse is known); 144 (forgetting the last step).
Hint: The hypotenuse is known. Add or take away?
Explanation: Big square minus small square leaves 144. Its side is 12, shorter than 13.

**D4 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: How far is it straight from (2, 1) to (8, 9)?
- A. 14. *(tests: adding across and up)*
- B. 10. *(right)*
- C. 8. *(tests: using only the up distance)*
Hint: Draw the right triangle. Across 6, up 8.
Explanation: 36 + 64 = 100, and 10 × 10 = 100.
Feedback A: 14 walks across, then up. Straight cuts the corner.
Feedback B: Yes. The straight line is the hypotenuse.
Feedback C: That's only the up part. Use both sides.

**D5 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: The ford is 4 km east and 3 km north. Straight-line distance?
Answer: 5 km.
Worked steps: 1. 4 × 4 = 16. 2. 3 × 3 = 9. 3. 16 + 9 = 25. 4. 5 × 5 = 25, so 5 km.
Tests: 7 (walking east, then north); 25 (forgetting the last step).
Hint: The straight path is the hypotenuse.
Explanation: The river path is 6 km, so it wiggles about 1 km extra.

**D6 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: A shelf bracket measures 30, 40 and 50 cm. Is the corner square?
- A. Yes. 900 + 1600 = 2500, which is 50 × 50. *(right)*
- B. No. 30 + 40 is 70, not 50. *(tests: adding lengths instead of squares)*
- C. You can't tell without a protractor. *(tests: withholds more than the evidence requires)*
Hint: Do the two small squares fill the big one?
Explanation: The squares fit exactly, so the corner opposite the 50 cm side is square.
Feedback A: Yes. The squares match, so it's a right angle.
Feedback B: Pythagoras adds squares, not lengths.
Feedback C: The lengths are enough. Check the squares.

**D7 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Sides 2 and 3 meet at the right angle. About how long is the hypotenuse?
- A. 5. *(tests: adding the sides)*
- B. About 3.6. *(right)*
- C. 13. *(tests: forgetting the last step)*

Explanation: 3.6 × 3.6 is about 13. It's longer than 3, shorter than 5.

### Set E. The Surveyor's Table (Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**E1 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: The angle is at your feet. Which side is opposite it?
- A. The tree, across from the angle. *(right)*
- B. The ground, next to the angle. *(tests: opposite and adjacent swapped)*
- C. Your sight line, the longest side. *(tests: naming the hypotenuse)*
Hint: Look straight across from the marked angle.
Explanation: Opposite is across from your angle. Here, that's the tree.
Feedback A: Yes. Across from the angle you marked.
Feedback B: The ground touches your angle. That's adjacent.
Feedback C: The longest side is the hypotenuse, across from the right angle.

**E2 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Now mark the angle at the treetop instead. What happens to the names?
- A. Nothing changes. Each side keeps its old name. *(tests: names glued to the shape)*
- B. All three names move, the hypotenuse too. *(tests: moving the hypotenuse too)*
- C. Opposite and adjacent swap. The hypotenuse stays. *(right)*
Hint: The hypotenuse depends on the right angle. The others depend on your angle.
Explanation: From the treetop, the ground is opposite and the tree is adjacent.
Feedback A: The names depend on which angle you marked.
Feedback B: The right angle didn't move, so the hypotenuse stays.
Feedback C: Yes. Names follow the angle you mark.

**E3 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: Up 3, across 4. Same angle, across 8. How far up?
Answer: 6.
Worked steps: 1. Across went from 4 to 8: times 2. 2. Up goes times 2 too: 3 × 2 = 6. 3. 6 ÷ 8 = 0.75, same as 3 ÷ 4.
Tests: 7 (adding the 4 extra across); 4 (keeping the old up).
Hint: Same angle means similar triangles. What's the scale factor?
Explanation: The triangle doubled, so up ÷ across stays 0.75.

**E4 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: You sight the poplar top at 40°, 10 m away. Eye 1.5 m up. Height?
Answer: 9.9 m.
Worked steps: 1. Table: tangent 40° = 0.84. 2. 10 × 0.84 = 8.4 m above your eye. 3. 8.4 + 1.5 = 9.9 m.
Tests: 8.4 (forgetting eye height); 7.9 (using sine, 0.64).
Hint: Distance × tangent, then add your eye height.
Explanation: The triangle starts at your eye, not the ground.

**E5 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Diagram: Hob starts straight across from the far post. The protractor lies flat.
Prompt: Hob walks 20 m along the bank, then measures 35°. Brook width?
Answer: 14 m.
Worked steps: 1. Want: across the brook (opposite). Know: along the bank (adjacent). 2. Tangent. 3. 20 × 0.70 = 14 m.
Tests: 11.4 (using sine, 0.57); 16.4 (using cosine, 0.82).
Hint: Which two sides: opposite and adjacent, or one with the hypotenuse?
Explanation: Opposite and adjacent means tangent.

**E6 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: A 5 m ladder leans at 70° to the ground. How high does it reach?
Answer: 4.7 m.
Worked steps: 1. Want: height (opposite). Know: ladder (hypotenuse). 2. Sine. 3. 5 × 0.94 = 4.7 m.
Tests: 1.7 (cosine, 0.34); 13.75 (tangent, 2.75).
Hint: The ladder is the hypotenuse. Which ratio uses it with opposite?
Explanation: 4.7 m is less than 5 m, as it must be.

**E7 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: A slope rises 6 m over 8 m across. About what angle?
- A. 0.75°. *(tests: reporting the ratio as the angle)*
- B. About 53°. *(tests: across ÷ up, the other angle)*
- C. About 37°. *(right)*
Hint: Work out up ÷ across. Then find it in the tangent column.
Explanation: 6 ÷ 8 = 0.75. That sits between 35° (0.70) and 40° (0.84), nearer 35°.
Feedback A: 0.75 is the ratio. Read the angle beside it.
Feedback B: That's 8 ÷ 6, the angle at the top. Use up ÷ across.
Feedback C: Yes. Between 35° and 40°, nearer 35°.

**E8 (Keeper's Desk, number with a given rule)**
Source: card H2, the Sea Island book.
Prompt: Rule: pole × gap ÷ difference + pole. Poles 5, gap 1000, difference 4. Peak height?
Answer: 1255 paces.
Worked steps: 1. 5 × 1000 = 5000. 2. 5000 ÷ 4 = 1250. 3. 1250 + 5 = 1255 paces.
Tests: 1250 (forgetting to add the pole); 20,005 (multiplying by 4).
Hint: Do the steps in order. Add the pole last.
Explanation: The sightings were 123 and 127 paces behind the poles: difference 4. It matches the book.

**E9 (Keeper's Desk, say why; spoken to a grown-up, not stored)**
Prompt: Why does one small table work for trees of every size?
Model answer for the grown-up: Right triangles with the same angle are similar, so their sides grow together. The ratio of two sides stays the same, so one number per angle works for any size.
Tests: thinking the ratio grows with the triangle.
Hint: What happens to up ÷ across when a triangle doubles?
Explanation: Same angle, similar triangles, same ratio.

- **Scale factor items:** "4 cm became 8 cm: 8 ÷ 4 = 2. Start: new ÷ old for the pair you know."
- **Shrinking items:** "A 6 by 4 card halved is 3 by 2. Start: divide every side by the same number."
- **Area items:** "A 1 by 1 square doubled is 2 by 2: four little squares. Start: draw the copy."
- **Center items:** "Center (0, 0), corner (1, 0), factor 3: (3, 0). Start: across and up from the center."

## Key terms (glossary)

- **similar**: the same shape, any size. Angles match; sides share one scale factor.
- **scale factor**: the number every length is multiplied by.
- **enlargement**: a similar copy grown or shrunk from a center.
- **center of enlargement**: the point the copy grows from.
- **corresponding sides**: sides in the same place on the shape and its copy.
- **ratio**: one length divided by another, like new ÷ old.
- **right angle**: a square corner, 90°.
- **hypotenuse**: the side across from the right angle; the longest side.
- **opposite**: the side across from the angle you marked.
- **adjacent**: the side next to the angle you marked, not the hypotenuse.
- **Pythagoras' theorem**: in a right triangle, the two small squares add up to the big square.
- **square root**: the side of a square with that area. 10 × 10 = 100, so the square root of 100 is 10.
- **tangent**: opposite ÷ adjacent for an angle.
- **sine**: opposite ÷ hypotenuse for an angle.
- **cosine**: adjacent ÷ hypotenuse for an angle.
- **clinometer**: a tool that measures how far up you are looking.
- **eye height**: how far your eye is above the ground.

## Off-screen invitation (optional, grown-up-led)

**Make a clinometer** (Sapling and Keeper's Desk): tape a drinking straw along the straight edge of a protractor. Tie a string through the small center hole and hang a washer on it. Hold it level: the string hangs at 90. Look up through the straw at a treetop. Your angle is how far the string has moved from 90. *Grown-up note: never look at the sun through the straw or any tool. Stay away from roads and water. Count paces or use a tape to measure the distance to the tree.*

**Shadow sticks** (Seedling and up): on a sunny day, stand a stick upright and measure its shadow. At the same moment, measure the shadow of a post or a grown-up. *Grown-up note: do not look at the sun.*

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** similar shapes as scaled copies (one scale factor, unchanged angles), finding missing lengths, why adding distorts, area growing by the factor squared, enlargement from a center on a grid, shadows and similar triangles, Pythagoras' theorem (finding the hypotenuse, a shorter side, a grid distance, and checking a right angle), naming sides from an angle, and first trigonometry: tangent, then sine and cosine, read from a rounded table.

**What it does not claim:** heights, banks and paths are made up. The table is rounded to two decimal places, so answers are approximate. A screen clinometer rehearses the reasoning; real sighting needs care and flat ground. It does not teach calculator inverse keys, the unit circle, or angles beyond right triangles.
