## Story scenes

### Scene 1. Léon's ferry board (Act III ch. 4, letters up and down the river)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** Léon's letters arrive in a hurry, and slightly damp. Dot can read every word. She still can't tell what happened.

**Short:**
> Léon writes in quick bits: "Ferry late. River high." Dot likes them on a sign. In a letter she wants whole sentences.

**Longer:**
> The courier tips out the bag. One letter smells of river.
>
> "Léon," says Dot. She reads it aloud. "Ferry late. River high. New rope. Back soon."
>
> "That's a whole letter?" says Pip.
>
> "It's how he writes his ferry board," says {guide}. "Four little bits. On a sign, everyone knows what he means."
>
> "But which ferry is late?" says Dot. "Whose rope is new? Back from where?"
>
> She turns the page over. On the back, in smaller writing: *À bientôt, Léon.* (See you soon, Léon.) *fluent check*
>
> "Each bit is missing something," says Dot. "Who did it, or what they did."
>
> Pip counts on his claws. "So we write back in whole sentences. And we ask about the rope."

### Scene 2. Pip's reply that never stops (Act III ch. 4)

**Hook:** Nuria offers a choice in her letter. Pip writes back at once. His reply is one sentence long. It is a very long sentence.

**Short:**
> Nuria asks a question. Pip's reply runs on and on without a stop. Dot helps him split it, join it and trim it.

**Longer:**
> Nuria's letter begins *¡Hola desde Vadoalto!* (Hello from Vadoalto!) *fluent check*
>
> "Either your courier comes up by the high ford, or our runner comes down the river path," Dot reads. "Neither way is quick. Which do you choose?"
>
> Pip is already writing. He hands it over.
>
> Dot reads: "The river path is better the runner can stay for supper we have plums Ember says hello."
>
> "That's four sentences pretending to be one," says Dot.
>
> "It's efficient," says Pip.
>
> "It's breathless." She takes a pencil. A period here. A *so* there. "And who is Ember, to Nuria?"
>
> Pip adds: *Ember, who is a pocket-dragon, says hello.*
>
> "Better," says Dot. "Now it sounds like you, only slower."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> Spanish has its own pair for neither and nor: *ni... ni...* *No vino ni el barco ni el corredor* means "Neither the boat nor the runner came." *fluent check*

### Scene 3. Five years apart (Act IV ch. 1, the old map and Hollis's account)

**Short:**

**Longer:**

>
> "So draining it must have caused the floods," says Pip.
>
> Bramble snorts. "My hat and my boots got soaked the same day. My hat didn't do it."
>
> "That's different," says Pip.
>
> "Is it? One flood, five years later. That's all the page says."
>
> {guide} writes Pip's sentence on the board: *Draining the Sponge must have caused the floods.* Then erases *must*.
>
> "What word fits what we actually know?" {guide} asks.
>
> Pip thinks. "Might?"
>
> "Might," says Bramble, satisfied. "For now."
>
> The magpie says nothing, which is rare.

**Longer:**

>

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer; worked-example steps appear one tap at a time. Every card is **A true thing about language**.

**Card 1. Three word jobs**
> A conjunction joins words or ideas. A preposition links a noun to the sentence, often showing place or time. An interjection shows a sudden feeling.

Worked example: *Oh! Pip hid the plum under the cushion, but Dot found it.*
1. Oh: a sudden feeling. Interjection.
2. Under: links "the cushion" to where the plum went. Preposition.
3. But: joins two ideas. Conjunction.

**Card 2. One word, two jobs**
> Some words, like before, after and since, can be a preposition or a conjunction. Look at what follows. A noun means preposition. A whole clause means conjunction.

Worked example: *Before dawn, and before the sun rose.*
1. Before dawn: dawn is a noun. Preposition.
2. Before the sun rose: the sun rose has a subject and a verb.
3. So that before is a conjunction.

**Card 3. Pairs that travel together**
> Some joining words come in pairs: either and or, neither and nor, both and and, not only and but also, whether and or. Keep each pair together.

Worked example: *Neither the boat ___ the runner came.*
1. Find the first half: neither.
2. Its partner is nor.
3. Neither the boat nor the runner came.

**Card 4. Same shape on both sides**
> After each half of a pair, use the same kind of words. By boat, on foot: two short phrases. That matching makes the sentence easy to follow.

Worked example: *either by boat or walking*
1. After either: by boat, a short phrase.
2. After or: walking, a different shape.
3. Match it: either by boat or on foot.

**Card 5. Clauses and phrases**
> A clause has a subject and a verb: the ferry leaked. A phrase is a group of words without both: across the river, at dawn, the old ferry.

Worked example: *At dawn, the ferry leaked.*
1. At dawn: no subject and verb. A phrase. It tells when.
2. The ferry leaked: subject, ferry; verb, leaked.
3. One phrase, one clause.

**Card 6. Main and subordinate**
> A main clause makes sense alone. A subordinate clause starts with a word like because, when or if, and leans on a main clause.

Worked example: *When the ferry leaked, Léon bailed it out.*
1. Léon bailed it out: makes sense alone. Main clause.
2. When the ferry leaked: leaves us waiting. Subordinate clause.

**Card 7. Relative clauses**
> A relative clause adds facts about a noun. It starts with a pointer word like who, which, that, whose, where or when. The pointer points back to the noun.

Worked example: *Léon is the ferry keeper who lives at Les Saules.*
1. Who points back to the ferry keeper.
2. Who lives at Les Saules is the relative clause.
3. Who is the relative pronoun.

**Card 8. Fragments**
> A fragment is part of a sentence standing alone. It is missing a subject, a verb, or the main clause it leans on.

Worked example: *Running along the river path.*
1. What is the verb? Running.
2. Who is running? We aren't told.
3. Fix it: The courier was running along the river path.

**Card 9. Run-ons and three fixes**
> A run-on squashes two sentences into one with no joining word. Fix it with a period, a comma plus a joining word, or a word like because.

Worked example: *The plums were ripe I ate six.*
1. The plums were ripe. That's one sentence.
2. I ate six. That's another.
3. Fixes: a period, ", so", or "Because the plums were ripe, I ate six."

**Card 10. Four sentence shapes**
> Simple: one clause. Compound: two main clauses joined. Complex: a main clause and a subordinate one. Compound-complex: two main clauses plus a subordinate one.

Worked example: *When the ferry leaked, Léon bailed it out, and Pip held the letters.*
1. When the ferry leaked: subordinate.
2. Léon bailed it out: main. Pip held the letters: main.
3. Two main plus one subordinate: compound-complex.

**Card 11. Grow, join and trim**
> Writers grow a sentence with details, join short ones, and trim extra words. Mix long and short sentences. Keep every fact the reader needs.

Worked example: *The bridge is open. It was mended in spring.*
1. Join: The bridge, which was mended in spring, is open.
2. Or start with a phrase: Mended in spring, the bridge is open.
3. Trim: "at this moment in time" becomes "now."

**Card 12. How sure? Modal verbs**
> Modal verbs like will, must, should, might, could and can't show how sure we are. Pick the word that matches your evidence.

Worked example: *Dark clouds over the high ford.*
1. Clouds are a clue, not proof.
2. "It will rain" is too sure.
3. "It might rain" fits the clue.

**Card 13. Looking back**
> To guess about the past, add have: must have, might have, can't have. Must have is very sure. Might have is maybe. Can't have means surely not.

Worked example: *The flood post is wet to the third mark.*
1. The wet line is right there.
2. Fresh mud sits on it.
3. The water must have reached the third mark.

**Card 14. Perhaps, surely, certainly**
> Some adverbs show how sure we are too. Perhaps means maybe. Surely and certainly sound very sure. "It might rain" matches "Perhaps it will rain."

Worked example: *The reeds might be spreading.*
1. Might means maybe.
2. Perhaps also means maybe.
3. Perhaps the reeds are spreading.

**Card 15. Must has two jobs**
> Must can give a rule: carts must keep off the bridge. Must can make a sure guess: she must be tired. Read around it to tell which.

Worked example: *You must close the gate. The gate must be stuck.*
1. You must close the gate: someone is telling you. A rule.
2. The gate must be stuck: a sure guess from clues.

## Benches

### (a) Word Jobs (Sapling)

**Header:** Tap each colored word. Is it joining, linking a noun, or showing a feeling?

**Colored words and their jobs:** Oh no (feeling) · into (links a noun) · by (links a noun) · until (joining) · Hooray (feeling) · from (links a noun) · before (links a noun)

**Sentences (A story from Willowbank):**
- Oh no, Léon dropped the rope into the river.
- Pip waited by the gate until the courier came.
- Hooray! The letter from Nuria arrived before supper.

**Take it out:** Tap a word to hide it. Does the sentence still make sense?

**Take it out feedback:**
- Hide into: Dropped the rope the river? Into showed where it went.
- Hide until: Now two ideas just bump together. Until joined them.
- Hide Hooray: The sentence still works. Hooray only added a feeling.

**One word, two jobs:** Before dawn or before the sun rose? Tap the one where before joins a clause.

### (b) Pairs That Travel Together (Sapling)

**Header:** Match each first half with its partner.

**Halves:** either → or · neither → nor · both → and · not only → but also · whether → or

**Mend the pair (from Pip's draft):**
- "The river path is neither short or dry." becomes "The river path is neither short nor dry."
- "Both the boat or the runner can carry seeds." becomes "Both the boat and the runner can carry seeds."

**Mend the pair feedback (any slip):** Find the first half. Which partner travels with it?

**Same shape:** Tap the half that doesn't match. Then choose a matching one.
- either by boat or walking → on foot
- both to read and writing → to write

### (c) Who, Which, Where (Seedling)

**Header:** Pick the word that points back. Then tap the noun it points to.

**Pointer words and when to use them:**
- who: for people
- which: for things
- that: for people or things
- whose: for belonging
- where: for places
- when: for times

**Join with a pointer:** Two sentences become one.
- Nuria keeps the flood post. She lives in Vadoalto. → Nuria, who lives in Vadoalto, keeps the flood post.
- The ferry leaks. Léon mends it every week. → The ferry, which Léon mends every week, leaks.
- The ford is near the mill. The courier crosses there. → The ford where the courier crosses is near the mill.

**Name it:** Tap the relative clause. Then tap its relative pronoun.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):** Many American editors use that when a clause picks out which one, and which with commas when it adds an extra fact. Both are correct English.

### (d) Mend the Letter (Seedling)

**Header:** Read Pip's reply line by line. Mark each line: sentence, fragment or run-on.

**Pip's reply (A story from Willowbank):**
1. Dear Nuria,
2. The river path is better the runner can stay for supper.
3. Because we have plums.
4. Ember says hello.
5. Waiting for your answer by the bridge.

**Line feedback:**
- Line 2 as sentence: Read it aloud. Where would you stop for breath?
- Line 3 as sentence: Because leaves us waiting. Because we have plums, what?

**Choose a fix:** For each line marked, pick one fix. Every fix shown works.

### (e) Sentence Shapes (Sapling, Keeper's Desk)

**Header:** Count the clauses. Then sort each sentence by its shape.

**Shape cards:**
- The ferry leaked. (simple)
- The ferry leaked, but Léon bailed it out. (compound)
- When the ferry leaked, Léon bailed it out. (complex)
- When the ferry leaked, Léon bailed it out, and Pip held the letters. (compound-complex)

**Phrase or clause?** Tap each chunk: phrase or clause.
- at dawn · the ferry leaked · across the river · because the rope snapped · the old blue boat

**Join and trim (Dot's notice):**
- Short: The bridge is open. It was mended in spring. Carts may cross it.
- Joined: Mended in spring, the bridge is open to carts.
- Trimmed: "At this moment in time, the ferry is, in fact, late." → "The ferry is late now."
- Grown with a naming phrase: Léon, the ferry keeper, writes in a hurry.

**Rhythm:** Read Dot's two versions aloud. One has three short sentences. One has one long one. Which sounds calmer? Either answer is fine.

**Who is doing it? (Keeper's Desk):** Read each opener. Tap the person or thing right after the comma. Is that who did it?
- Rowing across the river, the letter got wet. → The letter wasn't rowing. Léon was.
- Tired from the meeting, Bramble went home early. → Yes. Bramble was tired.

### (f) How Sure? (Seedling, Sapling, Keeper's Desk)

**Sureness line header (A made-up model):** Words for how sure we are, from surely not to very sure. Real meaning also depends on the rest of the sentence.

**The line:** can't · might · could · should · will · must

**Seedling: clue cards.** Read the clue. Slide the word along the line until it fits.
- Clue: Dark clouds over the high ford. → It might rain.
- Clue: The flood post is wet to the third mark, with fresh mud. → The water must have reached it.
- Clue: The bridge is dry and the brook is low. → The bridge can't have flooded today.

**Sapling: the Act IV cards (A story from Willowbank).**
- Hollis's page 4: the Sponge drained, a fine harvest, then a big flood five years later. → Draining might have made floods worse.
- The wet corner: reeds nobody planted. → Seeds could have floated or blown in.

**Swap it:** Swap the modal verb for perhaps, surely or certainly. Keep the same sureness.

**Two jobs for must:** Tap each sentence: a rule, or a sure guess?

**Keeper's Desk: what we know so far.** Help {guide} set a sureness word on each almanac line. Some lines are sure. Most are could.
- The low corner is wet now. (sure: it is)
- The reeds arrived by water. (could)
- Draining the Sponge caused the floods. (might have; one flood can't settle it)
- A record of floods before draining would help. (should)

**Almanac feedback (any line set to must):** What clue would make us that sure? Is it on the page yet?

### Set A. Word jobs (Sapling)

**A1 (Sapling, guided).** Steps shown first: 1. Read the sentence. 2. Hide "under." 3. What do we lose?

- Choices: Complex ✔ · Compound (`subordinate-is-coordinating`) · Simple (`one-sentence-one-clause`)
- Feedback: Compound: Because makes the second part lean on the first. · Simple: Count the verbs: slept and was. Two clauses.
- Hint: Can "because the story was long" stand alone?
- Explanation: One main clause and one subordinate clause make a complex sentence.

**E3 (Sapling).**
- Sentence: The bridge is open. It was mended in spring. Carts may cross it.
- Prompt: Which keeps all three facts in one sentence?
- Choices: The bridge is open, it was mended, carts may cross. (`comma-splice`) · The bridge was mended in spring and is open. (`trim-drops-meaning`) · Mended in spring, the bridge is open to carts. ✔
- Feedback: Commas: Commas alone can't join three sentences. · Mended and open: Where did the carts go? Keep all three facts.
- Hint: Check each choice for the bridge, spring and carts.
- Explanation: One opening phrase and one clause hold all three facts.

**E4 (Keeper's Desk, guided).** Steps shown first: 1. Read the opening phrase. 2. Who is rowing? 3. Who comes right after the comma?
- Sentence: Rowing across the river, the letter got wet.
- Prompt: What is wrong with this sentence?
- Choices: It says the letter was rowing ✔ · It needs a period after river (`opener-is-sentence`) · Nothing is wrong with it (`dangling-ok`)
- Feedback: Period: Rowing across the river would be a fragment then. · Nothing: Picture it. Is the letter holding the oars?
- Hint: Who or what comes right after the comma?
- Explanation: An opener describes whatever comes next. Here, that's the letter. Oops.

**E5 (Keeper's Desk).**
- Prompt: Which rewrite fixes the rowing sentence?
- Choices: The letter got wet, rowing across the river. (`move-only`) · As Léon rowed across, the letter got wet. ✔ · Rowing across the river. The letter got wet. (`fragment-fix`)
- Feedback: Moved: The letter is still the one rowing. Name the rower. · Period: Rowing across the river is now a fragment. Who rowed?
- Hint: Which choice says who was rowing?
- Explanation: As Léon rowed across names the rower. Now nobody thinks the letter rowed.

**E6 (Keeper's Desk, true / not true).**
- "Across the river is a phrase. It has no subject and verb." True.
- "When the ferry leaked is a main clause." Not true (`subordinate-is-main`): it leans on another clause.
- "At dawn can tell when something happened." True.
- Hint: Look back at cards 5 and 6.
- Explanation: Phrases lack a subject and verb. Subordinate clauses lean. Some phrases tell when or where.

### Set F. How sure? (Seedling, Sapling, Keeper's Desk)

**F1 (Seedling, guided).** Steps shown first: 1. Read the clue. 2. Is it proof or a clue? 3. Pick the matching word.
- Sentence: Dark clouds pile up over the high ford. It ___ rain this afternoon.
- Prompt: Which word fits how sure we can be?
- Choices: will (`too-sure`) · can't (`against-evidence`) · might ✔
- Feedback: Will: Clouds don't promise rain. Will sounds certain. · Can't: Can't means no chance. Clouds make rain possible.
- Hint: Do dark clouds always bring rain?
- Explanation: Might shows rain is possible. Clouds are a clue, not proof.

**F2 (Seedling).**
- Sentence: The flood post is wet to the third mark. Fresh mud sits on it. The water ___ reached the third mark.
- Prompt: Which words fit the evidence?
- Choices: must have ✔ · might have (`too-unsure`) · can't have (`against-evidence`)
- Feedback: Might: The wet line and fresh mud are right there. You can be surer. · Can't: The post is wet to the mark. That says it did.
- Hint: How strong are the clues?
- Explanation: Two strong clues point one way. Must have is a sure guess about the past.

**F3 (Sapling, Act IV, A story from Willowbank).**
- Sentence: Hollis's page 4: the Sponge drained, then one big flood five years later.
- Prompt: Bramble says one flood proves nothing. Which sentence fits?
- Choices: Draining the Sponge must have caused the floods. (`after-means-because`) · Draining might have made floods worse. ✔ · Draining the Sponge can't have changed a thing. (`too-sure-against`)
- Feedback: Must: One flood five years later can't show that. Bramble is right. · Can't: We can't rule it out either. The page is only one clue.
- Hint: Is one flood a clue or proof?
- Explanation: Might have says possible. The town needs more evidence before anyone says must.

**F4 (Sapling).**
- Prompt: Which means the same as "The reeds might be spreading"?
- Choices: Surely the reeds are spreading. (`adverb-strength`) · The reeds are certainly spreading. (`adverb-strength`) · Perhaps the reeds are spreading. ✔
- Feedback: Surely: Surely sounds very sure. Might is only maybe. · Certainly: Certainly means no doubt. Might leaves room for doubt.
- Hint: Which word means maybe?
- Explanation: Perhaps and might both mean maybe.

**F5 (Sapling).**
- Prompt: In which sentence does "must" give a rule?
- Choices: Carts must keep off the wet bridge. ✔ · The orchard must have flooded once. (`must-one-meaning`) · Bramble must be tired after the meeting. (`must-one-meaning`)
- Feedback: Orchard: That must is a sure guess about the past. · Bramble: That must is a guess. Nobody ordered Bramble to be tired.
- Hint: Which one is telling someone what to do?
- Explanation: Must can give a rule or make a sure guess. Here, carts are told what to do.

**F6 (Sapling, match).** Match each sentence to what its modal verb does.
- You may borrow the boat. → allowed · You should take your boots. → a good idea · It might flood tonight. → possible · Carts must stay off the bridge. → a rule
- Feedback for a wrong pair: Ask: is someone allowing, advising, guessing or ordering?
- Hint: May often means allowed. Might means maybe.
- Explanation: Modal verbs can allow, advise, order, or show how sure we are.

**F7 (Keeper's Desk, say why, read by a grown-up; never graded, nothing stored).**
- Prompt: Tell a grown-up which word you'd write about the Sponge, and why.
- Grown-up guide: A strong answer picks might or could, not must, and gives a reason: one flood after draining is one clue. It may name what would make us surer, such as a longer flood record or the reed corner's water. Any careful reason counts.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Conjunction:** a joining word, like and, but, because or when.
- **Preposition:** a word that links a noun to the sentence, like under, before or across.
- **Interjection:** a word that shows a sudden feeling, like oh, oops or hooray.
- **Subject:** who or what a sentence is about.
- **Verb:** a doing or being word, like ran, is or wrote.
- **Clause:** a group of words with a subject and a verb.
- **Main clause:** a clause that makes sense on its own.
- **Subordinate clause:** a clause that leans on a main clause, often starting with because, when or if.
- **Phrase:** a group of words without its own subject and verb, like across the river.
- **Relative clause:** a clause that adds facts about a noun, starting with who, which, that, whose, where or when.
- **Relative pronoun:** who, which, that or whose, pointing back to a noun.
- **Fragment:** part of a sentence standing alone as if it were whole.
- **Run-on:** two sentences squashed together with no joining word.
- **Modal verb:** a helping verb like might, must or should. It shows how sure we are, or what is allowed or needed.
- **Evidence:** the clues that tell us how sure we can be.
- **Ambiguity:** when words can mean two things, like must as a rule or a guess.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** the jobs of conjunctions, prepositions and interjections; correlative pairs and matching shapes; relative clauses and their pointer words; phrases and clauses, main and subordinate; fixing fragments and run-ons; simple, compound, complex and compound-complex sentences; joining and trimming; and modal verbs and adverbs for how sure we are, with evidence from the town's flood question.

**Terms:** this pack uses US terms (period, not full stop). The English national curriculum's Year 5 terms modal verb, relative pronoun, relative clause and ambiguity appear here. Parenthesis, brackets and dashes are taught in Speech Marks for the Puppets (pack 264), and cohesion in For the Next Keeper (pack 277).

**What it does not claim:** grammar rules here are school conventions for clear writing. Good writers use fragments and long sentences on purpose, and the "that or which" choice is a style preference. The sureness line is a simple model; real meaning depends on the whole sentence. Willowbank, its letters and its floods are invented. The pack does not decide whether draining the Sponge made floods worse: it only asks which words the evidence supports.

**Talk and writing:** "Say why" answers are spoken to you and never stored. Nothing the child writes is recorded.
