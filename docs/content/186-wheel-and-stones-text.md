# The Wheel and the Stones: all child-facing text

Companion to [spec 186](../specs/186-wheel-and-stones.md). Use as written, after independent spec review and a math check. `{guide}` is the explorer's chosen companion and is never gendered; `{courier}` is the explorer's courier. Pip is he/him, Dot she/her, Bramble she/her (with her magpie), the miller she/her (with her cat), the baker he/him, Ember the pocket-dragon, Nuria she/her. Real-world facts are in the [source audit](../source-audits/186-wheel-and-stones.md); items still marked `[check]` stay off screen until cleared. Nothing here is scored or timed.

## Labels used on every card

- **A story from Willowbank**: the town, its mill, its wheel, its stones and every character are made up.
- **A true thing about math**: checked math, explained simply.
- **A true thing from history**: something people really did or wrote.
- **A made-up model**: a simple game rule, shown on the card, that is not real measurement.

## Story scenes

### Scene 1. The wheel turns again (Act II, chapter 1: the weekly market)

**Hook:** The market needs flour. The wheel needs new paddles. The only planks left after the storm have slanted ends.

**Short:**
> The miller cuts a corner off a slanted plank and nails it to the other end. "Now it's a paddle," she says. Bramble counts the wood twice.

**Longer:**
> The storm cracked six paddles on the mill wheel. The only planks left were sawn on a slant, so each one leans like a pushed-over box.
>
> "Paddles should be square at the corners," says the miller. She draws a straight line down from one top corner, at a right angle. She saws along it. A triangle drops off.
>
> She fits the triangle onto the other end. Now the plank is a rectangle.
>
> "You've made it bigger," says Bramble. "It looks bigger."
>
> "Same wood," says the miller. "I only moved a piece."
>
> Pip measures the leaning side. "Then this long side is how tall it is."
>
> "No," says {guide}. "Tall is the straight-up line. The leaning side is longer than tall."
>
> The cat sits on the triangle. Nobody can move it for an hour.

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> A plank 6 squares along and 4 squares straight up covers 24 squares. Its leaning side is 5, but 6 × 5 counts wood that isn't there. Moving the triangle never adds or loses any.

### Scene 2. How far does the rim go? (Act II, chapter 2: a garden across the seasons)

**Hook:** Willowbank is keeping records now. The miller wants to write down how far the wheel's rim travels each day.

**Short:**
> The miller ties a ribbon to the rim. One turn. "How far did the ribbon go?" Pip says: "Across. Four meters." The cat disagrees.

**Longer:**
> The town keeps records now, so the miller wants one for the wheel. She ties a red ribbon to the rim and lets the wheel turn once.
>
> "Four meters," says Pip. "The wheel is four meters across. So the ribbon went four meters."
>
> The cat has been chasing the ribbon. The cat is out of breath.
>
> "The ribbon didn't go across," says Dot. "It went all the way around."
>
> {guide} unrolls a long tape along the bank. "Let's lay the rim out flat and see how many acrosses fit."
>
> One across. Two. Three. And a little bit more.
>
> "A bit more than three," says Pip slowly. "Every time?"
>
> "Every circle," says {guide}. "Big or small. Try the cat's bowl."

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> The way around any circle is a little more than 3 times the way across: about 3.14. That number is called pi. The wheel is 4 m across, so each turn is about 12.56 m.

### Scene 3. A cloth for the cake table (Act II, chapter 3: Ember's birthday)

**Hook:** The baker needs a cloth to keep flour off the birthday cake table. The table is 2½ m by 1¾ m.

**Short:**
> "Two and a half by one and three quarters," says the baker. "How much cloth is that?" Ember has never seen a fraction of a meter.

**Longer:**
> The baker's cake table is long, and the mill's flour dust gets everywhere. He wants a cloth to cover it.
>
> "It's two and a half meters long," he says, "and one and three quarters wide. How much cloth do I buy?"
>
> Ember pokes the tape measure. Ember thinks a meter should come in one piece.
>
> Dot chalks the table top on the floor and cuts it into quarter-meter squares. "Small squares," she says. "Count them, then put them back together into whole meters."
>
> Ten squares along. Seven squares across.
>
> "Seventy little squares," says Pip. "So seventy meters of cloth!"
>
> "Seventy little squares," says {guide}. "How many make one whole square meter?"
>
> Pip counts sixteen inside one meter square. He goes very quiet.

**Keeper's note** (A true thing about math):
> Each quarter-meter square is 1/16 of a square meter. Seventy of them is 70/16, which is 4 3/8 square meters.

### Scene 4. How much grain the stones grind (Act III, chapter 1: the floating market)

**Hook:** The Vadoalto boats bring sacks of grain. Their own millstone is small, half as wide as Willowbank's.

**Short:**
> "Your stone is twice as wide as ours," says the Vadoalto trader. "So it grinds twice as much?" The miller smiles. "More than that."

**Longer:**
> The Vadoalto boats are heavy with grain. "Our one small stone is slow," says the oldest trader. "Can your stones help?"
>
> The miller pulls the cloth off her round gray stones.
>
> "Ours is about half as wide as yours," says the trader. "So yours grinds twice as much."
>
> "Twice as wide," says the miller. "But the grinding happens on the whole flat face. Count the face."
>
> {guide} draws both stones on the floor and slices each one like a pie. The slices of the big stone fit together into a long, lumpy rectangle. So do the small stone's.
>
> Pip compares the two rectangles. "The big one is twice as long and twice as tall."
>
> "So how many small ones fit inside it?"
>
> Pip counts. "Four." He looks at the sacks. "Oh."

**Keeper's note** (A made-up model):
> On the Stone Floor, a stone grinds in step with its face area. Twice as wide gives four times the face, so four times the grain.

### Scene 5. A letter from upstream (Act III, chapter 4: letters up and down the river)

**Hook:** Nuria writes from Vadoalto. Her mill's wheel is 3½ m across. She wants to know how far its rim goes.

**Short:**
> Nuria writes: "¡Hola desde Vadoalto! (Hello from Vadoalto!) Our wheel is 3½ m across. How far does a rim go in one turn?"

**Longer** (Spanish line for a fluent reader's check):
> *¡Hola desde Vadoalto!* (Hello from Vadoalto!)
>
> Dear friends in Willowbank,
>
> The miller here says your wheel is bigger than ours. Ours is 3½ meters across. I measured it twice, standing in the cold water.
>
> She says the rim goes about three and a bit times across in one turn. My brother says exactly three. I side with the miller. We have a bet. The loser carries the flour.

## Teach cards

Each card is under 30 words. Worked steps appear one tap at a time.

**T1. Base and height** (A true thing about math)
> Pick a side to be the base. The height is the straight-up distance from it, at a right angle. A leaning side is not the height.
Worked example: 1. Base: 6 squares. 2. Leaning side: 5 squares. 3. Straight-up height, with its square corner: 4 squares.

**T2. Cut and slide** (A true thing about math)
> Cut a triangle off one end of a leaning shape. Slide it to the other end. You get a rectangle with the same area.
Worked example: 1. Base 6, height 4. 2. Cut the triangle and slide it. 3. Rectangle: 6 × 4 = 24 squares.

**T3. A triangle is half** (A true thing about math)
> Two copies of the same triangle make a leaning shape. So one triangle is half of it: base × height ÷ 2.
Worked example: 1. Two triangles make a 6 by 4 shape: 24 squares. 2. One triangle is half. 3. 24 ÷ 2 = 12 squares.

**T4. Any side can be the base** (A true thing about math)
> Turn the shape. Any side can be the base. Each base has its own straight-up height. The area stays the same.
Worked example: 1. Base 6, height 4: 24. 2. Turn it: base 5, height 4.8. 3. 5 × 4.8 = 24 again.

**T5. Small fraction squares** (A true thing about math)
> Cut one square meter into thirds one way and quarters the other. You get 3 × 4 = 12 tiles. Each tile is 1/12.
Worked example: 1. 2/3 m by 3/4 m covers 2 × 3 = 6 tiles. 2. 6 of 12 tiles. 3. 6/12 = 1/2 square meter.

**T6. Mixed-number sides** (A true thing about math)
> Split each side into wholes and parts. Find each piece's area. Add the pieces.
Worked example: 1. 2½ × 1¾: 2 × 1 = 2. 2. 2 × ¾ = 1½ and ½ × 1 = ½. 3. ½ × ¾ = 3/8. 4. Total: 4 3/8 m².

**T7. Parts of a circle at work** (A true thing about math)
> The center is the middle. The radius goes from the center to the rim. The diameter goes right across: two radii. The circumference is the way around.
Worked example: 1. The wheel's radius: 2 m. 2. Its diameter: 2 + 2 = 4 m.

**T8. A little more than three** (A true thing about math)
> Measure around a circle. Measure across. Divide round by across. Every circle gives about 3.14. We call it pi.
Worked example: 1. Hub: round 157 cm. 2. Across 50 cm. 3. 157 ÷ 50 = 3.14.

**T9. How far one turn goes** (A true thing about math)
> Circumference = pi × diameter. Or pi × 2 × radius: the same thing.
Worked example: 1. The wheel is 4 m across. 2. 3.14 × 4 = 12.56. 3. Each turn, the rim goes about 12.56 m.

**T10. Two handy pi numbers** (A true thing about math)
> 3.14 and 22/7 are both close to pi. Neither is exact. Pick 22/7 when the length fits sevens.
Worked example: 1. Across: 3½ m, which is 7/2. 2. 22/7 × 7/2 = 22/2. 3. 11 m.

**T11. Slice and line up** (A true thing about math)
> Slice a circle like a pie. Line the slices up, points up, points down. It makes a lumpy rectangle. More slices, straighter edges.
Worked example: 1. Long side: half the way around, pi × radius. 2. Short side: the radius. 3. Area: pi × radius × radius.

**T12. Radius or diameter?** (A true thing about math)
> Area needs the radius, times itself. If you know the diameter, halve it first. Radius squared means radius × radius, not radius × 2.
Worked example: 1. Stone 1.4 m across. 2. Radius 0.7 m. 3. 22/7 × 0.7 × 0.7 = 1.54 m².

**T13. Round parts in a bigger shape** (A true thing about math; Keeper's Desk)
> Split the shape into parts you know. A half circle is half of pi × radius × radius. Add the parts.
Worked example: 1. Door: 1 m by 2 m, 2 m². 2. Top: half a circle, radius ½ m: about 0.39 m². 3. About 2.39 m².

**T14. Trapezoids** (A true thing about math)
> A trapezoid has a parallel top and bottom. Two copies, one turned, make a leaning plank. One is half: (top + bottom) × height ÷ 2.
Worked example: 1. Top 3, bottom 7, height 4. 2. Two copies: a plank 10 long, 4 tall: 40. 3. Half: 20 squares.

**T15. Prisms** (A true thing about math; Keeper's Desk)
> A prism is the same shape all along. Find the end's area. Times the length. That is its volume, in cubic units.
Worked example: 1. Crate end: 4 × 3 = 12. 2. Length: 5. 3. 12 × 5 = 60 cubic units, like the crate layers.

**T16. A cylinder is a prism** (A true thing about math; Keeper's Desk)
> A cylinder's end is a circle. Find the circle's area. Times the length or thickness.
Worked example: 1. Radius 1 m: 3.14 × 1 × 1 = 3.14 m². 2. Length 2 m. 3. 3.14 × 2 = 6.28 m³.

## Real-world and history cards

**H1. A round field in Egypt** (A true thing from history; Sapling and up)
> In Egypt, more than 3,500 years ago, a scribe named Ahmes copied a book of math problems. One finds the area of a round field.
Keeper's note:
> The field is 9 khet across. Take away a ninth: 8 khet. Then 8 × 8 = 64 setat, an area unit. The rule is very close. Most of the papyrus is now in the British Museum.

**H2. Shapes with 96 sides** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> Archimedes lived in Syracuse, on Sicily, more than 2,200 years ago. He drew shapes with 96 sides inside and outside a circle.
Keeper's note:
> The way around the circle sits between the two shapes. So he showed pi is more than 3 10/71 and less than 3 1/7. That second number is 22/7.

**H3. A fraction from China** (A true thing from history; Keeper's Desk)
> Zu Chongzhi worked in China more than 1,500 years ago. He gave 22/7 as a rough pi and 355/113 as a close one.
Keeper's note:
> 355/113 is right to six decimal places. He showed pi is between 3.1415926 and 3.1415927. His book is lost; later writers recorded his numbers.

Feedback A: The rim is shared: half on top, half underneath.
Feedback B: The short side is the radius. The long side is part of the rim.
Feedback C: Yes. Pi × radius.

**D2 (Sapling, number)**
Prompt: The millstone is 1.4 m across. What is its face area? Use 22/7.
Answer: 1.54 m².
Worked steps: 1. Radius: 1.4 ÷ 2 = 0.7 m. 2. 0.7 × 0.7 = 0.49. 3. 22/7 × 0.49 = 1.54 m².
Tests: 6.16 (using 1.4 as the radius); 4.4 (the circumference, a length, not an area).
Hint: Area needs the radius. What's half of 1.4?
Explanation: Halve the diameter, square the radius, times pi. Area is in m².

**D3 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: A round pond has radius 3 m. What is radius squared?
- A. 9, because 3 × 3. *(right)*
- B. 6, because 3 × 2. *(tests: squaring as doubling)*
- C. 3, the radius itself. *(tests: forgetting to square)*
Hint: Squared means times itself.
Explanation: 3 squared is 3 × 3 = 9. Area is about 3.14 × 9.
Feedback A: Yes. Three times three.
Feedback B: That doubles it. Squared means 3 × 3.
Feedback C: Area needs radius × radius.

**D4 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: A 0.7 m stone grinds 5 sacks. How many does a 1.4 m stone grind?
Answer: 20 sacks.
Worked steps: 1. Small face: 22/7 × 0.35 × 0.35 = 0.385 m². 2. Big face: 1.54 m². 3. 1.54 ÷ 0.385 = 4. 4. 4 × 5 = 20 sacks.
Tests: 10 (twice as wide read as twice the area); 5 (ignoring size).
Hint: Twice as wide: how many small faces fit in the big one?
Explanation: Twice the radius gives four times the area, so four times the grain in this model.

**D5 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: A 1 m by 2 m door has a half circle on top. Area?
Answer: about 2.39 m² (2 + 0.3925).

Tests: 2.785 (a whole circle on top); 3.57 (using 1 m as the radius).
Hint: The top is half a circle 1 m across. What's its radius?
Explanation: Split into a rectangle and a half circle, then add.

**D6 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: A stone is 2.1 m across. Which pi makes the way around easiest?
- A. 3.14, because it is always best. *(tests: one approximation always wins)*
- B. 22/7, because 2.1 ÷ 7 = 0.3 is easy. *(right)*
- C. Neither. Only exact pi gives an answer. *(tests: withholding more than the evidence needs)*
Hint: Does 2.1 fit sevens?
Explanation: 2.1 ÷ 7 = 0.3, so 22/7 × 2.1 = 22 × 0.3 = 6.6 m.
Feedback A: 3.14 works, but 3.14 × 2.1 is harder: 6.594.
Feedback B: Yes. 22 × 0.3 = 6.6 m.
Feedback C: Both give a good answer. Neither is exact; both are close.

### Set F. Trapezoids, prisms and the millstone (Sapling and Keeper's Desk)

**F1 (Sapling, number)**
Picture: a paddle end. Top 3, bottom 9, straight-up height 4, slanted sides 5.
Prompt: How many squares does the paddle end cover?
Answer: 24 squares.
Worked steps: 1. Top + bottom: 3 + 9 = 12. 2. 12 × 4 = 48, two copies. 3. Half: 24.
Tests: 48 (forgetting to halve); 30 (the slanted 5 as height).
Hint: Two copies make a plank. Use the height with the square corner.
Explanation: (top + bottom) × height ÷ 2 = 24. The slanted side is not the height.

**F2 (Sapling, 3-choice)**
Prompt: Why does a trapezoid's rule end with ÷ 2?
- A. Because a trapezoid always has two short sides. *(tests: recalling the rule from side names)*
- B. Because you add its four sides and halve them. *(tests: area confused with perimeter)*
- C. Because two copies make a plank; one is half. *(right)*
Hint: Copy the trapezoid, turn it, and join them.
Explanation: Two copies make a leaning plank, (top + bottom) long. One trapezoid is half.
Feedback A: Side names don't make the rule. Join two copies.
Feedback B: Adding sides is perimeter. Area covers the inside.
Feedback C: Yes. Two copies, one plank, so halve it.

**F3 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: Trough end: a triangle, base 2 ft, height 1 ft. 6 ft long. Volume?
Answer: 6 cubic feet (6 ft³).
Worked steps: 1. End: 2 × 1 ÷ 2 = 1 ft². 2. Times the length: 1 × 6. 3. 6 ft³.
Tests: 12 (forgetting to halve the triangle); 9 (adding 2, 1 and 6).
Hint: Find the triangle's area first. Then times the length.
Explanation: Any prism: end area × length.

**F4 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**
Prompt: The seed crate is 4 by 3, and 5 long. Which works as a prism?
- A. End 4 × 3 = 12, times 5: 60. *(right)*
- B. End 4 + 3 = 7, plus 5: 12. *(tests: adding dimensions)*
- C. End's edge 14, times 5: 70. *(tests: perimeter for end area)*
Hint: The end's area, not its edge.
Explanation: 12 square units of end, 5 long: 60 cubic units, as the crate layers show.
Feedback A: Yes. End area × length.
Feedback B: Adding measures no space. Multiply.
Feedback C: 14 is the way around the end. Use its area.

**F5 (Keeper's Desk, number)**
Prompt: The millstone is 1.4 m across, 0.5 m thick. Volume? Use 22/7.
Answer: 0.77 m³.
Worked steps: 1. Radius: 0.7 m. 2. Face: 22/7 × 0.7 × 0.7 = 1.54 m². 3. 1.54 × 0.5 = 0.77 m³.
Tests: 3.08 (1.4 used as the radius); 2.2 (the circumference times the thickness).
Hint: A cylinder is a prism. Find the circle face first.
Explanation: Circle area × thickness. Volume is in cubic meters.

**F6 (Keeper's Desk, 3-choice)**

- A. Four times as much. *(tests: mixing up thicker with wider)*
- B. Twice as much. *(right)*
- C. The same, since the face is the same. *(tests: volume as face area only)*
Hint: Same face. How many face layers now?
Explanation: The face is the same, and the length doubles, so the volume doubles.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **base**: the side you choose to measure from.
- **height**: the straight-up distance from the base, at a square corner.
- **parallelogram**: a four-sided shape with two pairs of parallel sides; a leaning plank.
- **area**: how much flat surface a shape covers.
- **square meter (m²)**: the area of a square 1 m on each side.
- **center**: the middle point of a circle.
- **radius**: from the center to the rim.
- **diameter**: right across through the center; two radii.
- **circumference**: the whole way around a circle.
- **pi**: round ÷ across for any circle, about 3.14.
- **squared**: a number times itself.
- **khet**: an old Egyptian length unit.
- **setat**: an old Egyptian area unit.
- **sector**: a pie slice of a circle.
- **trapezoid**: a four-sided shape with one pair of parallel sides (UK: trapezium).
- **prism**: a solid that is the same shape all along its length.
- **volume**: how much space a solid fills, in cubic units (m³, ft³).

## Off-screen invitation (optional, grown-up-led)

**String and tins** (Seedling and up): wrap a string once around a tin, a cup and a plate. Mark it, lay it straight and measure. Measure across each one through the middle. Divide round by across. *Grown-up note: use a soft tape or string, not wire. Results near 3.1 or 3.2 are normal; tapes slip.*

**Paper pie** (Sapling and up): cut a paper circle into 16 slices and line them up, points up and down. *Grown-up note: scissors for an adult or with supervision.*

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** area of parallelograms (base × perpendicular height) by cut and slide; triangle area as half a parallelogram; area with fraction and mixed-number sides on a grid; parts of a circle in use; pi as round ÷ across; circumference = pi × diameter; circle area = pi × radius × radius by lining up sectors; choosing 3.14 or 22/7; choosing radius or diameter; trapezoid area as half of two copies; volume of prisms and cylinders as end area × length.

**What it does not claim:** the wheel, stones, cloths and grain amounts are made up. Real milling depends on much more than stone area. It does not prove the sector argument with limits, and it does not teach volume of cones or spheres.
