Léon is a kingfisher (he/him), the miller is she/her, Bramble is a girl witch (she/her) with a magpie, the librarian is they/them. Lines marked `[check]` need a source check before build. ## 2. Story scenes (A story from Willowbank)

### 2.1 Léon's letter (Act III, chapter 4)

**Hook:** A damp letter from Les Saules. Léon has found a very old bridge.

**Short:**
> Léon writes: "Near the big river there's an old stone bridge. Everyone says the Romans left it. How would we know?"

**Longer:**
> The letter is slightly damp, as Léon's letters always are.
>
> *Bonjour, Willowbank !* (Hello, Willowbank!)
>
> *Près de la grande rivière, il y a un vieux pont de pierre.* (Near the big river there is an old stone bridge.)
>
> *Ici, tout le monde dit : « Ce sont les Romains qui l'ont laissé. »* (Here, everyone says: "The Romans left it.")
>
> "My ferry passes under it every morning," Léon goes on. "It has round arches and a curved back like a sleeping cat. My aunt says it is two thousand years old. The baker here says his grandfather saw it mended. Who is right? How would anyone know?"
>
> Pip turns the letter over, hoping for a drawing of food. There isn't one.
>
> "There's a new shelf in the archive," says the librarian. "It won't tell us about Léon's bridge. But it can tell us how people find out."

`[French text: needs review by a fluent French reader before build.]`

**Keeper's note:**
> Some bridges called "Roman" turn out to be much younger. One in western France, long called Gallo-Roman, was dated by a historian in 2017 to the early Middle Ages. Its name probably came from a Roman road nearby. To check, historians look at records, building materials and how the stones were laid.

### 2.2 The miller's question (Act III, chapter 4, after Léon's letter)

**Hook:** The miller reads Léon's letter twice. Then she asks a question about water.

**Short:**
> "Water to a whole town, with no pump?" says the miller. "My mill race only works because water runs downhill. Was it the same trick?"

**Longer:**
> The miller reads Léon's letter twice, then looks out at her mill race, the narrow channel that brings water to her wheel.
>
> "The Romans carried water to whole towns," she says. "Nobody had pumps for that. So how?"
>
> Her cat watches the water slide along the race and drop onto the wheel.
>
> "Your race slopes," says {guide}. "Just a little. The water always goes downhill."
>
> "Then their channels must have sloped too," says the miller. "Very gently. For a very long way." She frowns. "Too steep and it would wear the channel out. Too flat and it would just sit there."
>
> "There's a model valley on the Workshop table," says {guide}. "You could try it."
>
> The miller is already putting on her coat.

**Keeper's note:**
> The channel to Nîmes in France dropped only about 12 to 17 meters in about 50 kilometers. The official Pont du Gard site gives an average of about 25 centimeters for every kilometer. That is about 1 centimeter for every 40 meters.

### 2.3 Bramble's ribbons (Act II, chapter 3, or any later visit)

**Hook:** Bramble says her ribbons are older than Roman roads. The librarian fetches a book.

**Short:**
> "My ribbons are older than Roman roads," says Bramble. The librarian checks. Roman roads came first, by a very long way.

**Longer:**
> Bramble ties a blue ribbon on the gatepost and steps back to admire it.
>
> "These knots are ancient," she says. "Older than Roman roads, probably."
>
> The librarian opens the Roads and Water book. "The Via Domitia, a Roman road through the south of France, was begun about 2,100 years ago."
>
> Bramble's magpie looks away politely.
>
> "Who taught you the knots?" asks {guide}.
>
> "My grandmother."
>
> "And who taught her?"
>
> "Her grandmother." Bramble counts on her fingers and gives up. "Fine. Not older than Roman roads. Older than Pip's breakfast, though."
>
> "Everything's older than my breakfast," says Pip sadly. "I ate it."

**Keeper's note:**
> Dates before the year 1 count backwards (BCE). There is no year zero. From 118 BCE to 2026 CE is 118 + 2026 − 1 = 2,143 years. Historians give the road's start as about 120 to 118 BCE, so "about 2,100 years" is honest.

## 3. The archive cards (A true thing from history)

Each card: a reading at two lengths, then a teach card, then its problem set. Teach-card worked examples reveal one step per tap.

### Card 1. Building a road

**Map strip (before the reading):** A map of the Mediterranean Sea with Italy, Gaul (now France) and Spain marked, and a timeline: Ancient Egypt, then Ancient Rome, then today.

**Map caption:** Rome grew up in Italy, by the Mediterranean Sea. Its roads reached Gaul and Spain.

**Short reading:**
> Roman builders dug two ditches and piled the earth into a bank. They put big stones at the bottom and smaller gravel on top.

**Longer reading:**
> Many Roman roads were built the same way. First, builders dug two shallow ditches along the sides. They piled the earth from the ditches into a long bank in the middle. On top, they pressed big stones or cobbles in hard. Then came a layer of smaller gravel or pebbles, packed down tight.
>
> The top was curved, higher in the middle. Rain ran off to the sides and into the ditches, so the road stayed firm.
>
> Builders used whatever stone or gravel was close by. On soft, wet ground they sometimes laid timber and brushwood underneath. Some roads had big flat paving stones on top. Many did not: a hard gravel top was common.
>
> Soldiers built many roads, especially for the army. Hired workers, and people forced to work, built others.

**Teach card:** A curved top keeps a road dry. Rain runs off the sides into the ditches.

**Worked example (tap by tap):**
1. Rain falls on a flat road.
2. The water sits in puddles and soaks in.
3. Rain falls on a curved road.
4. The water runs to the sides, into the ditches.

### Card 2. Reading a milestone

**Short reading:**
> Romans put up stone posts called milestones. They showed how far it was to a town, in Roman numerals.

**Longer reading:**
> Along big Roman roads stood milestones: tall stone posts. One found near Narbonne is almost 2 meters tall. Many carried the emperor's name and how many Roman miles it was to a town.
>
> The numbers were Roman numerals. I is 1, V is 5, X is 10, L is 50, C is 100.
>
> Usually you add them up: XVI is 10 + 5 + 1 = 16. But a smaller one before a bigger one is taken away: IV is 5 − 1 = 4.
>
> Old Roman stones often show four as IIII. Today we usually write IV. Both mean four.
>
> Roman numerals have no zero. There is no numeral for "nothing".

**Teach card:** Add the numerals up. But a smaller one before a bigger one is taken away.

**Worked example (tap by tap):** A milestone reads XIV.
1. Read from the left: X is 10.
2. Next is I, then V. I comes before a bigger numeral.
3. So IV means 5 − 1 = 4.
4. 10 + 4 = 14 Roman miles.

**Numeral key (always visible on this card):** I = 1 · V = 5 · X = 10 · L = 50 · C = 100 · D = 500 · M = 1000

### Card 3. Paces and miles

**Short reading:**
> A Roman pace was two steps. A Roman mile was 1,000 paces. That's about one and a half kilometers.

**Longer reading:**
> Romans counted long distances in paces. One step with your left foot and one with your right made one pace. A thousand paces made a Roman mile. Our word "mile" comes from the Latin *mille*, a thousand.
>
> A Roman pace was about 1.48 meters. So a Roman mile was about 1.48 kilometers. That's a bit longer than a kilometer.
>
> Experts aren't sure of the exact length, because they're not sure of the exact Roman foot. "About 1.48 km" is the honest answer.

**Teach card:** 1 pace = 2 steps. 1 Roman mile = 1,000 paces, about 1.48 km.

**Worked example (tap by tap):** A milestone says II.
1. II means 2 Roman miles.
2. 1 Roman mile is about 1.48 km.
3. 2 × 1.48 = 2.96 km.
4. That's about 3 km.

### Card 4. Water walks downhill

**Short reading:**
> Romans carried water to towns in long channels. Each channel sloped gently downhill all the way. No pumps: gravity did the work.

**Longer reading:**
> Towns needed lots of fresh water. Romans found a spring, often far away and higher up, and built a channel called an aqueduct to the town.
>
> The channel sloped downhill the whole way, but only a little. Then the water moved by itself.
>
> Most of an aqueduct was a covered channel at ground level or below it, sometimes in tunnels. It wound along hillsides to keep its gentle slope. Arches were built only where the channel had to cross a valley or low ground.
>
> The famous Pont du Gard in southern France is one of those crossings. It has three rows of arches, about 49 meters high. The water ran along the very top, on its way to the town of Nîmes.
>
> The whole channel was about 50 km long. It dropped only about 12 to 17 meters in all. Books give different numbers, because people measured from different points.

**Teach card:** Drop per km tells how steep a channel is. Divide the drop by the distance.

**Worked example (tap by tap):** A channel drops 1 meter over 4 km.
1. 1 meter is 100 centimeters.
2. Share 100 cm over 4 km.
3. 100 ÷ 4 = 25.
4. It drops 25 cm each kilometer.

**Pont du Gard card (Details):**
> The Pont du Gard was built about 2,000 years ago. The official site says about the year 50 CE. Newer digs point to the middle of the first century CE. Older books said about 19 BCE. When new evidence turns up, dates can change.

### Card 5. Water in town

**Short reading:**
> At Nîmes the water reached a round stone basin. Pipes carried it to fountains, baths and some homes.

**Longer reading:**
> When the channel reached Nîmes, the water poured into a round stone basin. Holes in its side led into pipes, and the pipes went to different parts of town.
>
> Most people collected water at public fountains, for drinking, cooking and washing. Water also went to the public baths, where people washed, and to public toilets called latrines.
>
> A few richer families paid to have a pipe to their own house. Most homes had no pipe at all.
>
> A Roman writer, Vitruvius, said the public fountains should never run short.

**Teach card:** Aqueduct water fed fountains, baths, latrines and a few paying homes.

**Worked example (tap by tap):**
1. Water pours into the round basin.
2. Pipes lead to different streets.
3. One pipe reaches a street fountain.
4. Neighbors fill their jugs there.

### Card 6. How we know

**Short reading:**
> We know about Roman roads and water from what survives: roads, bridges, carved stones, and books Romans wrote.

**Longer reading:**
> Some things survive: the Pont du Gard, a piece of the Via Domitia found under a square in Narbonne in 1997, and many milestones.
>
> Some Romans wrote about building. An architect called Vitruvius wrote about how much a water channel should slope. But old copies of his book give different numbers.
>
> Archaeologists dig carefully and date what they find. Newer digs changed the date of the channel to Nîmes.
>
> Every source tells us some things. Every source leaves things out. A milestone tells a distance. It doesn't tell who walked past it.

**Teach card:** A source tells some things and not others. Say only what it shows.

**Worked example (tap by tap):** A milestone says "2 miles from Ratae".
1. It tells: the distance to Ratae.
2. It tells: an emperor's name is carved on it.
3. It doesn't tell: who walked this road.
4. It doesn't tell: how busy the road was.

## 4. Problem sets

Format for each item: prompt; choices with the right answer marked; the misconception each wrong choice tests; hint; explanation; feedback per choice (20 words or fewer). Positions of right answers vary. "Say why" items are read by a grown-up and never auto-graded.

### Set A. Milestone numbers (Card 2)

**A1 (Sprout, guided).** Which numeral means 10?
- Steps shown first: "I is one stick." · "X looks like two sticks crossed."
- Choices: **X** ✓ · V (*confuses V and X*) · I (*confuses I with ten*)
- Hint: Look at the numeral key. Find 10.
- Explanation: X is 10. V is 5 and I is 1.
- Feedback: X: "Yes. X is ten." · V: "V is five, half of ten." · I: "I is one. Find the crossed one."

**A2 (Seedling).** A milestone says XX. How many Roman miles is that?
- Choices: 11 (*reads X as 10 and the second X as 1*) · **20** ✓ · 2 (*counts symbols, not values*)
- Hint: Each X is 10. Add them.
- Explanation: XX is 10 + 10 = 20. A real milestone of the Via Domitia, near Narbonne, says XX.
- Feedback: 11: "Each X is ten, not one." · 20: "Yes. Ten and ten make twenty." · 2: "There are two Xs, but each one is worth ten."

**A3 (Seedling).** A milestone says XIV. How far is it?
- Choices: 16 (*reads IV additively, as 1 + 5*) · 4 (*drops the X*) · **14** ✓
- Hint: I comes before V. What does that mean?
- Explanation: X is 10, and IV is 5 − 1 = 4. So XIV is 14.
- Feedback: 16: "That's XVI. Here I comes before V, so take it away." · 4: "Don't forget the X at the start." · 14: "Yes. Ten, then five take away one."
- I'm stuck worked example: XIX: X is 10. IX is 10 − 1 = 9. 10 + 9 = 19. Now try XIV the same way.

**A4 (Seedling).** An old Roman stone shows IIII. What number is it?
- Choices: **4** ✓ · 6 (*mixes it up with VI*) · Not a real number (*thinks only IV is allowed*)
- Hint: Add the sticks.
- Explanation: IIII is 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 4. Old stones often used IIII. Today we usually write IV.
- Feedback: 4: "Yes. Romans often carved four this way." · 6: "That's VI. Count the sticks here." · Not a real number: "It's real. Many Roman stones show IIII for four."

**A5 (Sapling).** Which is the usual way to write 49 today?
- Choices: IL (*invents a subtraction pair: I can only go before V or X*) · LIX (*misreads as 49; it is 59*) · **XLIX** ✓
- Hint: Make 40, then make 9.
- Explanation: 40 is XL (50 − 10). 9 is IX (10 − 1). Together: XLIX.
- Feedback: IL: "I only comes before V or X. Build 40, then 9." · LIX: "That's 50 + 9, which is 59." · XLIX: "Yes. XL is 40, IX is 9."

**A6 (Sapling, guided).** A building's date stone says MDCCC. What year is that?
- Steps shown first: "M = 1000" · "D = 500" · "CCC = 300"
- Choices: 1300 (*skips the D*) · **1800** ✓ · 2300 (*reads D as 1000*)
- Hint: Add one group at a time.
- Explanation: 1000 + 500 + 300 = 1800. Roman milestones were dated by emperors' titles, not by years like this. Later builders carved dates this way.
- Feedback: 1300: "Don't skip the D. It's worth 500." · 1800: "Yes. M, D, then three Cs." · 2300: "D is 500, half of M."

**A7 (Keeper's Desk, number answer).** Write this year as a number: MMXXVI.
- Answer: 2026
- Working, revealed on request: MM = 2000 · XX = 20 · VI = 6 · 2000 + 20 + 6 = 2026
- Misconception watched: reading VI as 4 (*mixes up the order rule*).
- Hint: Split it into groups: MM, XX, VI.
- Explanation: Two thousands, two tens, and a six make 2026.
- Feedback (if 2024): "Check the end. V then I means add: 6."

**A8 (Keeper's Desk, say why).** A carved number might be LIII or LIIII. Why does that matter?
- For the grown-up: LIII is 53 and LIIII is 54. Scholars really do disagree about one milestone in Britain like this, because one carved mark is hard to see. A good answer notices that one tiny stroke changes the distance, and that the number could not have a zero to fill a gap. There is no single right wording.
- Follow-up, also read aloud: How would you write zero miles in Roman numerals? (There is no numeral for it. Let the child notice that.)

### Set B. Paces, miles and kilometers (Card 3)

**B1 (Seedling, guided).** One pace is two steps. How many steps make 1,000 paces?
- Steps shown first: "1 pace = 2 steps" · "1,000 × 2 = ?"
- Answer: 2,000 steps
- Misconception watched: 1,000 (*thinks a pace is one step*).
- Hint: Every pace has two steps in it.
- Explanation: 1,000 paces × 2 steps = 2,000 steps. That's one Roman mile of walking.
- Feedback (if 1,000): "A Roman pace was two steps: left and right."

**B2 (Seedling).** How many paces are in 3 Roman miles?
- Choices: **3,000** ✓ · 1,003 (*adds instead of multiplying*) · 6,000 (*counts steps, not paces*)
- Hint: Each Roman mile is 1,000 paces.
- Explanation: 3 × 1,000 = 3,000 paces.
- Feedback: 3,000: "Yes. A thousand paces for each mile." · 1,003: "Three miles is three thousands, not one thousand and three." · 6,000: "That's the steps. Count the paces."

**B3 (Sapling, number answer).** The Via Domitia milestone says XX. About how many kilometers is that?
- Answer: about 30 km (exactly 29.6 using 1.48)
- Working, revealed on request: XX = 20 · 20 × 1.48 = 29.6 · about 30 km
- Misconceptions watched: 21.48 (*adds 1.48 instead of multiplying*); about 14 (*divides; thinks a kilometer is longer*).
- Hint: Each Roman mile is about 1.48 km. Multiply.
- Explanation: 20 Roman miles × 1.48 km ≈ 29.6 km, about 30 km.

**B4 (Sapling).** Which is longer?
- Choices: A kilometer (*thinks the bigger number means the bigger unit*) · They are the same (*thinks a Roman mile is a kilometer*) · **A Roman mile** ✓
- Hint: A Roman mile is about 1.48 km.
- Explanation: A Roman mile is about 1.48 km, so it is longer than one kilometer.
- Feedback: A kilometer: "A Roman mile is about one and a half kilometers." · Same: "Close, but a Roman mile is nearly half a kilometer more." · A Roman mile: "Yes. About 1.48 km."

**B5 (Keeper's Desk).** About how many Roman miles is 10 km?
- Choices: about 15 (*multiplies when it should divide*) · about 10 (*thinks the units match*) · **about 7** ✓
- Hint: A Roman mile is longer than a km. Will the number be bigger or smaller?
- Explanation: 10 ÷ 1.48 ≈ 6.8, about 7 Roman miles. Longer units mean a smaller count.
- Feedback: 15: "Roman miles are longer, so you need fewer of them. Divide." · 7: "Yes. 10 ÷ 1.48 is about 6.8." · 10: "The units aren't the same size. A Roman mile is longer."

### Set C. Downhill by a little (Card 4)

**C1 (Sprout).** Which channel will carry water from the spring to the town?
- Picture choices: a channel sloping up to the town (*thinks water can climb by itself*) · **a channel sloping gently down to the town** ✓ · a flat channel (*thinks water moves without a slope*)
- Hint: Which way does water go by itself?
- Explanation: Water runs downhill by itself. The channel must slope down to the town.
- Feedback: Up: "Water can't climb up by itself." · Down: "Yes. Downhill, a little at a time." · Flat: "On the flat, the water just sits."

**C2 (Seedling, guided).** A channel drops 2 meters over 8 km. How much each km?
- Steps shown first: "2 m = 200 cm" · "200 ÷ 8 = ?"
- Choices: 4 cm (*divides 8 by 2*) · 16 cm (*multiplies 2 × 8*) · **25 cm** ✓
- Hint: Share the drop over the kilometers.
- Explanation: 200 cm ÷ 8 = 25 cm each km. That's the same slope as 1 m over 4 km.
- Feedback: 4 cm: "Share the drop, 200 cm, over 8 km." · 25 cm: "Yes. The same as the Nîmes average." · 16 cm: "Divide the drop by the distance, don't multiply."

**C3 (Sapling, number answer).** The Nîmes channel dropped about 25 cm each km, for 50 km. Total drop?
- Answer: about 12.5 m (1,250 cm)
- Working, revealed on request: 25 cm × 50 = 1,250 cm · 1,250 cm = 12.5 m
- Misconception watched: 75 (*adds 25 and 50*).
- Hint: 25 cm for each of 50 kilometers. Multiply.
- Explanation: 25 × 50 = 1,250 cm, which is 12.5 m. Some books say 17 m; they measured from different points.

**C4 (Sapling).** Which channel is gentler?
- Choices: 1 m drop over 2 km (*thinks a shorter channel is gentler*) · **1 m drop over 4 km** ✓ · They are the same (*looks only at the drop*)
- Hint: Same drop. Which one spreads it over more km?
- Explanation: The same drop over a longer way is gentler: 25 cm each km, against 50 cm.
- Feedback: 2 km: "Same drop, shorter way, so it's steeper." · 4 km: "Yes. The drop is spread out more." · Same: "Same drop, but over different distances."

**C5 (Keeper's Desk).** Were Roman aqueducts mostly arches?
- Choices: **No, mostly channels at or below ground** ✓ · Yes, on arches nearly all the way (*thinks the famous parts are the whole*) · No, mostly pumps and pipes (*thinks Romans pumped the water*)
- Hint: Where were arches needed?
- Explanation: Arches were built where a channel crossed low ground. For Rome's aqueducts, only about a tenth of the length was on arches.
- Feedback: Arches all the way: "Arches are the parts we photograph. Most of the way was hidden." · Covered channels: "Yes. Arches only crossed valleys and low ground." · Pumps: "No pumps. Gravity moved the water."

**C6 (Keeper's Desk, say why).** In a straight line it's 25 km. Why did the channel run 50 km?
- Context line shown above: The spring and Nîmes are about 25 km apart in a straight line.
- For the grown-up: The channel had to keep a steady gentle slope, so it followed the hillsides around valleys and hills where it could. Going straight would have meant much bigger bridges or deeper tunnels. Accept any answer that links the longer route to keeping the slope gentle or avoiding huge bridges.

### Set F. What the stone tells us (Card 6)

**Source card for F1 to F3 (A true thing from history):**
> A milestone of the emperor Hadrian was found in 1771 near Thurmaston, by a Roman road in England. It names Hadrian and gives the distance: 2 Roman miles from Ratae, the Roman town at Leicester. Experts date it to 119 to 120 CE from the emperor's titles carved on it. It was found about 375 meters farther out than 2 Roman miles from Leicester's east gate. It is now in a museum in Leicester.

**F1 (Sapling).** What does this milestone tell us?
- Choices: How many people used the road (*thinks a source tells more than it says*) · **How far it was to Ratae** ✓ · Who carved it (*assumes a maker's name*)
- Hint: What is written on the stone?
- Explanation: It gives the distance to Ratae and names Hadrian. It says nothing about travelers or the carver.
- Feedback: People: "The stone doesn't count travelers." · Distance: "Yes. 2 Roman miles from Ratae." · Carver: "No carver's name is on it."

**F2 (Sapling).** How do experts know when it was made?
- Choices: A date in Roman numerals on it (*thinks Romans wrote years like ours*) · Its size and shape (*thinks size dates a stone*) · **The emperor's titles carved on it** ✓
- Hint: Read how the experts dated it.
- Explanation: Hadrian's titles changed over his reign, so they show when the stone was carved.
- Feedback: Numerals: "The stone has no year on it. The titles give the date." · Titles: "Yes. His titles date the stone." · Size: "Size can't date it. Read the card again."

**F3 (Keeper's Desk).** It was found 375 m past 2 Roman miles. What can we say?
- Choices: The Romans measured the miles badly (*jumps to blame without evidence*) · The stone must be a modern fake (*overclaims from one odd fact*) · **We can't tell where counting began** ✓
- Hint: Where does "2 miles from Ratae" start counting?
- Explanation: The experts who study it suggest the distances may have been measured from a different place. The card alone can't settle it.
- Feedback: Measured badly: "Maybe they started counting somewhere else. We can't tell." · Fake: "One odd distance doesn't make a stone fake." · Can't be sure: "Yes. Where they started counting is the open question."

**F4 (Keeper's Desk).** An expert says this stone is "probably" the oldest Latin writing in Gaul. How sure?
- Source line shown above: A milestone marked XX, with the name of Gnaeus Domitius, was found in 1949 near Narbonne. A French scholar calls it probably the oldest Latin inscription in Gaul.
- Choices: **A careful expert judgment** ✓ · Certain, it's proven (*treats "probably" as proof*) · Just a wild guess (*underclaims an expert's reasoned view*)
- Hint: What does "probably" mean?
- Explanation: "Probably" means the expert has good reasons but isn't certain. A new find could change it.
- Feedback: Certain: "'Probably' means not quite certain." · Expert judgment: "Yes. Good reasons, still open to new finds." · Just a guess: "It's more than a guess. The expert has reasons."

**F5 (Keeper's Desk, say why).** Old copies of Vitruvius give different slope numbers. What should a careful book say?
- For the grown-up: Vitruvius wrote a rule for the smallest slope of a water channel, but the copies and editions that survive disagree on the number. A good answer says the book should give both readings, or say the number is uncertain, and not pick one as if it were sure. Ask: what would help settle it? (Older copies; measuring real channels.)

## 5. The Channel Bench (A made-up model)

**Rules card (always one tap away):**
> In this made-up valley:
> - The town is 6 meters lower than the spring.
> - Your channel drops the same amount each kilometer.
> - Drop per km = total drop ÷ length.
> - Steeper than 0.5 m per km: too steep here. The water wears the channel.
> - Gentler than 0.25 m per km: too flat here. The water slows and mud settles.
> - In between: the water reaches the fountain.
>
> These limits are for this game. Real Roman channels had many different slopes.

**Leg cards:**

| Leg | Card | Line |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hill | Around the hill | 12 km. Easy digging. Work: 12. |
| Hill | Tunnel through the hill | 4 km. Hard digging in the dark. Work: 8. |
| Valley | Around the valley | 14 km. Easy digging. Work: 14. |

**Run button:** Run the water

**Result lines:**
- Length: {km} km. Work: {work}.
- Drop per km: {fall} m ÷ {km} km = {g} m. (Tap to see the working.)
- Flows: "The water reaches the town fountain. Steady and clear."
- Too steep: "The water rushes. In this model, it wears the channel away."
- Too flat: "The water barely moves. In this model, mud settles and it stops."

**c1. Which way does water go?**
- Card: Tip the test channel. Which way does the water go?
- Choices on the bench: Flat · Gently down · Steeply down
- After all three: "Flat: it sat still. Gently down: it flowed. Steeply down: it rushed. Water needs a slope."

**c2. Around or through?**
- Card: The valley has a bridge already. Choose: around the hill, or through it?
- After both runs: "Around the hill: 18 km, 0.33 m per km, it flows. Through: 10 km, 0.6 m per km, too steep here. Shorter isn't always better."

**c3. Spring to fountain**
- Card: Lay the whole channel. Find a route where the water reaches the fountain.
- Builder's hint (after two runs that don't flow): Look at the drop per km. Is it too big or too small? Make the route longer or shorter.
- After a working run: "It flows! Is there another route that works? Which costs less work?"
- Keep my route stems: I chose this route because… · What it costs is… · A worry about it is…

**c4. What if the spring were lower? (Keeper's Desk)**
- Card: Now the town is only 4 m below the spring. Which routes still flow?
- After testing: "With 4 m, only the shortest route flows: tunnel and bridge, 0.4 m per km. The best route depends on the fall."
- Second card: Try 8 m. What changes?
- After testing: "With 8 m, three routes flow. Tunnel and bridge is now too steep here."

**Keeper's note on the bench:**
> The real channel to Nîmes was not even. Some stretches dropped much more gently than others, and engineers chose where to spend the drop. Its average, about 25 cm per km, would just pass this game's rule. That's luck of the made-up numbers, not a Roman rule.

## 6. Key terms

- **Aqueduct:** a channel that carries water from a spring to a town.
- **Arch:** a curved stone shape that holds up weight over a gap.
- **Camber:** the curve on top of a road, higher in the middle.
- **Ditch:** a long dug channel beside a road that collects rain.
- **Gravity:** the pull that makes things, and water, go downhill.
- **Inscription:** words carved into stone or metal.
- **Milestone:** a stone post by a road that shows a distance.
- **Pace:** two steps, about 1.48 meters for the Romans.
- **Roman mile:** 1,000 paces, about 1.48 kilometers.
- **Roman numerals:** letters Romans used for numbers: I, V, X, L, C, D, M.
- **Source:** anything that tells us about the past.
- **Spring:** a place where water comes up out of the ground.
- **Drop per km:** how far a channel goes down in each kilometer.

## 7. Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** how Roman roads were built to shed water; how aqueducts moved water by gentle slope with no pumps; Roman numerals on milestones (with no zero); Roman miles of 1,000 paces, about 1.48 km; drop per distance as a simple rate; and how historians use surviving structures, inscriptions and writing, each with limits.

**What it does not claim:** that every Roman road was paved; that aqueducts were mainly arches; exact single figures where sources differ (the Nîmes channel's drop is given as about 12 to 17 m, and its date as the middle of the first century CE, with the older view noted); that Léon's bridge is Roman (it is fictional and its age is left open). The Channel Bench is a made-up model: its slope limits are game numbers, not Roman rules or engineering advice.

**Sources in brief:** the official Pont du Gard site and UNESCO for the bridge and aqueduct; English Heritage and Historic England for road building; the Roman Inscriptions of Britain database for Hadrian's milestone; French scholarly publications for the Via Domitia milestone; National Geographic for how much of Rome's aqueduct length was on arches. Full list in the [source audit](../source-audits/135-roads-and-water.md).

> *Safety:* adult-led. Use cold tap water only, a small amount, and wipe up spills so nobody slips. Keep it away from electrics. This is a play test, not a model of a real aqueduct.
