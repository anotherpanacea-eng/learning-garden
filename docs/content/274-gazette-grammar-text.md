## Story scenes

### Scene 1. Pie eaten (any time; the standalone set)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** The press prints its first practice sheet: PIE EATEN AT CAFÉ. Pip has a crumb on his nose.

**Short:**
> The practice headline says "Pie eaten." It doesn't say who ate it. Pip did. The sentence leaves him out.

**Longer:**
> The press clanks. Out slides the first practice sheet: **PIE EATEN AT CAFÉ.**
>
> "Lovely," says the librarian. "Short and true."
>
> "It doesn't say who," says Dot.
>
> Everyone looks at Pip. Pip looks at the ceiling. There is a crumb on his nose.
>
> "The pie was eaten," says {guide}. "By whom?"
>
> "By a fox," says Pip, very quietly. "A hungry one."
>
> The librarian sets new letters: **FOX EATS PIE.**
>
> "Same news," they say. "But now the fox comes first."
>
> Pip sighs. "I liked the first one better."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> "Pie eaten" is short for "A pie was eaten." That is the passive voice. The passive can leave out who did it.

### Scene 2. Two columns (Act IV, chapter 3)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** Under its two big stories, this week's *Gazette* prints two short pieces. One rarely says who did anything. One keeps saying "we."

**Short:**
> The miller's short report says hay was swept away. The librarian's note says "we drained the Sponge." Grammar can't tell which explanation is true.

**Longer:**
> A copy of the *Willowbank Gazette* is pinned to the library door. Below the two big stories are two short pieces, side by side.
>
> Pip reads the miller's report aloud. "Hay was swept from the low fields. Fences were knocked flat. The mill wheel was stopped by the water."
>
> Then the librarian's note. "We drained the Sponge. We cut the brook straight. Then the floods came closer together."
>
> "The miller's hardly says who," says Pip. "Sneaky."
>
> "Who knocked the fences flat, then?" asks Bramble. "The wind. Nobody hid anything. Sometimes the doer is weather."
>
> "And the librarian's keeps saying *we*," says Dot. "That doesn't make it true either."
>
> {guide} looks from one column to the other. "So the grammar shows where each writer is looking."
>
> "But not who's right," says Bramble.
>
> Nobody argues with that. Not even Pip.

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> A passive sentence can leave out the doer. Sometimes the doer is unknown, or obvious, or just weather. Sometimes a writer leaves it out on purpose. The sentence alone can't tell you which.

### Scene 3. Three posters (Act IV, chapter 4)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** Before the meeting, three posters go up in the library, one for each plan. Each mixes orders and guesses.

**Short:**
> Three posters, one for each plan. Each gives an order and imagines what would happen. None of them can prove what will.

**Longer:**
> The librarian pins up the notice first. **MEETING TONIGHT. Bring a lantern. Come and have your say.**
>
> Then the posters go up, one for each plan.
>
> **RAISE THE WALL!** *If the wall were higher, the water would stay in the brook.*
>
> **LET THE SPONGE COME BACK!** *We propose that the lowest field be left wet. The marsh would hold the water.*
>
> **DO A LITTLE OF EACH!** *If we built some of each, nobody would lose everything.*
>
> Dot counts them off. "Three orders. And three *woulds*."
>
> "*Would* is a guess, not a promise," says the librarian. "Every poster has one."
>
> "So the posters can't settle it," says {guide}.
>
> "No," says Bramble, folding her arms. "That's what the meeting is for."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> Commands like "Raise the wall!" are in the imperative mood. "If the wall were higher" uses the subjunctive for something that isn't so. "Would" marks the conditional. A prediction is not a proof, whichever plan it supports.

### Scene 4. Hollis's sentence (Act IV, Keeper's Desk)

**Hook (A story from Willowbank):** Page 4 says "The Sponge is drained." The librarian underlines it. By whom?

**Short:**
> Hollis wrote "The Sponge is drained" without saying who drained it. A few lines later Hollis asks, "Wet years, or our doing?"

**Longer:**
> The librarian lays page 4 on the desk and underlines four words: *The Sponge is drained.*
>
> "Passive," says Dot. "No doer."
>
> "So Hollis was hiding who did it," says Pip.
>
> "Read on," says the librarian.
>
> Pip reads. *Wet years, or our doing?*
>
> "*Our*," says {guide}. "Hollis counts themself in."
>
> Pip frowns. "Then why leave it out the first time?"
>
> "Perhaps everyone knew it was the town," says the librarian. "Hollis was writing a diary, not a headline."
>
> "Or perhaps they didn't want to think about it yet," says Bramble.
>
> The librarian nods. "Both could be true. The page doesn't say."

**Keeper's note (A true thing about language):**
> Hollis also wrote "the brook cut straight," leaving out "is." Leaving out words a reader can fill in is called ellipsis. "Pie eaten" does it too.

## Teach cards

Each card is 30 words or fewer; worked-example steps appear one tap at a time. All cards are **A true thing about language**. Cards 1 to 6 are Seedling; 7 to 9 Sapling; 10 Keeper's Desk.

**Card 1. Subject and object**
> The subject usually comes before the verb and says who or what the sentence is about. The object comes after the verb and receives the action.

Worked example: *Dot found the hat.*
1. The verb is "found."
2. Who found? Dot. Dot is the subject.
3. Found what? The hat. The hat is the object.

**Card 2. Active voice**
> In the active voice, the subject is the doer. Most sentences we say are active.

Worked example: *The baker baked the bread.*
1. Who did the baking? The baker.
2. The baker is the subject.
3. The doer is the subject, so it is active.

**Card 3. Passive voice**
> In the passive voice, the receiver becomes the subject. The verb uses a form of "be" plus a past participle, like "was baked."

Worked example: *The bread was baked by the baker.*
1. The subject is "the bread." Bread didn't bake anything.
2. The verb is "was baked": "be" plus a past participle.
3. The doer has moved into "by the baker." Passive.

**Card 4. The doer can disappear**
> A passive sentence doesn't have to say who did it. "The bread was baked at dawn" is a whole sentence. The "by" part is optional.

Worked example: *The bell was rung at noon.*
1. The subject is "the bell."
2. The verb is "was rung."
3. Who rang it? The sentence doesn't say.

**Card 5. Not every "was" is passive**
> A passive needs "be" plus a past participle, with the receiver as subject. "Puff was sleepy" and "Ember was singing" are not passive.

Worked example: *Three sentences.*
1. "Puff was sleepy": "was" plus a describing word. Not passive.
2. "Ember was singing": "was" plus an -ing verb. Not passive.
3. "The song was sung": "was" plus a past participle. Passive.

**Card 6. Why choose the passive?**
> Writers choose the passive when the doer is unknown, obvious or unimportant, or to keep attention on what happened. It can also hide a doer.

Worked example: *Three passives, three reasons.*
1. "The bridge was built long ago." Nobody remembers who built it.
2. "The letters were delivered." Dot delivers them, so it's obvious.
3. "Mistakes were made." By whom? This sentence won't say.

**Card 7. Statements and commands**
> A verb's mood shows its job. A statement tells what is: indicative. A command tells someone what to do: imperative. A command's subject, "you," is usually unsaid.

Worked example: *Two café notices.*
1. "The café opens at eight." It tells a fact. Indicative.
2. "Wipe your paws." It gives an order. Imperative.
3. Who should wipe? You, the reader. "You" is understood.

**Card 8. The subjunctive**
> The subjunctive is for things wished, untrue right now, or formally asked for. "If I were a dragon" imagines what isn't so. "We ask that he be here" asks formally.

Worked example: *If Pip were taller, he would reach the pie.*
1. Is Pip taller? No. The "if" part imagines it.
2. The verb is "were," not "was."
3. In everyday talk, many people say "was." Formal writing usually uses "were."

**Card 9. The conditional**
> A "would" sentence says what might happen if something else happened. School grammar calls this the conditional. It is a prediction, not a fact.

Worked example: *If the bell rang now, Dot would come.*
1. The "if" part: the bell rang now. It's imagined.
2. The "would" part: Dot would come.
3. Did Dot come? We can't tell. It's a what-if.

**Card 10. Grammar for effect (Keeper's Desk)**
> To talk about a writer's grammar, name the feature, quote it, then say its effect on the reader. Naming a feature alone isn't enough.

Worked example: *Grahame's Mole digs out of his house (1908).*
1. Feature: a list of verbs joined by "and."
2. Quote: "scraped and scratched and scrabbled."
3. Effect: the digging feels long, busy and hard work.

## Benches

### (a) Who Did It?

**Header:** Tap the doer. Then choose a tray: doer first, doer after "by," or doer left out.

**Practice sheet (standalone set, A story from Willowbank):**
- Pip ate the pie. *Doer first: Pip. Active.*
- The pie was eaten by Pip. *Doer after "by." Passive.*
- The pie was eaten. *Doer left out. Passive.*
- Dot found the hat. *Doer first: Dot. Active.*
- The hat was found on the bridge. *Doer left out. "The bridge" is a place, not a doer.*
- The bell was rung by the miller. *Doer after "by." Passive.*
- A drum was played at dusk. *Doer left out. Passive.*

**Gazette lines (Act IV, after page 4; the two short pieces from bench (c)):**

- Fences were knocked flat. *Doer left out.*
- The mill wheel was stopped by the water. *Doer after "by": the water.*

**Wrong tray (any):** Find the verb first. Then ask: who or what did it?

**"Left out" follow-up:** Who might have done it? Any sensible guess is fine. The sentence doesn't say.

### (b) Flip It

**Header:** Drag the tiles to flip the sentence. Keep the same meaning and the same time.

**Sentences and their flips:**
- The miller rang the bell. → The bell was rung by the miller.
- Ember lit the candles. → The candles were lit by Ember.
- The librarian is printing the paper. → The paper is being printed by the librarian.
- Dot will deliver the letters. → The letters will be delivered by Dot.
- The baker has mended the window. → The window has been mended by the baker.

**"By" switch:** Tap to hide or show the "by" part. *Still a whole sentence. Now the doer is gone.*

**Time check feedback (tense changed):** Same meaning, but the time changed. Keep "is," "will" or "has."

**Swap feedback (meaning changed):** Now the candles are doing the lighting. Keep who did what.

### (f) Old Pages, Old Books (Keeper's Desk)

**Header:** Name the feature. Quote it. Say its effect.

**Card 1 (A true thing from history):** Kenneth Grahame, *The Wind in the Willows*, 1908. The Mole digs out of his house: "So he scraped and scratched and scrabbled and scrooged."
- Feature choices: a list of verbs joined by "and" ✔ · a passive verb · a question
- Effect line: The repeated "and" makes the digging go on and on.

**Card 2 (A true thing from history):** William Shakespeare, *Twelfth Night*, written about 1601. The play opens: "If music be the food of love, play on."
- Mood line: "If music be" is subjunctive. "Play on" is imperative, a command to the musicians.
- Today we would say "If music is." The old form sounds formal and grand.

**Card 3 (A true thing from history):** William Strunk Jr., *The Elements of Style*, 1918. Rule 10: "Use the active voice."
- Mood line: The rule is itself a command: imperative mood.
- Strunk also wrote that the passive is sometimes necessary. Style advice isn't a law of grammar.

**Card 4 (Act IV, A story from Willowbank):** Hollis's page 4: "The Sponge is drained and the brook cut straight past the new fields."
- Feature choices: a command · a passive with the doer left out ✔ · a "would" sentence
- Ellipsis line: "the brook cut straight" leaves out "is."
- Two readings line: "Is drained" can also just describe the Sponge, like "is dry." Either way, no doer is named.
- Say why (to a grown-up): Why might Hollis leave out who drained it? Any reason from the page is fine.

## Problem sets

Every item has the "I'm stuck" ladder. Choices appear in the order written; ✔ marks the right one. Tags in backticks name the misconception a wrong choice tests. Sets A to D and F are the standalone set; Set E opens after page 4 comes free.

### Set A. Who did it? (Sprout, Seedling)

**A1 (Sprout, guided).** Steps shown first: 1. Find the verb: "was eaten." 2. Look for "by." 3. Who comes after "by"?
- Prompt: "The pie was eaten by Pip." Who did the eating?
- Choices: The pie (`subject-is-doer`) · Pip ✔ · Nobody, it doesn't say (`by-phrase-ignored`)
- Feedback: The pie: The pie is the subject, but pies don't eat. · Nobody: Look after "by." Someone is named.
- Hint: Look for the word "by."
- Explanation: In a passive, the doer can come after "by." Here it's Pip.

**A2 (Sprout).**
- Prompt: "Dot found the hat." Which is the subject?
- Choices: found (`verb-as-subject`) · the hat (`object-is-subject`) · Dot ✔
- Feedback: Found: "Found" is the verb, the action. · The hat: The hat was found. It's the object.
- Hint: Who did the finding?
- Explanation: Dot did the finding, so Dot is the subject.

**A3 (Seedling).**
- Prompt: "The hat was found on the bridge." Who found it?
- Choices: The hat, since it comes first (`subject-is-doer`) · The sentence doesn't say ✔ · The bridge, where it was found (`place-as-doer`)
- Feedback: The hat: Hats can't find things. It's what was found. · The bridge: "On the bridge" tells where, not who.
- Hint: Is there a "by" part?
- Explanation: No "by" part, so the finder is left out.

**A4 (Seedling, guided sort).** Steps shown first: 1. Find the verb. 2. Is it "be" plus a past participle? 3. Is the subject the receiver?
- Prompt: Sort each sentence: passive or not passive.
- Cards: The baker baked forty loaves (not passive) · Forty loaves were baked this morning (passive) · The café was full (not passive) · Ember was blowing bubbles (not passive) · The window was mended by the baker (passive) · The brook rose (not passive)
- Feedback for "The café was full" in passive (`be-means-passive`): "Full" describes the café. Nothing was done to it.
- Feedback for "Ember was blowing bubbles" in passive (`be-means-passive`): Ember is doing the blowing. Active.
- Feedback for "The brook rose" in passive (`no-doer-means-passive`): Nothing was done to the brook. No "be" either.
- Hint: Check card 5.
- Explanation: Passive needs "be," a past participle, and a receiver as subject.

**A5 (Seedling).**
- Prompt: Which sentence is passive?
- Choices: The lamp was lit by the librarian. ✔ · Puff was very sleepy after the big supper. (`be-means-passive`) · The librarian lit the lamp at the door. (`past-means-passive`)
- Feedback: Puff: "Sleepy" describes Puff. Nothing was done to Puff. · Lit the lamp: Past tense, but the doer comes first. Active.
- Hint: Which subject had something done to it?
- Explanation: The lamp received the action, with "was lit." Passive.

### Set B. Flip it (Seedling, Sapling)

**B1 (Seedling, guided build).** Steps shown first: 1. The object becomes the subject. 2. Add "was" and a past participle. 3. Add "by" and the doer.
- Prompt: Flip it: "The miller rang the bell."
- Tiles: The bell · was rung · by the miller
- Answer: The bell was rung by the miller.
- Hint: What was rung?
- Explanation: The bell moves to the front. The miller moves after "by."

**B2 (Seedling).**
- Prompt: Which flip keeps the meaning of "Ember lit the candles"?
- Choices: The candles were lit by Ember. ✔ · Ember was lit up by the candles. (`swap-without-meaning`) · The candles lit Ember. (`swap-without-meaning`)
- Feedback: Lit up: Now the candles shine on Ember. That's a new story. · Candles lit Ember: The doer changed. Keep who did what.
- Hint: Who did the lighting, before you flipped it?
- Explanation: Flipping changes the order, not who did what.

**B3 (Sapling).**
- Prompt: Make it passive: "The librarian is printing the paper."
- Choices: The paper will have been printed by the librarian. (`tense-drift`) · The paper is being printed by the librarian. ✔ · The paper is printing the librarian. (`swap-without-meaning`)
- Feedback: Will have been: That's the future. The printing is happening now. · Printing the librarian: Now the paper is the doer. Keep who did what.
- Hint: "Is printing" means it's happening now.
- Explanation: "Is printing" becomes "is being printed." Same time, same meaning.

**B4 (Sapling).**
- Prompt: "The bell was rung in the night." Why might a writer leave out who?
- Choices: Leaving it out always hides something (`passive-always-hides`) · A passive is never allowed a doer (`by-banned`) · They may not know who rang it ✔
- Feedback: Always hides: Sometimes. Often the writer just doesn't know. · Never allowed: "The bell was rung by the miller" is fine.
- Hint: Think of card 6's reasons.
- Explanation: The doer may be unknown, obvious or unimportant. Or hidden. The sentence alone can't tell you.

**B5 (Sapling, true / not true).** Read each and choose True or Not true.
- "Passive sentences are bad English." Not true (`passive-is-wrong`): they're correct and sometimes the best choice.
- "A passive sentence can name the doer with 'by.'" True.
- "Every sentence with 'was' is passive." Not true (`be-means-passive`): "Puff was sleepy" isn't.
- "'The brook rose' names nothing that acted on it, but it isn't passive." True.
- Hint: Look back at cards 4 and 5.
- Explanation: The passive is a choice, not a mistake. It needs "be" plus a past participle.

### Set C. Moods at the press (Seedling, Sapling)

**C1 (Seedling, guided sort).** Steps shown first: 1. Does it tell, order, imagine or predict? 2. Look for "if," "that" or "would." 3. Choose a tray.
- Prompt: Sort each café line by the mood of its bold verb.
- Cards: The café **opens** at eight (statement) · **Wipe** your paws, please (command) · The baker asks that every customer **wait** in line (formal ask) · With a bigger oven, the bread **would be** ready sooner (would)
- Feedback for "Wipe your paws" in statement (`mood-by-punctuation`): It tells you to do something. That's a command.
- Feedback for "every customer wait" in statement (`subjunctive-is-error`): "Wait," not "waits." That's a formal ask.
- Hint: Ask: what is this sentence's job?
- Explanation: Statements tell, commands order, the subjunctive asks or imagines, the conditional predicts.

**C2 (Seedling).**
- Prompt: "Wipe your paws." Who is told to wipe?
- Choices: Nobody, it has no subject (`imperative-no-subject`) · The paws (`object-is-subject`) · You, the reader ✔
- Feedback: Nobody: A command's subject is "you." It's understood, not written. · The paws: The paws get wiped. They're the object.
- Hint: Who is reading the notice?
- Explanation: Commands speak to "you," even when "you" isn't written.

**C3 (Sapling).** Shown above the question: A formal letter to the council. You do not run the café.
- Prompt: Which opening suits the letter?
- Choices: If I am the one in charge of the café now (`real-for-unreal`) · If I were in charge of the café now ✔ · If I was in charge of the café now (`informal-for-formal`)
- Feedback: I am: You don't run the café now. For something untrue now, formal writing uses "were." · I was: Many people say this, and it's fine in talk. Formal writing usually uses "were."
- Hint: You don't run the café now. Which verb does formal writing use?
- Explanation: For something untrue right now, formal writing uses "were." For a real maybe, "If I am ever" is fine.

**C4 (Sapling).**
- Prompt: "If Pip were a dragon, he would breathe fire." Is Pip a dragon?
- Choices: No, it's only imagined ✔ · Yes, the sentence says he is (`subjunctive-is-fact`) · Maybe, "were" can't tell us (`were-tells-nothing`)
- Feedback: Yes: "If Pip were" imagines. It doesn't claim. · Maybe: "If he were" marks something imagined. Pip is a fox.
- Hint: What does "if... were" do?
- Explanation: The subjunctive "were" imagines what isn't so.

**C5 (Sapling).**
- Prompt: "The librarian asks that every reader return books." Why "return," not "returns"?
- Choices: It's a spelling mistake in the notice (`subjunctive-is-error`) · Formal asks can use the plain verb ✔ · "Every reader" means lots of readers (`were-only-plural`)
- Feedback: Mistake: It's on purpose. A formal ask uses the plain verb. · Lots of readers: "Every reader" is singular. Normally it's "returns."
- Hint: Look at card 8's formal ask.
- Explanation: After "asks that," formal English can use the plain verb: return, be, wait.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Verb:** a word for an action or a state, like *ran*, *is* or *seems*.
- **Subject:** who or what the sentence is about, usually before the verb.
- **Object:** who or what receives the action, usually after the verb.
- **Doer:** the one who does the action.
- **Receiver:** the one the action is done to.
- **Tense:** the verb form that shows time: past, present or future.
- **Active voice:** the subject is the doer. *Pip ate the pie.*
- **Passive voice:** the receiver is the subject. *The pie was eaten.*
- **Past participle:** the verb form after "has" or "was," like *eaten*, *rung* or *mended*.
- **Mood:** what job a verb does: tell, order, imagine or predict.
- **Indicative:** the mood for statements and facts.
- **Imperative:** the mood for commands.
- **Subjunctive:** the mood for wishes, things untrue right now, and formal asks: *if I were*, *that he be*.
- **Conditional:** a "would" sentence about what might happen.
- **Formal:** careful language for official letters, reports and speeches.
- **Headline:** the big title over a newspaper story.
- **Synonym:** a word with nearly the same meaning, like *big* and *large*.
- **Antonym:** a word with the opposite meaning, like *big* and *little*.
- **Ellipsis:** leaving out words a reader can fill in. The mark ... is also called an ellipsis.
- **Hyphen:** a short line joining words, as in *well-known*.
- **Colon:** the mark : which can introduce a list.
- **Semicolon:** the mark ; which can join two closely linked sentences.
- **Bullet points:** a list with a dot before each line.
- **Effect:** what a writer's choice does to the reader.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** subject and object; active and passive voice, how to build a passive, and why writers choose it; the moods school grammar names (indicative, imperative, subjunctive, conditional), including formal subjunctives such as "if I were" and "that he be"; the Year 6 grammar words; and, at Keeper's Desk, how to talk about a writer's grammar: name the feature, quote it, say its effect.

**UK terms:** schools in England say *semi-colon* and *full stop*, and the Year 6 word list is part of the national curriculum's grammar appendix. This pack uses US spellings.

**About "if I was":** many people say "if I was" in everyday talk, and it is not marked wrong here. The pack teaches "were" as the usual choice in formal writing for something untrue now ("If I were in charge", when you are not), as the English national curriculum does. For a real possibility, "If I am ever in charge" is correct, and no item marks it wrong.

**About moods:** US school standards name five moods, adding the interrogative (questions); question sentences are taught with sentence types in the clauses pack. Many grammarians treat "would" as a modal verb rather than a mood. The pack uses the school names because children will meet them.

**About the passive and blame:** style guides have long advised the active voice, and some readers think every passive is a way of dodging. The passive is correct English and often the best choice. Wording can change how much readers blame someone, which is why the pack asks "by whom?" without assuming an answer.

**About "was" plus a describing word:** some sentences can be read two ways. "The gate was closed" can describe the gate (like "was shut") or report that someone closed it. The practice items avoid such sentences, or add "by" or a time, so each has one answer. Hollis's "The Sponge is drained" can be read either way; both readings name no doer.

**What it does not claim:** the grammar of the Gazette's pieces and the meeting posters never shows which plan is right or which explanation is true. That is for evidence, at the Keeper's Desk and the meeting. Willowbank, the press, the Gazette and Hollis's page are invented. Grahame, Shakespeare and Strunk are real, public-domain books.
