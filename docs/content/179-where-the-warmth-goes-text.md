## Story scenes

### Scene 1. The cold is getting in (Act I, chapter 4: mend the village)

**Short:** Puff's tea is cold before it reaches the cup. "The cold is getting in!" says Pip. {guide} smiles. "Or the warmth is getting out."

**Longer:** The storm had cracked the café shutters, and a thin wind found every gap. Puff poured the tea. It was barely warm.

"The cold is getting in," said Pip, wrapping his tail around his paws.

{guide} pointed at the crack. "Feel that? The air by the window is cold. But the teapot isn't filling up with cold. It's losing warmth. Warmth always moves from the warm thing to the cooler thing."

Across the lane, the baker raked the last embers out of his oven and slid in the loaves. "No fire?" asked Dot, adjusting her hat.

"The bricks remember," said the baker. "I warm them all morning. Then they give it back to the bread."

"So where does the teapot's warmth go?" asked Pip.

"Let's follow it," said {guide}. "Then we'll mend the shutters."

**Keeper's note (A true thing from history):** In 1796 Count Rumford wrote about fireplaces. Many sent warmth straight up the chimney. His fireplace was shallow, with angled sides, so more warmth reached the room. Builders still make fireplaces in his style.

### Scene 2. Apples in the sun (Act II, chapter 1: the weekly market, at the summer Market Fair)

**Short:** At midday the orchard keeper's apples feel warm and soft. Dot wants an awning. Bramble says her net will do. Who is right?

**Longer:** By midday the market square was bright and still. The orchard keeper turned an apple in her hand. "Warm as toast," she said. "They'll be soft by teatime."

"An awning," said Dot. "A big one."

Bramble was already unrolling a fishing net. "This will do. It covers the whole stall."

"It covers it," said {guide}. "Does it shade it?"

The magpie hopped onto the net and peered through the holes at the sunny apples below.

"There's only one way to know," said {guide}. "We put a crate under the cover and a crate in the open sun, and we feel both at the same time."

Pip volunteered to test the apples. Several times.

**Keeper's note (A true thing from science):** Shade cools a spot because it stops the sunlight reaching it. Nothing in the shade makes cold.

### Scene 3. Clack on the green (Act II, at the summer Market Fair)

**Short:** Pip rolls a ball. Clack! His ball stops dead and Ember's ball rolls away. "Where did my ball's go go?" asks Pip.

**Longer:** On the green, Pip and Ember played bowls with two wooden balls the same size. Pip rolled first. His ball hit Ember's with a sharp clack, stopped almost dead, and Ember's ball rolled away across the grass.

"Hey," said Pip. "My ball was moving. Now it isn't. Where did its go go?"

"Some went into my ball," said Ember. "Look how far it went."

"And some went into the clack," said {guide}. "You heard it. Sound carries energy away. A little warmed the balls too, though you'd never feel it."

"Again," said Pip. "Faster this time."

The clack was louder. Ember's ball went further. Ember sighed and went to fetch it.

## Teach cards

Each worked example reveals one step per tap.

**T1. Sunlight warms things** (A true thing from science). Sunlight warms ground, stone, water and apples. Things in the sun get warmer than the same things in the shade.
Worked example: (1) Two stone steps, one sunny, one shady. (2) Feel both at midday. (3) The sunny step is warmer.

**T2. Shade blocks the warming.** Shade is a place the sunlight can't reach. It stays cooler because it isn't being warmed. Shade doesn't make cold.
Worked example: (1) Morning: both steps cool. (2) Midday: the sunny step is warm. (3) Evening: it cools again.

**T3. The year goes round.** Spring, summer, autumn, winter, then spring again. Summer days are long and warm. Winter days are short and cold.
Worked example: (1) Summer: lanterns lit late. (2) Winter: lanterns lit early. (3) Early lighting means a short day.

**T4. A shade that works.** A shade works when it sits between the Sun and the spot. Test it: compare the shaded spot with a sunny spot.
Worked example: (1) Midday Sun is high. (2) Hang the canvas overhead. (3) Feel both crates. (4) The shaded crate is cooler.

**T5. A bump moves energy.** When a moving ball hits a still one, energy moves across. The moving ball slows. The still ball speeds off. Both change.
Worked example: (1) Pip's ball rolls. (2) Clack! (3) Pip's ball nearly stops. (4) Ember's ball rolls away.

**T6. The clack carries energy away.** Some energy leaves as sound and a little warmth. A faster roll hands on more: the target goes further.
Worked example: (1) Slow roll: soft clack, short roll. (2) Fast roll: loud clack, long roll.

**T7. Warmth moves from hotter to cooler.** Warmth always moves from the hotter thing to the cooler thing, until they match. Cold doesn't flow anywhere.
Worked example: (1) Hot tea, cool room. (2) Warmth moves from tea to room. (3) The tea cools until it matches the room.

**T8. Conduction.** Warmth passes along through a material that stays put. Metals pass it along fast. Wood and cloth pass it slowly.
Worked example: (1) A metal spoon stands in hot tea. (2) Warmth passes up the spoon. (3) Soon the handle is warm.

**T9. Convection.** Warm air or water rises, and cooler air or water slides in below. The moving air or water carries the warmth around.
Worked example: (1) The stove warms the air beside it. (2) Warm air rises. (3) Cooler air flows in. (4) The room warms.

**T10. Radiation.** Warm things give out invisible light called infrared. It needs no air. That is how the Sun's warmth crosses space.
Worked example: (1) The oven walls are hot. (2) They give out infrared. (3) The bread warms, even where it isn't touching.

**T11. Big difference, fast cooling.** The bigger the difference, the faster warmth moves. A cozy slows it down. A cozy makes no warmth of its own.
Worked example: (1) Very hot tea cools fast. (2) Nearly cool tea cools slowly. (3) A cozy slows both.

**T12. Power is how fast** (Keeper's Desk). Power is how fast energy moves. One watt means one joule of energy each second.
Worked example: (1) A 6 W lantern. (2) Each second: 6 joules. (3) Ten seconds: 6 × 10 = 60 joules.

**T13. Energy = power × time.** Energy depends on power and on time. A small power for a long time can add up to a lot.
Worked example: (1) 2,000 W for 3 s. (2) 2,000 × 3. (3) 6,000 joules.

**T14. Same job, different power.** A higher power does the same job sooner. The energy for the job stays the same.
Worked example: (1) The job needs 336,000 J. (2) 2,000 W: 168 s. (3) 1,000 W: 336 s. (4) Same energy, twice the time.

## History cards

**Rumford's cannon (A true thing from history).** About 1797-98, Count Rumford watched cannon being bored in Munich. The metal grew hot. Rubbing seemed to make heat without end. He argued heat is motion.

**Herschel's thermometers (A true thing from history).** In 1800, William Herschel split sunlight into colors. A thermometer past the red, where no light showed, got warmer still. He had found infrared.

**Joule's paddle (A true thing from history).** In 1845, James Joule let falling weights turn a paddle in water. The water warmed a tiny bit. The same work always gave the same warming.

**A story about Joule (labeled: a story people tell).** People tell a tale: in 1847, on his honeymoon, Joule took a thermometer to a waterfall. It was written down years later. No result was recorded, so we call it a story.

**The watt (A true thing from history).** In 1882, William Siemens suggested naming the unit of power after James Watt. British scientists agreed that year. An international meeting agreed in 1889.

**Keeper's note: Dewar's flask (A true thing from history).** In 1892, James Dewar showed a flask with two walls and empty space between. Little warmth could cross by conduction or convection. Silvery walls, added later, cut radiation too.

### Set 4. Watts at the Keeper's Desk

**4.1 (number with steps)**
Prompt: A 2,000 W kettle runs for 1 minute. How many joules?
Answer: 120,000 J.
Steps: (1) 1 minute = 60 seconds. (2) 2,000 joules each second. (3) 2,000 × 60 = 120,000.
Misconception tested: watt-is-energy (answering 2,000 J).
Hint: Watts tell you joules each second.
Explanation: Energy = power × time: 2,000 W for 60 s moves 120,000 J.

**4.2 (number with steps)**
Given: warming 1 kg of water by 1 °C takes about 4,200 J. Ignore losses.
Prompt: How long does a 2,000 W kettle take, 20 to 100 °C?
Answer: 168 seconds (2 minutes 48 seconds), ignoring losses.
Steps: (1) Warming: 100 − 20 = 80 °C. (2) Energy: 4,200 × 80 = 336,000 J. (3) Time: 336,000 ÷ 2,000 = 168 s. (4) 168 s = 2 min 48 s.
Misconception tested: dividing by 80 twice, or forgetting the temperature change.
Hint: Find the energy first, then divide by the power.
Explanation: A real kettle takes longer: some energy warms the kettle and the room.

**4.3 (3-choice)**
Prompt: The same water in a 1,000 W kettle. What changes?
- A. Same time, half the energy. *(misconception: power-is-energy)*
- B. Twice the time, the same energy. *(right)*
- C. Twice the time, twice the energy. *(misconception: slower uses more)*
Hint: The job (warming this water) hasn't changed.
Feedback: A: The water needs the same energy. Lower power takes longer. B: Yes. Half the rate, twice the time. C: The job needs 336,000 J either way.
Explanation: 336,000 ÷ 1,000 = 336 s. Same energy, moved at half the rate.

**4.4 (3-choice)**
Prompt: Which moves more energy: 6 W for 12 hours, or 2,000 W for 2 minutes?
- A. The kettle, because it has more power. *(misconception: powerful-uses-more)*
- B. Both the same, because both are watts. *(misconception: watt-is-energy)*
- C. The lantern, left on 12 hours. *(right)*
Hint: Work out energy = power × time for each.
Feedback: A: Power is how fast. Time matters too. B: Watts measure rate, not total. C: Yes. 259,200 J beats 240,000 J.
Explanation: Lantern: 6 × 43,200 s = 259,200 J. Kettle: 2,000 × 120 s = 240,000 J.

**4.5 (3-choice)**
Given: 1 kg of water falls 100 m and lands, giving up about 1,000 J.
Prompt: About how much warmer could the water get?
- A. About a quarter of a degree. *(right)*
- B. About 24 degrees. *(misconception: decimal slip)*
- C. None, because falling makes no warmth. *(misconception: energy-vanishes)*
Hint: 1 °C needs about 4,200 J. Is 1,000 J more or less?
Feedback: A: Yes. 1,000 ÷ 4,200 is about 0.24. B: Check the decimal. 1,000 is less than 4,200. C: The splash ends as warmth.
Explanation: 1,000 ÷ 4,200 ≈ 0.24 °C: tiny, and hard to measure (simplified: ignores evaporation and splashing air).

## "I'm stuck" worked examples (step 2 of the ladder)

- **Sun and shade:** A cat on a sunny wall and a cat in the shade. Which wall feels warmer? Start: which wall can the sunlight reach?
- **Seasons:** Snow on the bridge, lanterns lit early. Which season? Start: is the day short or long?
- **Awning:** Afternoon Sun is low in the west. Where does the curtain go? Start: point to the Sun.
- **Clack:** A marble hits a still marble. Which one slows? Start: watch the one that was moving.
- **Hotter to cooler:** Ice in warm water. Which way does warmth move? Start: which is warmer?
- **What moves?** A radiator warms the air by it. Does the air move? Start: watch the curtains above it.
- **Teapot:** Tea 60 °C, room 20 °C. Half the difference stays. Start: 60 − 20 = 40, half is 20, so 40 °C.
- **Watts:** A 10 W lamp for 5 seconds. Start: 10 joules each second, so 10 × 5 = 50 J.

## Key terms (glossary)

- **Shade:** a place the sunlight can't reach.
- **Season:** one of the four parts of the year that repeat.
- **Day length:** how many hours of daylight a day has.
- **Collision:** when moving things bump into each other.
- **Energy:** what makes things move, warm up, light up or make sound.
- **Temperature:** how hot or cold something is, measured in degrees.
- **Conduction:** warmth passed along through something that stays put.
- **Convection:** warm air or water moving and carrying warmth.
- **Radiation (infrared):** invisible light that carries warmth, even across empty space. Not the nuclear kind.
- **Insulator:** something that slows warmth moving. It makes none.
- **Power:** how fast energy moves.
- **Watt (W):** one joule each second.
- **Joule (J):** a unit for an amount of energy.
- **Kilowatt-hour:** an amount of energy, used on electricity bills. Not a power.

## Grown-up panel

**What this teaches:** sunlight warms things and shade blocks it; testing a shade against an unshaded spot; the seasons as a repeating pattern; energy moving in a collision, including into sound; warmth moving from hotter to cooler by conduction, convection and radiation; and, at the Keeper's Desk, power as the rate energy moves.

**What it does not claim:** the benches are made-up models with stated rules. The teapot numbers, the clack tally and the awning results are simplified pictures, not measurements. The kettle items ignore energy lost to the room. Why seasons happen (Earth's tilt) is left to the Sky-Watching Night pack. Nothing here is safety advice about heaters, ovens or kettles.

**Safety:** Hot things in this bench stay on the screen. Off screen, a grown-up leads, chooses what to touch and checks it first.
